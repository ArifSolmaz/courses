🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  1. Compute the torque of a force about an axis, $\tau=rF\sin\phi$, with its sign

How this week is workedBu hafta nasıl çalışılır

Every problem, worked example and experiment in this course follows the same six steps. Step 4 is the one added in September 2026, and it is the one that separates a physicist from a calculator operator.

draw → choose a law → rearrange to the symbolic answer → check a limiting case → substitute with units → interpret

  1. Symbolic answer first. Isolate the unknown in symbols, before any number appears.
  2. One limiting case. Push a parameter to a value whose answer you already know — a mass to zero, an angle to $90^\circ$, a friction coefficient to zero — and confirm the formula behaves sensibly. If it does not, the algebra is wrong and you have just caught it for free, before wasting arithmetic on it.
  3. Then numbers, with units carried through every line.

Why bother: the symbolic route is less arithmetic, not more, and it is where the marks are. A correct symbolic answer with a slip in the final multiplication is nearly full credit; a correct number with no derivation is not.

From a force to an angular acceleration

For rotation about a fixed axis, take counterclockwise positive. A force's torque component is $\tau=rF\sin\phi=F\ell_\perp$, where $\ell_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the force's line of action. Add signed torques before using $$\sum\tau=I\alpha.$$ A 2.0 kg solid disk of radius 0.20 m has $I=\tfrac12MR^2=0.0400$ kg m². A tangential 3.0 N push gives $\tau=Fr=0.600$ N m. Divide both sides of $0.600=0.0400\alpha$ by $I$: $$\alpha=15.0\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}.$$ From rest, after 2.0 s, $\omega=\alpha t=30.0$ rad/s and $\theta=\alpha t^2/2=30.0$ rad. Work and rotational energy agree: $$W=\tau\theta=0.600(30.0)=18.0\,\mathrm J =\tfrac12I\omega^2.$$ TR: Önce toplam torku bul, sonra $I$'ya böl. Kuvveti doğrudan $I\alpha$'ya eşitlemek birim bakımından yanlıştır.

Rotational Kinetic Energy & Rolling Motion

Rotational Kinetic Energy

$$K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2$$

Rolling Without Slipping

When an object rolls without slipping: $$v_{\text{cm}} = R\omega, \quad a_{\text{cm}} = R\alpha$$

Total kinetic energy of a rolling object: $$K_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{cm}}^2 + \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{cm}}^2\left(1 + \frac{I}{mR^2}\right)$$

Rolling Down an Incline

Using energy conservation for an object starting from rest at height $h$:

$$mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\left(1 + \frac{I}{mR^2}\right)$$

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1 + I/(mR^2)}}$$

Key result: For the same ramp, release from rest, sufficient static friction to prevent slipping, and negligible dissipative losses, the object with the smallest $I/(mR^2)$ ratio reaches the bottom first. A solid sphere ($I/(mR^2) = 2/5$) beats a solid cylinder ($1/2$), which beats a ring ($1$). The shape matters, not the mass or radius!

Pulley-Rope Systems

When a pulley has mass (and therefore moment of inertia), we must include its rotational dynamics.

Atwood Machine with a Massive Pulley

Two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ connected by a rope over a pulley of mass $M$ and radius $R$:

For a nonzero pulley inertia and nonzero angular acceleration, the tensions on the two sides are different. Choose downward on the $m_1$ side and upward on the $m_2$ side as positive:

$$m_1 g - T_1 = m_1 a$$ $$T_2 - m_2 g = m_2 a$$ $$T_1 R - T_2 R = I\alpha = I \frac{a}{R}$$

Solving for the acceleration:

$$a = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2 + I/R^2}$$

For a solid disk pulley ($I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$):

$$a = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2 + M/2}$$

Key insight: The pulley's inertia acts like extra mass in the denominator, slowing the system down compared to a massless-pulley Atwood machine.

Eliminate tensions slowly. Add the two mass equations and use the pulley equation divided by $R$:

$$\begin{aligned}(m_1-m_2)g-(T_1-T_2)&=(m_1+m_2)a,\\T_1-T_2&=\frac{I}{R^2}a,\\(m_1-m_2)g&=\left(m_1+m_2+\frac{I}{R^2}\right)a.\end{aligned}$$

For $m_1=3.0\,\mathrm{kg}$, $m_2=1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and a $2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ disk pulley, $$a=\frac{(3-1)(9.81)}{3+1+2/2}=3.92\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

Then $T_1=3(9.81-3.92)=17.7\,\mathrm N$ and $T_2=1(9.81+3.92)=13.7\,\mathrm N$. TR: Gerilme farkı makaraya açısal ivme kazandırır. Eşit kütle sınırında $a=0$ ve gerilmeler eşit olabilir; makaraya kütle vermek tek başına her durumda fark yaratmaz.

Worked example 11.1

🔧 Worked Example 1: Moment of Inertia & Rolling

Problem: A solid cylinder (mass $4\,\mathrm{kg}$, radius $0.1\,\mathrm m$) and a hollow cylinder (same mass and radius) roll without slipping down a 2-meter high ramp ($30^\circ$). Find the speed of each at the bottom and the time each takes.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Paper solution. Assume both cylinders start from rest and the hollow cylinder is a thin shell. Rolling without slipping requires $\omega=v/R$. Write $I=\beta MR^2$ with $\beta=1/2$ for the solid cylinder and $\beta=1$ for the thin shell. $$Mgh=\tfrac12Mv^2+\tfrac12\beta MR^2(v/R)^2 \Rightarrow v=\sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+\beta}}.$$ For $h=2\,\mathrm m$, the solid cylinder has $v=\sqrt{\frac{39.24}{1.5}}=5.11\,\mathrm{m/s}$; the hollow cylinder has $v=\sqrt{\frac{39.24}{2}}=4.43\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

The path length is $s=h/\sin30^\circ=4\,\mathrm m$. Their constant accelerations are $a=g\sin30^\circ/(1+\beta)$: $3.27$ and $2.45\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. From $s=\tfrac12at^2$, multiply by 2 and divide by $a$: $t=\sqrt{2s/a}$. Thus $t_{\rm solid}=1.56\,\mathrm s$ and $t_{\rm hollow}=1.81\,\mathrm s$. A thicker hollow cylinder would need its inner radius to determine $I$.

Angular momentum and its conservationAçısal momentum ve korunumu

This section closes the course's stated learning outcome on angular momentum. It sits here because it is part of Dönme Hareketi Dinamiği — the dynamics of rotational motion — and it is the rotational twin of the momentum you used in Week 09.

The definition

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis with moment of inertia $I$ and angular velocity $\omega$:

Key equation 11.1
$$\,L = I\omega\, \qquad [\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}]$$

Compare the two columns and you have learned most of this section already:

Linear (Week 09)Rotational (now)
momentum $p = mv$angular momentum $L = I\omega$
force $\sum F$torque $\sum \tau$
$\sum F = \dfrac{dp}{dt}$$\sum \tau = \dfrac{dL}{dt}$
$p$ is conserved when $\sum F_{\text{ext}} = 0$$L$ is conserved when $\sum \tau_{\text{ext}} = 0$

Why $\sum\tau = dL/dt$ is the more fundamental statement

You have been using $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ all week. Start from the general form and see where it comes from:

$$\sum\tau = \frac{dL}{dt} = \frac{d(I\omega)}{dt}$$

If $I$ is constant, it comes out of the derivative and $d\omega/dt = \alpha$, giving

$$\sum\tau = I\frac{d\omega}{dt} = I\alpha.$$

So $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ is the special case in which the body's shape does not change. Read the derivative as a slope, as always: torque is the rate at which angular momentum changes. This matters because the interesting case is precisely the one where $I$ does change.

Conservation, and the one demonstration everybody remembers

With no external torque, $L$ is constant. If $I$ changes, $\omega$ must change to compensate:

$$I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2$$

A skater pulls her arms in. Her hands move closer to the axis, so $I$ falls — and because $I$ is a mass-times-distance-squared quantity, halving the effective radius of her arms quarters their contribution. With $L$ fixed, $\omega$ must rise. She spins faster, and nothing pushed her.

Worked example — symbolic first. A turntable of moment of inertia $I_0$ spins freely at $\omega_0$. A lump of clay of mass $m$ is dropped gently onto it at radius $r$ (no external torque about the axis, and the clay arrives with no angular momentum). Find the new angular velocity.

The clay adds $mr^2$ to the moment of inertia, so conservation gives

$$I_0\omega_0 = (I_0 + mr^2)\,\omega_1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\,\omega_1 = \frac{I_0}{I_0 + mr^2}\,\omega_0\,}$$

Limiting checks. $m \to 0$ gives $\omega_1 \to \omega_0$ ✅. $r \to 0$, the clay landing on the axis, also gives $\omega_1 \to \omega_0$ ✅ — dropping mass exactly on the axis changes nothing, which is a genuinely non-obvious prediction. And $mr^2 \gg I_0$ sends $\omega_1 \to 0$ ✅.

Now numbers. $I_0 = 0.0250\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $\omega_0 = 12.0\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, $m = 0.200\ \mathrm{kg}$ at $r = 0.150\ \mathrm{m}$:

$$mr^2 = (0.200)(0.150)^2 = 4.50\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},$$ $$\omega_1 = \frac{0.0250}{0.0250 + 0.00450}(12.0) = \frac{0.0250}{0.0295}(12.0) = 10.2\ \mathrm{rad/s}.$$

Is kinetic energy conserved here? No — check it: $K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2$ falls from $1.80\ \mathrm J$ to $1.53\ \mathrm J$. The clay had to be accelerated up to speed by friction as it settled, and that dissipates energy. This is the rotational version of the perfectly inelastic collision from Week 09: angular momentum is conserved, kinetic energy is not. The same asymmetry, for the same reason.

Point masses connect inertia to rotational energy

Problem. A very light $1.00\,\mathrm{m}$ rod carries a $2.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ mass at one end and a $3.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ mass at the other. A fixed perpendicular axis lies $30.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the $3.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ end. The rotating system has kinetic energy $100\,\mathrm{J}$. Find its moment of inertia and its rotation rate in revolutions per second.

Reasoning and algebra. Draw the pivot between the masses. Distances in $I=\sum mr^2$ are measured from that pivot, not from an arbitrary end of the rod:

$$ r_3=0.300\,\mathrm{m},\qquad r_2=1.00-0.300=0.700\,\mathrm{m}. $$

Treat the attached objects as point masses and neglect the rod's own inertia:

Key equation 11.2
$$ \begin{aligned} I&=m_2r_2^2+m_3r_3^2\\ &=(2.00)(0.700)^2+(3.00)(0.300)^2\\ &=0.980+0.270=1.25\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}. \end{aligned} $$

This provides a bridge to rotational energy. Starting with $K=\tfrac12 I\omega^2$, isolate the unknown before substituting:

Key equation 11.3
$$ \begin{aligned} \omega^2&=\frac{2K}{I},\\ \omega&=\sqrt{\frac{2(100\,\mathrm{J})}{1.25\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}}} =12.6\,\mathrm{rad/s},\\ f&=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}=2.01\,\mathrm{rev/s}. \end{aligned} $$

Check. Substituting back gives $\tfrac12(1.25)(12.6)^2\approx100.0\,\mathrm{J}$. The lighter mass contributes more inertia because it lies much farther from the pivot and the distance is squared. The energy determines an angular-speed magnitude; it does not tell us clockwise or counterclockwise.

Only the perpendicular force component turns the nut

Problem. A $1.10\,\mathrm{kg}$ wrench extends directly east from a nut. A $150\,\mathrm{N}$ force is applied $15.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ east of the nut, directed $30.0^\circ$ north of east. Find the torque produced by this applied force about the centre of the nut, including its direction when viewed from above.

Reasoning and algebra. Draw the position vector $\mathbf r$ eastward from the nut to the force application point. The angle between $\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf F$ is $30.0^\circ$. Only the northward force component is perpendicular to the wrench:

$$ \begin{aligned} r&=15.0\,\mathrm{cm}=0.150\,\mathrm{m},\\ F_\perp&=F\sin\theta=(150\,\mathrm{N})\sin30.0^\circ =75.0\,\mathrm{N},\\ \tau&=rF_\perp =(0.150\,\mathrm{m})(75.0\,\mathrm{N}) =\boxed{11.3\,\mathrm{N\,m}}. \end{aligned} $$

Rounded to three significant figures, the torque is $11.3\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. The northward force acting at the eastward end turns the wrench counterclockwise in the horizontal plane; the torque vector points upward by the right-hand rule.

Check. The full product $rF=22.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}$ would require a force at $90^\circ$ to the wrench. Here $\sin30^\circ=1/2$, so the actual torque is half that maximum. The eastward component pushes along the wrench and has zero moment arm about the nut. The wrench's mass is unnecessary when calculating the torque of the specified applied force.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Kuvvetin tamamını doğrudan kol uzunluğuyla çarpmıyoruz. Dönmeye katkı yapan, anahtara dik olan $75.0\,\mathrm{N}$'luk bileşendir. $15\,\mathrm{cm}$'yi $0.150\,\mathrm{m}$'ye çevirerek tork birimini $\mathrm{N\,m}$ elde ederiz. Açıyı yanlışlıkla kuzey ekseninden ölçmek sinüs–kosinüs karışıklığına yol açar.

Source: Young/Freedman, University Physics with Modern Physics, 14th Global Edition, Chapter 10, §10.2 Problems, question 1 (10_testbank.doc). Original givens retained; wording and worked explanation expanded.

Infer a braking torque from the stopping angle

Problem. A uniform solid sphere has mass $1.85\,\mathrm{kg}$ and diameter $45.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. It spins about its centre at $2.40\,\mathrm{rev/s}$, then slows uniformly to rest after $18.2$ revolutions. Find the net braking torque. Take its initial direction of rotation as positive.

Reasoning and algebra. First obtain the radius and the correct solid-sphere inertia. Diameter is twice the radius:

$$ \begin{aligned} R&=0.450/2=0.225\,\mathrm{m},\\ I&=\tfrac25MR^2 =\tfrac25(1.85)(0.225)^2 =0.0375\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}. \end{aligned} $$

The stopping time is not given, so choose an angular kinematics equation that eliminates time:

$$ \omega_f^2=\omega_i^2+2\alpha\Delta\theta. $$

Convert both rotational quantities consistently, then rearrange:

$$ \begin{aligned} \omega_i&=2.40(2\pi)\,\mathrm{rad/s},\qquad \Delta\theta=18.2(2\pi)\,\mathrm{rad},\\ \alpha&=\frac{0-\omega_i^2}{2\Delta\theta} =-0.994\,\mathrm{rad/s^2},\\ \tau_{\mathrm{net}}&=I\alpha =(0.0375)(-0.994) =\boxed{-0.0372\,\mathrm{N\,m}}. \end{aligned} $$

Interpretation and check. The magnitude is approximately $0.0372\,\mathrm{N\,m}$; the negative sign indicates a torque opposing the initial rotation. As an independent check, the constant torque does work $\tau\Delta\theta=-\tfrac12 I\omega_i^2$, removing all initial rotational kinetic energy. Keeping radians in both equations makes this energy comparison consistent.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Bu problemde zaman aramaya gerek yok; $\omega^2$ bağıntısı doğrudan verilen açıyla çalışır. Atalet momentinde çapı yarıçap yerine kullanmak sonucu dört kat büyütür. İvme ve torkun eksi çıkması, seçtiğimiz pozitif dönme yönüne karşı etki ettiklerini gösterir; torkun büyüklüğünü söylerken pozitif değer kullanırız.

Source: Young/Freedman, University Physics with Modern Physics, 14th Global Edition, Chapter 10, §10.2 Problems, question 11 (10_testbank.doc). Original givens retained; wording and worked explanation expanded.

Equal opposite forces can still produce rotation

Problem. A uniform thin beam of length $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ and mass $5.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ lies horizontally east–west. A $200\,\mathrm{N}$ force pushes downward at its east end; another $200\,\mathrm{N}$ force pushes upward at its west end. Find its angular acceleration about its centre of mass.

Reasoning and algebra. Place the origin at the beam's centre. Each force acts at a distance $L/2=1.00\,\mathrm{m}$. Draw both arrows: they tend to lower the east end and raise the west end. Their force vectors cancel, but their torques have the same rotational sense:

$$ \begin{aligned} \tau_{\mathrm{net}}&=F\frac{L}{2}+F\frac{L}{2}=FL,\\ &=(200\,\mathrm{N})(2.00\,\mathrm{m})=400\,\mathrm{N\,m}. \end{aligned} $$

This pair is called a force couple. For rotation about the perpendicular central axis of a uniform slender beam,

Key equation 11.4
$$ \begin{aligned} I_{\mathrm{cm}}&=\frac{ML^2}{12} =\frac{(5.00)(2.00)^2}{12} =1.67\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2},\\ \alpha&=\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{net}}}{I_{\mathrm{cm}}} =\frac{400}{1.67}\,\mathrm{rad/s^2} =240\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}. \end{aligned} $$

Direction and check. With east as $+x$, north as $+y$, and upward as $+z$, both $\mathbf r\times\mathbf F$ terms point north. Thus the angular-acceleration vector points north. The beam's weight acts through its centre of mass, so it contributes no torque about that point. Cancellation of the two applied forces alone does not cancel their rotational effect.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Kuvvetlerin eşit ve zıt olması, torklarının da eşit ve zıt olduğu anlamına gelmez. Her iki kuvvet aynı dönme eğilimini oluşturduğu için torkları topladık. Merkezden her uca uzaklık $1.00\,\mathrm{m}$'dir. Son adımda $\tau=I\alpha$ denkleminde iki tarafı $I$'ya bölerek açısal ivmeyi yalnız bıraktık.

Source: Young/Freedman, University Physics with Modern Physics, 14th Global Edition, Chapter 10, §10.2 Problems, question 13 (10_testbank.doc). Original givens retained; wording and worked explanation expanded.

A rolling shell accelerates more slowly than a sliding point

Problem. A thin cylindrical shell is released from rest on a ramp inclined $30^\circ$ above horizontal. It rolls without slipping. Find how long it takes to travel the first $3.1\,\mathrm{m}$ along the ramp. Use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Reasoning and algebra. Choose downhill as positive. Draw weight, normal force and static friction; the friction force points uphill and supplies the torque needed to spin up the shell. Let $f$ be its positive magnitude. Translation and rotation give

$$ mg\sin\theta-f=ma,\qquad fR=I\alpha. $$

For a thin shell, $I=mR^2$. Rolling without slipping adds $a=\alpha R$. Substitute this constraint into the torque equation:

$$ fR=(mR^2)\frac{a}{R} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f=ma. $$

Return to the translational equation and isolate the acceleration:

$$ mg\sin\theta-ma=ma \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a=\frac{g\sin\theta}{2} =\frac{9.81\sin30^\circ}{2} =2.45\,\mathrm{m/s^2}. $$

Starting from rest with constant acceleration, $s=\tfrac12at^2$. Therefore

$$ t=\sqrt{\frac{2s}{a}} =\sqrt{\frac{2(3.1\,\mathrm{m})}{2.45\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}} =\boxed{1.59\,\mathrm{s}\approx1.6\,\mathrm{s}}. $$

Check. The downhill acceleration is half of $g\sin30^\circ$, because some gravitational energy becomes rotation. Mass and radius cancel for this specified shape. Static friction is compatible with mechanical-energy conservation during ideal rolling; there is no sliding at the contact point.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Önce öteleme ve dönme denklemlerini birlikte çözüyoruz. Kaymadan yuvarlanma koşulu, $a$ ile $\alpha$'yı bağlar. Sürtünmeyi yok saymak dönmeyi başlatacak torku da yok saymak olur. Zamanı bulurken karekök almayı unutmuyoruz; $2s/a$'nın birimi saniye karedir.

Source: Young/Freedman, University Physics with Modern Physics, 14th Global Edition, Chapter 10, §10.2 Problems, question 18 (10_testbank.doc). Original givens retained; wording and worked explanation expanded.

One rolling sphere links energy distance and rotation

Problem. A uniform solid sphere of mass $1.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ and diameter $30.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ rolls from rest without slipping down a $35^\circ$ incline of length $7.0\,\mathrm{m}$. Find its final linear speed, angular speed and total rotation angle. Explain which results depend on mass or radius. Use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Reasoning and algebra. The vertical drop is $h=s\sin35^\circ$, while the rolling distance is $s=7.0\,\mathrm{m}$. The radius is $R=0.150\,\mathrm{m}$. Gravitational energy becomes both translational and rotational kinetic energy:

$$ \begin{aligned} mgh&=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12I\omega^2,\\ I&=\tfrac25mR^2,\qquad \omega=v/R,\\ mgh&=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac15mv^2=\tfrac7{10}mv^2,\\ v&=\sqrt{\frac{10gh}{7}} =\sqrt{\frac{10(9.81)(7.0\sin35^\circ)}{7}} =\boxed{7.50\,\mathrm{m/s}}. \end{aligned} $$

The two no-slip relations now supply the remaining quantities:

Key equation 11.5
$$ \omega=\frac{v}{R}=\frac{7.50}{0.150} =50.0\,\mathrm{rad/s},\qquad \Delta\theta=\frac{s}{R}=\frac{7.0}{0.150} =46.7\,\mathrm{rad}. $$

Interpretation and check. For this fixed shape and height, $v$ depends on neither mass nor radius: both cancel in the energy equation. Angular speed depends inversely on radius, and not on mass. A larger sphere can have the same centre-of-mass speed while turning more slowly. The check $R\Delta\theta=7.0\,\mathrm{m}$ recovers the distance along the ramp, not its vertical height.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Çapı önce ikiye bölerek yarıçapı bulduk. Enerji hesabında düşey yükseklik $s\sin35^\circ$, yuvarlanma açısında ise eğim boyunca alınan $s$ kullanılır. $\omega=v/R$ yerine konduğunda dönme enerjisindeki $R^2$ sadeleşir; doğrusal hızın yarıçaptan bağımsız olması buradan gelir.

Source: Young/Freedman, University Physics with Modern Physics, 14th Global Edition, Chapter 10, §10.2 Problems, question 22 (10_testbank.doc). Original givens retained; wording and worked explanation expanded.
