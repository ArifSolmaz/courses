11.2Concepts, demonstrations and worked examplesKonular, gösterimler ve çözümlü örnekler

Before you move a slider: Predict the rolling order from $\dfrac{I}{MR^2}$ before running the comparison. State the no-slip condition and explain the two kinetic-energy terms.

From a force to an angular acceleration

For rotation about a fixed axis, take counterclockwise positive. A force's torque component is $\tau=rF\sin\phi=F\ell_\perp$, where $\ell_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the force's line of action. Add signed torques before using $$\sum\tau=I\alpha.$$ A 2.0 kg solid disk of radius 0.20 m has $I=\tfrac12MR^2=0.0400$ kg m². A tangential 3.0 N push gives $\tau=Fr=0.600$ N m. Divide both sides of $0.600=0.0400\alpha$ by $I$: $$\alpha=15.0\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}.$$ From rest, after 2.0 s, $\omega=\alpha t=30.0$ rad/s and $\theta=\alpha t^2/2=30.0$ rad. Work and rotational energy agree: $$W=\tau\theta=0.600(30.0)=18.0\,\mathrm J =\tfrac12I\omega^2.$$ TR: Önce toplam torku bul, sonra $I$'ya böl. Kuvveti doğrudan $I\alpha$'ya eşitlemek birim bakımından yanlıştır.

Torque selects the force that turns about an axis

Picture. Draw a wrench, its pivot, the position vector from pivot to hand and the applied force. Extend the force's line of action. The shortest distance from pivot to that line is the lever arm $d_\perp=r\sin\phi$:

$$\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F,\qquad |\boldsymbol\tau|=Fd_\perp.$$

The right-hand rule gives the axial direction. In a planar problem, assign a sign to clockwise/counterclockwise turning and sum those signed torques. For the fixed-axis rigid-body model with constant $I$, $\sum\tau_z=I\alpha$ connects this turning effect to angular acceleration.

Predict. At the same distance from a hinge, compare pushing directly toward the hinge with pushing perpendicular to the door. Then double the distance while keeping the perpendicular force fixed.

Use the line of action

The radial push has zero torque because its line passes through the hinge. The perpendicular push gives magnitude $rF$; doubling its distance doubles torque. A large force is not enough to guarantee a large torque. The torque vector describes the axis and sense of turning, not the translation of the hand.

Transfer. A robot joint, a valve handle and a motor shaft use the same geometry. For a rotating shaft, power $P=\tau_z\omega$ is positive when torque and angular velocity have the same sign and negative during braking. Its units are watts, while torque itself is reported in $\mathrm{N\,m}$.

Rotational Kinetic Energy & Rolling Motion

Rotational Kinetic Energy

$$K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2$$

Rolling Without Slipping

When an object rolls without slipping: $$v_{\text{cm}} = R\omega, \quad a_{\text{cm}} = R\alpha$$

Total kinetic energy of a rolling object: $$K_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{cm}}^2 + \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{cm}}^2\left(1 + \frac{I}{mR^2}\right)$$

Rolling Down an Incline

Using energy conservation for an object starting from rest at height $h$:

$$mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\left(1 + \frac{I}{mR^2}\right)$$

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1 + I/(mR^2)}}$$

Key result: For the same ramp, release from rest, sufficient static friction to prevent slipping, and negligible dissipative losses, the object with the smallest $I/(mR^2)$ ratio reaches the bottom first. A solid sphere ($I/(mR^2) = 2/5$) beats a solid cylinder ($1/2$), which beats a ring ($1$). The shape matters, not the mass or radius!

Pulley-Rope Systems

When a pulley has mass (and therefore moment of inertia), we must include its rotational dynamics.

Atwood Machine with a Massive Pulley

Two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ connected by a rope over a pulley of mass $M$ and radius $R$:

For a nonzero pulley inertia and nonzero angular acceleration, the tensions on the two sides are different. Choose downward on the $m_1$ side and upward on the $m_2$ side as positive:

$$m_1 g - T_1 = m_1 a$$ $$T_2 - m_2 g = m_2 a$$ $$T_1 R - T_2 R = I\alpha = I \frac{a}{R}$$

Solving for the acceleration:

$$a = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2 + I/R^2}$$

For a solid disk pulley ($I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$):

$$a = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2 + M/2}$$

Key insight: The pulley's inertia acts like extra mass in the denominator, slowing the system down compared to a massless-pulley Atwood machine.

Eliminate tensions slowly. Add the two mass equations and use the pulley equation divided by $R$:

$$\begin{aligned}(m_1-m_2)g-(T_1-T_2)&=(m_1+m_2)a,\\T_1-T_2&=\frac{I}{R^2}a,\\(m_1-m_2)g&=\left(m_1+m_2+\frac{I}{R^2}\right)a.\end{aligned}$$

For $m_1=3.0\,\mathrm{kg}$, $m_2=1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and a $2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ disk pulley, $$a=\frac{(3-1)(9.81)}{3+1+2/2}=3.92\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

Then $T_1=3(9.81-3.92)=17.7\,\mathrm N$ and $T_2=1(9.81+3.92)=13.7\,\mathrm N$. TR: Gerilme farkı makaraya açısal ivme kazandırır. Eşit kütle sınırında $a=0$ ve gerilmeler eşit olabilir; makaraya kütle vermek tek başına her durumda fark yaratmaz.

Worked example 11.1

🔧 Worked Example 1: Moment of Inertia & Rolling

Problem: A solid cylinder (mass $4\,\mathrm{kg}$, radius $0.1\,\mathrm m$) and a hollow cylinder (same mass and radius) roll without slipping down a 2-meter high ramp ($30^\circ$). Find the speed of each at the bottom and the time each takes.

Paper solution. Assume both cylinders start from rest and the hollow cylinder is a thin shell. Rolling without slipping requires $\omega=v/R$. Write $I=\beta MR^2$ with $\beta=1/2$ for the solid cylinder and $\beta=1$ for the thin shell. $$Mgh=\tfrac12Mv^2+\tfrac12\beta MR^2(v/R)^2 \Rightarrow v=\sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+\beta}}.$$ For $h=2\,\mathrm m$, the solid cylinder has $v=\sqrt{\frac{39.24}{1.5}}=5.11\,\mathrm{m/s}$; the hollow cylinder has $v=\sqrt{\frac{39.24}{2}}=4.43\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

The path length is $s=h/\sin30^\circ=4\,\mathrm m$. Their constant accelerations are $a=g\sin30^\circ/(1+\beta)$: $3.27$ and $2.45\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. From $s=\tfrac12at^2$, multiply by 2 and divide by $a$: $t=\sqrt{2s/a}$. Thus $t_{\rm solid}=1.56\,\mathrm s$ and $t_{\rm hollow}=1.81\,\mathrm s$. A thicker hollow cylinder would need its inner radius to determine $I$.

Angular momentum and its conservationAçısal momentum ve korunumu

This section closes the course's stated learning outcome on angular momentum. It sits here because it is part of Dönme Hareketi Dinamiği — the dynamics of rotational motion — and it is the rotational twin of the momentum you used in Week 09.

The definition

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis with moment of inertia $I$ and angular velocity $\omega$:

Key equation 11.1
$$\,L = I\omega\, \qquad [\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}]$$

Compare the two columns and you have learned most of this section already:

Linear (Week 09)Rotational (now)
momentum $p = mv$angular momentum $L = I\omega$
force $\sum F$torque $\sum \tau$
$\sum F = \dfrac{dp}{dt}$$\sum \tau = \dfrac{dL}{dt}$
$p$ is conserved when $\sum F_{\text{ext}} = 0$$L$ is conserved when $\sum \tau_{\text{ext}} = 0$

Why $\sum\tau = dL/dt$ is the more fundamental statement

You have been using $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ all week. Start from the general form and see where it comes from:

$$\sum\tau = \frac{dL}{dt} = \frac{d(I\omega)}{dt}$$

If $I$ is constant, it comes out of the derivative and $d\omega/dt = \alpha$, giving

$$\sum\tau = I\frac{d\omega}{dt} = I\alpha.$$

So $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ is the special case in which the body's shape does not change. Read the derivative as a slope, as always: torque is the rate at which angular momentum changes. This matters because the interesting case is precisely the one where $I$ does change.

Conservation, and the one demonstration everybody remembers

With no external torque, $L$ is constant. If $I$ changes, $\omega$ must change to compensate:

$$I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2$$

A skater pulls her arms in. Her hands move closer to the axis, so $I$ falls — and because $I$ is a mass-times-distance-squared quantity, halving the effective radius of her arms quarters their contribution. With $L$ fixed, $\omega$ must rise. She spins faster, and nothing pushed her.

Worked example — symbolic first. A turntable of moment of inertia $I_0$ spins freely at $\omega_0$. A lump of clay of mass $m$ is dropped gently onto it at radius $r$ (no external torque about the axis, and the clay arrives with no angular momentum). Find the new angular velocity.

The clay adds $mr^2$ to the moment of inertia, so conservation gives

$$I_0\omega_0 = (I_0 + mr^2)\,\omega_1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\,\omega_1 = \frac{I_0}{I_0 + mr^2}\,\omega_0\,}$$

Limiting checks. $m \to 0$ gives $\omega_1 \to \omega_0$ ✅. $r \to 0$, the clay landing on the axis, also gives $\omega_1 \to \omega_0$ ✅ — dropping mass exactly on the axis changes nothing, which is a genuinely non-obvious prediction. And $mr^2 \gg I_0$ sends $\omega_1 \to 0$ ✅.

Now numbers. $I_0 = 0.0250\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $\omega_0 = 12.0\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, $m = 0.200\ \mathrm{kg}$ at $r = 0.150\ \mathrm{m}$:

$$mr^2 = (0.200)(0.150)^2 = 4.50\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},$$ $$\omega_1 = \frac{0.0250}{0.0250 + 0.00450}(12.0) = \frac{0.0250}{0.0295}(12.0) = 10.2\ \mathrm{rad/s}.$$

Is kinetic energy conserved here? No — check it: $K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2$ falls from $1.80\ \mathrm J$ to $1.53\ \mathrm J$. The clay had to be accelerated up to speed by friction as it settled, and that dissipates energy. This is the rotational version of the perfectly inelastic collision from Week 09: angular momentum is conserved, kinetic energy is not. The same asymmetry, for the same reason.

Angular momentum is an account about a chosen origin

For a particle, $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$: the cross product selects momentum transverse to the position vector. Momentum directed straight through the origin contributes no angular momentum about that origin. For the fixed-axis cases in this course, use the axial component $L_z=I\omega$.

Predict. A rotating assembly pulls two masses inward with negligible external torque about its axis. What changes: angular momentum, angular speed, or kinetic energy?

Conservation does not freeze every quantity

$L_z$ stays constant while $I$ decreases, so $\omega$ increases. Rotational kinetic energy $K=L_z^2/(2I)$ increases; work by the pulling mechanism supplies that increase. Conserved angular momentum does not imply conserved kinetic energy.

Draw a boundary around the assembly and name any motor, bearing or contact torque crossing that boundary before claiming conservation. In rolling problems, distinguish rotation about the centre from translation of the centre; $v_{\rm cm}=R\omega$ requires rolling without slipping. If a pulley accelerates, its two rope tensions generally differ because their torques must accelerate the pulley.

Worked example 11.2

Move a counterweight inward

Problem. A light rotor carries two point masses: $0.100\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $0.0800\,\mathrm m$ and $0.200\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $0.0400\,\mathrm m$ from its axis. A controller holds $30.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. During a redesign the first radius is halved. Compare the rotational energies at the same speed.

Picture and principle. Draw each mass at its radius. Point-mass inertia is $I=\sum mr^2$, and $K=I\omega^2/2$. Compute the two inertias before applying the common angular speed.

Limiting check. Moving a point mass to the axis removes its contribution. Halving its radius quarters that contribution, not necessarily the entire rotor inertia.

Numbers and units. $$I_{\rm old}=0.000960\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2},\quad I_{\rm new}=0.000480\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}.$$ At $30.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ the energies are $0.432\,\mathrm J$ and $0.216\,\mathrm J$, a $50.0\%$ reduction.

Interpret. This compares separately driven designs at the same speed. It does not assume angular momentum conservation during a moving-weight manoeuvre.

TÜRKÇE

Her kütlenin katkısı $mr^2$ ile bulunur. Burada iki tasarım aynı açısal hızda karşılaştırılır; hareket sırasında açısal momentum korunumu varsayılmaz.

Worked example 11.3

Find the usable direction for a handle force

Problem. A technician can apply a $40.0\,\mathrm N$ force at the end of a $0.180\,\mathrm m$ handle. A fastener needs $6.00\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. What range of angles between the handle and force can supply at least this torque magnitude?

Picture and principle. Draw the position vector from the fastener to the hand. Torque magnitude is $\tau=rF\sin\theta$. The requirement is $\sin\theta\ge\tau_{\min}/(rF)$; within $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$, the two boundaries are $\theta_0$ and $180^\circ-\theta_0$.

Limiting check. A force along the handle gives zero torque. At $90^\circ$ it gives the maximum $rF$; if that were below the requirement no angle would work.

Numbers and units. $$\theta_0=\sin^{-1}\!\left[\frac{6.00\,\mathrm{N\,m}}{(0.180\,\mathrm m)(40.0\,\mathrm N)}\right]=56.4^\circ.$$ The allowed range is approximately $56.4^\circ$ to $124^\circ$.

Interpret. Pulling harder is not the only way to increase torque; making the force more perpendicular also helps.

TÜRKÇE

Torku kuvvetin kola dik bileşeni oluşturur. Aynı kuvvet, uygun açıyla uygulandığında daha büyük tork sağlar.

Worked example 11.4

Size a rotational brake and its energy load

Problem. A flywheel with inertia $0.0250\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ spins at $60.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. A constant brake torque must stop it within $3.00$ revolutions. Find the minimum torque magnitude and the energy the brake must absorb.

Picture and principle. Draw braking torque opposite rotation. Work–energy gives $\tau_b\Delta\theta=I\omega_0^2/2$, so $\tau_{b,\min}=I\omega_0^2/(2\Delta\theta)$. Absorbed energy is the initial rotational kinetic energy.

Limiting check. Allowing a larger stop angle reduces the required torque but does not reduce the energy to dissipate.

Numbers and units. $$E=\tfrac12(0.0250)(60.0)^2\,\mathrm J=45.0\,\mathrm J,\qquad \tau_{b,\min}=\frac{45.0\,\mathrm J}{3.00(2\pi)\,\mathrm{rad}}=2.39\,\mathrm{N\,m}.$$

Interpret. Torque rating and energy capacity answer different design questions. A gentle stop still converts all the initial kinetic energy.

TÜRKÇE

Durma açısı büyüdükçe gereken tork azalır, fakat yok edilmesi gereken enerji değişmez. Tork ve enerji kapasitesi ayrı koşullardır.

Worked example 11.5

Rotate a fixture without accelerating its centre

Problem. Two actuators apply equal opposite horizontal forces of $18.0\,\mathrm N$ along parallel lines separated by $0.120\,\mathrm m$. A freely moving fixture has centre-of-mass moment of inertia $0.0360\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ about the perpendicular axis. Find its centre-of-mass acceleration and angular acceleration in the horizontal plane.

Picture and principle. Draw the two opposing forces on opposite sides of the centre. The resultant force is zero, but the couple torque is $\tau=Fd$. Therefore $a_{\rm CM}=0$ and $\alpha=Fd/I$. Vertical forces are balanced.

Limiting check. As the line separation tends to zero, the couple torque vanishes even though both forces remain.

Numbers and units. $$\tau=(18.0\,\mathrm N)(0.120\,\mathrm m)=2.16\,\mathrm{N\,m},\qquad \alpha=\frac{2.16\,\mathrm{N\,m}}{0.0360\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}}=60.0\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}.$$

Interpret. Zero net force does not mean zero angular acceleration. The separation used here is between the two force lines, not one force’s distance from the centre.

TÜRKÇE

Kuvvetlerin toplamı sıfırken torkları sıfır olmayabilir. Kuvvet çiftinde uzaklık, iki kuvvetin etki çizgileri arasındadır.

Worked example 11.6

Will a hollow roller keep up?

Problem. A thin-walled cylindrical roller starts from rest and rolls without slipping down a $15.0^\circ$ incline over $0.600\,\mathrm m$. A transfer mechanism allows $0.900\,\mathrm s$. Determine whether the roller arrives in time. Use $I=mR^2$ and assume enough static friction.

Picture and principle. Draw downhill $mg\sin\theta$ and uphill static friction $f$. Translation gives $mg\sin\theta-f=ma$; rotation gives $fR=I(a/R)$. Thus $a=g\sin\theta/[1+I/(mR^2)]$ and $t=\sqrt{2s/a}$.

Limiting check. With vanishing rotational inertia the acceleration tends to $g\sin\theta$. More rotational inertia means a slower descent.

Numbers and units. $$a=\frac{9.81\sin15.0^\circ}{2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}=1.27\,\mathrm{m/s^2},\qquad t=\sqrt{\frac{2(0.600)}{9.81\sin15.0^\circ/2}}\,\mathrm s=0.972\,\mathrm s.$$

Interpret. It misses the deadline. The no-slip assumption also needs sufficient friction; here $\mu_s\ge\tfrac12\tan15.0^\circ=0.134$.

TÜRKÇE

Yerçekimi enerjisi hem ötelemeyi hem dönmeyi besler. Kaymadan yuvarlanma için yeterli statik sürtünme gerekir.

Worked example 11.7

How high can a rolling ball climb?

Problem. A solid sphere of radius $0.0250\,\mathrm m$ rolls without slipping at $1.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ up a $10.0^\circ$ ramp. Neglect losses and use $I=2mR^2/5$. Find its maximum vertical rise and how many turns it makes before stopping.

Picture and principle. Draw the uphill motion and label rise $h=s\sin\theta$. With $\omega=v/R$, initial energy is $mv^2/2+I(v/R)^2/2=7mv^2/10$. Thus $h=7v^2/(10g)$ and turn count $n=s/(2\pi R)=h/(2\pi R\sin\theta)$.

Limiting check. At zero entry speed both rise and turns vanish. The rise is independent of ramp angle, but the path and turns are not.

Numbers and units. $$h=\frac{7(1.20)^2}{10(9.81)}\,\mathrm m=0.103\,\mathrm m,\qquad n=\frac{7(1.20)^2/[10(9.81)]}{2\pi(0.0250)\sin10.0^\circ}=3.77\ \text{turns}.$$

Interpret. Ignoring rotational energy underestimates the rise. Rolling without slipping allows static friction to exchange translation and rotation without dissipating energy.

TÜRKÇE

İlk enerjide dönme enerjisini unutma. Aynı düşey yükselme farklı eğimlerde farklı yol ve tur sayısı demektir.
