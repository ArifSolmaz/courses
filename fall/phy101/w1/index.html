  1. Units cancel like factors. $72\,\mathrm{km/h}\times\dfrac{1000\,\mathrm m}{1\,\mathrm{km}}\times\dfrac{1\,\mathrm h}{3600\,\mathrm s}=20\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Each ratio equals 1; the physical speed is unchanged, only its description.
  2. Square the whole quantity. $(-3\,\mathrm m)^2=+9\,\mathrm{m^2}$, but $-3^2=-9$ without parentheses. Keep parentheses around negative components.
  3. A right triangle hides in every vector. For a hypotenuse $A$ at angle $\theta$ from the horizontal, the sides are $A\cos\theta$ and $A\sin\theta$, and $A=\sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2}$. This one triangle is §1.8.
  4. Undo an operation on both sides. From $A_x=A\cos\theta$, divide both sides by $\cos\theta$ (if it is not zero) to get $A=A_x/\cos\theta$. Nothing "moves across" the equals sign; the same operation is applied to both sides.

Angle safety: $\arctan(A_y/A_x)$ on a calculator returns an angle between $-90^\circ$ and $+90^\circ$; the true direction may differ from it by $180^\circ$. Sketch the signs first — §1.8 returns to this trap with Fig. 1.20.

Section 1.1: The nature of physicsFiziğin doğası

Physics is an experimental science. Physicists observe what nature does and look for patterns that connect one phenomenon to another. A physical law describes a relationship found in nature; a physical theory is an explanatory framework that connects observations and makes testable predictions. A theory does not become a law as evidence accumulates: they serve different roles.

Watch out

Caution — the meaning of "theory". In everyday speech a theory is a guess. In physics it is the opposite: an explanation of natural phenomena built on observation and on accepted fundamental principles. Biological evolution is a theory in exactly this sense — the product of generations of observation — and so is Newton's mechanics.

A theory is never the final word. New observations can force a theory to be revised or replaced. A reproducible disagreement within the stated assumptions and measurement uncertainties can refute a prediction. First check the apparatus, analysis and assumptions; an unexplained discrepancy alone is not enough. No number of agreeing experiments can establish a theory for every possible situation. What a theory carries instead is a range of validity.

The standard example is Galileo's. Legend has it that he dropped light and heavy objects from the Leaning Tower of Pisa (his real experiments were more careful than the legend) and concluded that the acceleration of a freely falling object does not depend on its weight. Drop a feather and a cannonball and they plainly do not fall together — but that does not make Galileo wrong; it shows where his theory stops. In a vacuum the two fall side by side. His theory applies to objects on which the force of the air is much smaller than the weight, and a feather is outside that range.

Engineering reading. A rigid link, a frictionless bearing and a massless cable are idealizations used in engineering models. Each model has a range of validity. Knowing the range is part of knowing the model.

Section 1.2: Solving physics problemsFizik problemlerini çözme

Almost every student says at some point, "I understand the concepts, but I can't solve the problems." In physics those are the same thing: understanding a concept means being able to apply it. You do not know physics until you can do physics.

Every Problem-Solving Strategy and every worked example in the textbook — and every worked example and problem in this course — follows four stages. Remember them as I SEE: Identify, Set up, Execute, Evaluate.

Problem-Solving Strategy 1.1 — Solving physics problems

Stage: IDENTIFY the relevant concepts

  • What you actually do: Use the physical situation to decide which concepts apply. Name the target variables — the quantities you are asked for. List the known quantities, stated or implied. Do this whether the answer wanted is a number or an algebraic expression.

Stage: SET UP the problem

  • What you actually do: Choose the equations you will use and decide how. Check that the symbols you identified match the symbols in those equations. Draw a sketch — graph paper and a ruler help.

Stage: EXECUTE the solution

  • What you actually do: Do the mathematics: solve the chosen equations for the target variables.

Stage: EVALUATE your answer

  • What you actually do: Is it reasonable? (A thrown baseball rising 1.0 mm or 100 km is not.) If the answer is algebraic, does it behave sensibly when a variable becomes very large or very small? Note any result of general importance, and ask how you would solve a harder version.

How this maps onto the course method. The course's six steps — draw → choose a law → rearrange to the symbolic answer → check a limiting case → substitute with units → interpret — are I SEE with the EXECUTE stage split in two: the symbolic answer comes first, and one limiting case is checked before any number is substituted. The limiting-case check is the textbook's EVALUATE advice ("confirm that it correctly represents what would happen if the variables had very large or very small values") moved before the arithmetic, where it catches an algebra slip for free.

Idealized modelsİdealleştirilmiş modeller

In physics a model is a simplified version of a physical system that would be too complicated to analyse in full. Take a thrown baseball. The real ball has raised seams, spins, is pushed by air resistance and wind, and even weighs slightly less as it climbs. Include everything and the problem is hopeless. Instead we idealize: treat the ball as a point object (a particle) with no size, shape or rotation; remove the air; make the weight constant. The result is the model of Chapter 3, and it is simple enough to solve.

The skill is in what to leave out. Leave out too much — ignore gravity altogether — and the model predicts a thrown ball flies off in a straight line forever. A useful model simplifies enough to be manageable and keeps the essential features. Its predictions are only as good as its assumptions: the vacuum model of Galileo works well for a cannonball and badly for a feather.

Section 1.3: Standards and unitsStandartlar ve birimler

Experiments require measurements, and a measurement is reported as a number. Any number used to describe a physical phenomenon quantitatively is a physical quantity — your height and your weight are two of them. Some quantities are so fundamental that they can only be defined by saying how to measure them: an operational definition. Distance is what a ruler measures; a time interval is what a stopwatch measures. Other quantities are defined by a rule for computing them from measured ones: average speed is distance (ruler) divided by time (stopwatch).

To measure is to compare with a standard. "The basketball hoop is 3.05 metres above the floor" means the height is 3.05 times the length we agreed to call one metre. The standard defines a unit, and a number without its unit says nothing — "3.05" on its own is not a height. Since 1960 the scientists' and engineers' system of units has officially been the International System, SI (from the French Système International).

Quantity: Time

  • SI unit: second, s

  • How the standard is defined today: Since 1967: the duration of 9 192 631 770 periods of radiation corresponding to the unperturbed ground-state hyperfine transition of caesium-133. Atomic clocks realize this standard. Before this, the ephemeris second was based on a fraction of a tropical year; earlier time standards used the mean solar day.

Quantity: Length

  • SI unit: metre, m

  • How the standard is defined today: Since 1983: the distance light travels in vacuum in 1/299 792 458 second — that is, the speed of light is defined to be exactly $c=299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m/s}$. From 1960 to 1983 the metre was a number of wavelengths of krypton-86 light.

Quantity: Mass

  • SI unit: kilogram, kg

  • How the standard is defined today: Since 20 May 2019 (adopted in 2018): fixed through Planck's constant, $h=6.626\,070\,15\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}$ (exactly), together with the definitions of the metre and the second. Until that date it was the mass of a platinum-iridium cylinder kept near Paris — an awkward standard to copy. The gram is 0.001 kg and is not a fundamental unit.

Length, mass and time are the three fundamental quantities of mechanics. Every other mechanical unit is derived from them by multiplication and division: speed is length over time, $\mathrm{m/s}$; the newton, the SI unit of force, is $\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s^2}$.

For mass the prefixes attach to the gram, not the kilogram.

Section 1.4: Using and converting unitsBirimleri kullanma ve dönüştürme

An algebraic symbol in a physics equation always stands for a number and a unit together: $d$ might be 10 m, $t$ might be 5 s, $v$ might be 2 m/s. That has a consequence you will use every week: an equation must be dimensionally consistent. You cannot add apples to automobiles; terms must have compatible dimensions and represent compatible quantities. Convert to a common unit before adding numerical values. If an object at constant speed $v$ covers distance $d$ in time $t$,

$$\begin{aligned} d&=vt \\ \text{and with the numbers above} \\ 10\ \mathrm m&=\Big(2\ \frac{\mathrm m}{\mathrm s}\Big)(5\ \mathrm s). \end{aligned}$$

The second in the denominator of m/s cancels the second of $t$, and the product has the unit of $d$, as it must. Units are multiplied and divided exactly like algebraic symbols.

Watch out

Caution — always use the units. Write every number with its unit and carry the units through every line. If at any stage an expression has inconsistent units, you have found an error — before it costs you the answer. The textbook does this in every example; so does this course.

Problem-Solving Strategy 1.2 — Unit conversions

  • IDENTIFY: In most problems it is best to work in the fundamental SI units (m, kg, s) throughout. If the answer is wanted in other units (km, g, h), convert at the end.
  • SET UP and EXECUTE: The same physical quantity written in two units gives an equality, $1\ \mathrm{min}=60\ \mathrm s$. This does not say the number 1 equals the number 60; it says one minute is the same time interval as sixty seconds. So the ratio $(1\ \mathrm{min})/(60\ \mathrm s)$ equals 1, and so does its reciprocal $(60\ \mathrm s)/(1\ \mathrm{min})$. Such ratios are unit multipliers: multiplying by one of them changes the description of a quantity, never the quantity. To find the number of seconds in 3 min,

$$\begin{aligned} 3\ \mathrm{min}&=(3\ \mathrm{min})\Big(\frac{60\ \mathrm s}{1\ \mathrm{min}}\Big) \\ &=180\ \mathrm s. \end{aligned}$$

  • EVALUATE: Done correctly, the unwanted units cancel. Multiply 3 min by the other ratio and you get $\tfrac1{20}\ \mathrm{min^2/s}$, a valid time quantity in mixed units that has not completed the requested conversion to seconds — which is exactly why the units must be written at every stage. Then ask whether the result is reasonable: a second is a smaller unit than a minute, so there must be more seconds than minutes in the same interval; 180 > 3.

IDENTIFY, SET UP. A speed is a length over a time, so two unit multipliers are needed: one taking miles to metres, one taking hours to seconds. Use the exact international-mile definition, rather than the textbook's rounded multiplier: $1\ \mathrm{mi}=1.609344\ \mathrm{km}$, $1\ \mathrm{km}=1000\ \mathrm m$, $1\ \mathrm h=3600\ \mathrm s$. Symbolic route: $v_{\rm SI}=v\,\dfrac{L_{\rm km/mi}\,L_{\rm m/km}}{T_{\rm s/h}}$.

Limiting check. Every multiplier equals 1, so a speed of zero stays zero and the ratio of any two speeds is unchanged by the conversion.

EXECUTE. Arrange each ratio so the unwanted unit cancels by division:

$$\begin{aligned} 1\ \mathrm{mi/h}&=\frac{1609.344}{3600}\ \mathrm{m/s}\\ &=0.44704\ \mathrm{m/s},\\ v&=(763.0)(0.44704)\ \mathrm{m/s}\\ &=341.09152\ \mathrm{m/s}\\ &\approx341\ \mathrm{m/s}. \end{aligned}$$

The course reports this as $341\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (three significant figures); retaining the four figures in the given speed would give $341.1\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The converter below uses the same exact definition.

Section 1.5: Uncertainty and significant figuresBelirsizlik ve anlamlı basamaklar

Measurements have uncertainty. A ruler might report a book-cover thickness as 3 mm, while a micrometer reports 2.91 mm. Their difference is a discrepancy between readings, not the uncertainty of either measurement. Uncertainty describes the dispersion of values reasonably attributable to the measured quantity, given the available information. It can include resolution, repeatability, calibration, alignment and environmental effects. Careful technique matters.

Error is different. Measurement error is the measured value minus a reference value; the true error is usually not known exactly. An uncertainty is not a known error or a guaranteed maximum error. Do not infer it solely from the final displayed digit.

Stating uncertainty. Write the value with its uncertainty and explain how that uncertainty was obtained. For example, $(56.47\pm0.02)\ \mathrm{mm}$ needs a statement of whether 0.02 mm is a standard uncertainty or an expanded interval with a specified coverage. The notation alone does not specify a probability. The shorthand $1.6454(21)$ means $1.6454\pm0.0021$. Its two uncertainty digits illustrate the notation; in laboratory reports this course normally rounds uncertainties to one significant figure. Relative uncertainty is $u(x)/|x|$; multiplying by 100 gives percent uncertainty. It is distinct from percent error against a reference. A resistor's ±10% label is a manufacturing tolerance, not a measurement uncertainty.

Significant figures. They communicate the precision of reported numbers, but are not a full uncertainty analysis. The customary introductory rules below avoid unjustified digits when no explicit uncertainties are supplied. 2.91 mm has three significant figures; its last reported decimal place is 0.01 mm, but calibration or other effects may make its uncertainty larger. Leading zeros do not count: 0.25 km has two figures, 0.250 km has three.

Table 1.2 — using significant figures in a calculation

Operation: Multiplication or division

  • Rule: The result can have no more significant figures than the factor with the fewest significant figures.

  • Example: $\dfrac{0.745\times2.2}{3.885}=0.42$; $\quad1.32578\times10^7\times4.11\times10^{-3}=5.45\times10^4$

Operation: Addition or subtraction

  • Rule: The result is limited by the term with the least resolved reported decimal place (after expressing all terms in the same units) — the position of the last digit matters, not the count of significant figures.

  • Example: $27.153+138.2-11.74=153.6$; $\quad123.62+8.9=132.5$ (not 132.52)

Try it on $\pi$. Draw a large circle and measure its circumference and diameter to the nearest millimetre: 424 mm and 135 mm. The calculator says $424/135=3.140740741$. That looks like a disagreement with $\pi=3.141592654$, but each measurement has only three significant figures, so the measured ratio can have only three: 3.14, and within three figures it agrees.

Rounding, not truncating. Examples and problems in the textbook and in this course give data to three significant figures, so answers should usually have three, or in doubtful cases one more at most. (Many real numbers have fewer: a car speedometer gives two.) A ten-digit calculator answer misrepresents the accuracy; in Example 1.1 it would have been wrong to write 341.09152 m/s. When you shorten, round: $525\ \mathrm m/311\ \mathrm m=1.688102894$ becomes 1.69, not 1.68. In a calculation with several steps, keep extra figures in the intermediate values and round only the final answer — this course's displayed intermediates are rounded for reading; the arithmetic carries full precision rule is exactly this advice.

Scientific notation shows significant figures cleanly. The Earth–Moon distance written as 384,000,000 m does not say how many figures are meaningful; written as $3.84\times10^8\ \mathrm m$ it plainly has three, and $4.00\times10^{-7}$ also has three even though two of them are zeros. Write the number as a value between 1 and 10 times a power of 10.

Exact numbers. A mathematical coefficient or a counted exact number has no measurement uncertainty; a measured value does not become exact merely because it is written as an integer. For example, the 2 in $v_x^2=v_{0x}^2+2a_x(x-x_0)$ (Eq. 2.13 next week) is exactly 2, with infinitely many significant figures, and so is the exponent 2. The same is true of defined conversions such as $1\ \mathrm{in.}=2.54\ \mathrm{cm}$.

Resolution, precision and accuracy. Resolution is the smallest change an instrument can display or distinguish. Precision concerns agreement among repeated measurements under stated conditions. Accuracy concerns closeness to the true value. A watch that displays seconds has fine resolution, but it may still be systematically several minutes slow. More digits alone do not demonstrate either precision or accuracy.

Section 1.6: Estimates and orders of magnitudeKestirimler ve büyüklük mertebeleri

We have insisted on knowing the accuracy of a number. But even a very crude number can be useful. Sometimes you know how to calculate a quantity but must guess the input data; sometimes the exact calculation is too hard and you approximate roughly. Either way the result is a guess — but a guess that is right to within a factor of two, or ten, can still settle a question. These are order-of-magnitude estimates; Enrico Fermi called them back-of-the-envelope calculations, and problems that ask for them (the textbook's Exercises 1.15–1.20) expect you to guess the data rather than look it up.

The method: pick the physical relation (density × volume, rate × time, count × size); estimate each input to the nearest power of ten or to one figure; multiply; then ask only whether the answer is small, comparable or huge compared with something you know.

Try it (Test your understanding of §1.6): estimate the total number of teeth in the mouths of everyone on your campus. Hint: count your own. The answer depends on your enrolment — the method does not.

Section 1.7: Vectors and vector additionVektörler ve vektör toplama

Some quantities — time, temperature, mass, density — are completely described by one number with a unit. These are scalar quantities, and they combine by ordinary arithmetic: $6\ \mathrm{kg}+3\ \mathrm{kg}=9\ \mathrm{kg}$, $4\times2\ \mathrm s=8\ \mathrm s$.

Many of the important quantities in physics are not like that. To describe the motion of an aeroplane you must say not only how fast it moves but in what direction; speed together with direction of motion is called velocity. A force — a push or a pull — likewise needs both how hard and which way. A quantity with a magnitude and a direction in space is a vector quantity, and vectors do not combine by ordinary arithmetic.

The simplest vector quantity is displacement: a change in the position of an object. It is a vector because walking 3 km north from your front door does not put you where walking 3 km southeast would: same magnitude, different direction.

Notation. A vector is printed in boldface italic with an arrow, $\vec{\mathbf A}$; when you write by hand, always put the arrow on top. If you do not distinguish vectors from scalars in your notation you will not distinguish them in your thinking either. A vector is drawn as a line with an arrowhead: the length of the line shows the magnitude, the arrowhead shows the direction. A displacement is always a straight arrow from the starting point to the ending point, even when the actual path is curved — and if the object returns to where it began, the displacement for the whole trip is zero, no matter how far it travelled.

Equal, negative, parallel, antiparallel. Two vectors are parallel if they have the same direction, and equal if they have the same magnitude and the same direction — no matter where in space they are drawn. The negative of $\vec{\mathbf A}$, written $-\vec{\mathbf A}$, has the same magnitude and the opposite direction: if $\vec{\mathbf A}$ is 87 m south, $-\vec{\mathbf A}$ is 87 m north. Two vectors with opposite directions, whatever their magnitudes, are antiparallel.

The magnitude of a vector is written with the same letter in lightface italic, or between vertical bars — equation (1.1) of the textbook:

$$\begin{aligned} (\text{magnitude of }\vec{\mathbf A})&=A \\ &=\lvert\vec{\mathbf A}\rvert. \end{aligned}$$

A magnitude is a scalar, and it is nonnegative (zero for the zero vector). A vector can never equal a scalar: "$\vec{\mathbf A}=6\ \mathrm m$" is as wrong as "2 oranges = 3 apples". When you draw vector diagrams, use a scale, as on a map: 1 cm on the paper for 5 km of displacement.

Vector addition and subtractionVektör toplama ve çıkarma

Let a particle undergo displacement $\vec{\mathbf A}$ and then displacement $\vec{\mathbf B}$. The result is the same as a single displacement $\vec{\mathbf C}$ from the original start to the final point. $\vec{\mathbf C}$ is the vector sum, or resultant — equation (1.2):

$$\vec{\mathbf C}=\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf B}.$$

The boldface plus sign is a warning: adding vectors is a geometrical operation, not the arithmetic of $2+3=5$. To do it, place the tail of the second vector at the head (tip) of the first, and draw the sum from the first tail to the last head.

Three constructions give the same answer. (i) Head to tail, as above. (ii) In the reverse order — equation (1.3):

$$\begin{aligned} \vec{\mathbf C}&=\vec{\mathbf B}+\vec{\mathbf A} \\ \text{so} \\ \vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf B}&=\vec{\mathbf B}+\vec{\mathbf A}. \end{aligned}$$

Vector addition obeys the commutative law. (iii) Draw $\vec{\mathbf A}$ and $\vec{\mathbf B}$ tail to tail; the sum is the diagonal of the parallelogram they span.

Watch out

Caution — magnitudes in vector addition. It is a common error to conclude that if $\vec{\mathbf C}=\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf B}$ then $C=A+B$. In general this is wrong. The magnitude of $\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf B}$ depends on $A$, on $B$ and on the angle between them. Only when the two are parallel does $C=A+B$; when they are antiparallel, $C=\lvert A-B\rvert$. Everywhere in between, $C$ lies between those two values.

Three or more vectors. For $\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf B}+\vec{\mathbf C}$ you may first add $\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf B}=\vec{\mathbf D}$ and then $\vec{\mathbf R}=\vec{\mathbf D}+\vec{\mathbf C}$; or first $\vec{\mathbf B}+\vec{\mathbf C}=\vec{\mathbf E}$ and then $\vec{\mathbf R}=\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf E}$. The results are identical — vector addition obeys the associative law. In practice you need draw no intermediate vector at all: lay the arrows head to tail in any order you like, and the sum runs from the tail of the first to the head of the last.

Subtraction. Since $-\vec{\mathbf B}$ is $\vec{\mathbf B}$ reversed, the difference is defined as a sum — equation (1.4):

$$\vec{\mathbf A}-\vec{\mathbf B}=\vec{\mathbf A}+(-\vec{\mathbf B}).$$

Draw it either way: put the tail of $-\vec{\mathbf B}$ at the head of $\vec{\mathbf A}$, or place $\vec{\mathbf A}$ and $\vec{\mathbf B}$ head to head, in which case $\vec{\mathbf A}-\vec{\mathbf B}$ runs from the tail of $\vec{\mathbf A}$ to the tail of $\vec{\mathbf B}$.

Multiplying a vector by a scalar. The displacement $2\vec{\mathbf A}$ points the same way as $\vec{\mathbf A}$ and is twice as long — the same as adding $\vec{\mathbf A}$ to itself. In general $c\vec{\mathbf A}$ has magnitude $\lvert c\rvert A$; it is parallel to $\vec{\mathbf A}$ when $c>0$ and antiparallel when $c<0$. So $3\vec{\mathbf A}$ is parallel to $\vec{\mathbf A}$ and $-3\vec{\mathbf A}$ is antiparallel and three times as long. The scalar may itself be a physical quantity: in $\vec{\mathbf F}=m\vec{\mathbf a}$ the mass $m$ is a positive scalar, so $\vec{\mathbf F}$ has the direction of $\vec{\mathbf a}$ and magnitude $ma$ — and the unit of force is the unit of mass times the unit of acceleration.

Section 1.8: Components of vectorsVektörlerin bileşenleri

Adding vectors with a scale drawing works, but only to the accuracy of your ruler, and the right-triangle shortcut of Example 1.5 works only when the two vectors are perpendicular. What is needed is a method that is simple and general. It is the method of components.

Set up a rectangular (Cartesian) coordinate system. Think of $\vec{\mathbf A}$ as a displacement, and regard it as the sum of a displacement parallel to the $x$-axis and a displacement parallel to the $y$-axis. The two numbers $A_x$ and $A_y$ that say how much displacement there is along each axis are the components of $\vec{\mathbf A}$. If $+x$ is east and $+y$ is north, a vector that is 2.00 m east and 1.00 m north has $A_x=+2.00\ \mathrm m$ and $A_y=+1.00\ \mathrm m$. The same idea works for any vector quantity, not just displacement.

Watch out

Caution — components are not vectors. $A_x$ and $A_y$ are numbers. They are printed in lightface italic with no arrow, precisely because they are not vectors themselves.

Finding the components. Describe the direction of $\vec{\mathbf A}$ by the angle $\theta$ it makes with the positive $x$-axis, measured from $+x$ toward $+y$ (so $+y$ is at $90^\circ$, $-x$ at $180^\circ$, $-y$ at $270^\circ$ or $-90^\circ$). Then from the definitions of the trigonometric functions, $A_x/A=\cos\theta$ and $A_y/A=\sin\theta$ — equations (1.5):

$$\begin{aligned} A_x&=A\cos\theta \\ A_y&=A\sin\theta \\ (\theta\text{ measured from }+x\text{ toward }+y). \end{aligned}$$

The signs take care of themselves. In the first quadrant both cosine and sine are positive, so both components are positive. In the second quadrant ($\theta$ between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$) the cosine is negative and the sine positive: a vector 2.00 m west and 1.00 m north has $B_x=-2.00\ \mathrm m$, $B_y=+1.00\ \mathrm m$. In the third quadrant both are negative.

Watch out

Caution — use the angle as defined. If the angle you are given is measured from a different axis, or in the other sense, the relations are different. Example 1.6 is about exactly this.

Using components to do vector calculations

1. Magnitude and direction from the components. A vector is described completely either by its magnitude and direction or by its components, and each description can be recovered from the other. Pythagoras on the right triangle gives equation (1.6),

$$A=\sqrt{A_x^{\,2}+A_y^{\,2}}$$

(always the positive root; valid for any pair of mutually perpendicular axes), and the definition of the tangent gives equations (1.7),

$$\begin{aligned} \tan\theta&=\frac{A_y}{A_x} \\ \theta&=\arctan\frac{A_y}{A_x}. \end{aligned}$$

Watch out

Caution — the $180^\circ$ ambiguity. Any two angles differing by $180^\circ$ have the same tangent. If $\tan\theta=A_y/A_x=+1$ your calculator returns $\theta=45^\circ$, but $225^\circ$ has tangent $+1$ as well — and if $A_x=-2\ \mathrm m$ and $A_y=-2\ \mathrm m$ (third quadrant) the true answer is $225^\circ$. The calculator cannot know; only the sketch can. Always draw the vector and check which of the two possibilities is right. This is the textbook's Fig. 1.20, and it is the single most common error in this chapter.

On an axis. If $A_x=0$ and $A_y\ne0$, do not divide by zero: the direction is $90^\circ$ for $A_y>0$ and $270^\circ$ for $A_y<0$. If both components are zero, direction is undefined. Software uses $\operatorname{atan2}(A_y,A_x)$ to handle quadrants and vertical vectors; the zero vector still needs a separate check.

2. Multiplying a vector by a scalar. Each component of $\vec{\mathbf D}=c\vec{\mathbf A}$ is $c$ times the corresponding component of $\vec{\mathbf A}$ — equations (1.8):

$$D_x=cA_x,\qquad D_y=cA_y.$$

So $2\vec{\mathbf A}$ has twice each component (same direction, twice the magnitude) and $-3\vec{\mathbf A}$ has three times each component with the opposite sign — exactly what §1.7 said in words.

3. Adding vectors with components. This is the payoff. The $x$-component of a sum is the sum of the $x$-components, and likewise for $y$ — the textbook's equations (1.9), and the first of this week's key equations:

Key equation 1.9
$$R_x=A_x+B_x,\qquad R_y=A_y+B_y$$

For any number of vectors $\vec{\mathbf A},\vec{\mathbf B},\vec{\mathbf C},\vec{\mathbf D},\vec{\mathbf E},\dots$ this extends to equations (1.10):

$$\begin{aligned} R_x&=A_x+B_x+C_x+D_x+E_x+\cdots \\ R_y&=A_y+B_y+C_y+D_y+E_y+\cdots. \end{aligned}$$

Then equations (1.6) and (1.7), with $A$ replaced by $R$, give the magnitude and direction of the resultant. Components add; magnitudes do not — and this single fact is what makes the whole method work.

Three dimensions. Nothing changes in principle. Add a $z$-axis perpendicular to the $xy$-plane; a vector then has components $A_x$, $A_y$, $A_z$, the sum has a third component $R_z=A_z+B_z+C_z+\cdots$, and the magnitude is equation (1.11):

$$A=\sqrt{A_x^{\,2}+A_y^{\,2}+A_z^{\,2}}.$$

We have used displacements throughout, but the method applies to every vector quantity. When force arrives in Chapter 4, forces will be added by exactly these rules.

Problem-Solving Strategy 1.3 — Vector addition

  • IDENTIFY: decide what the target variable is — the magnitude of the sum, the direction, or both.
  • SET UP: sketch the vectors head to tail with suitable axes, draw the resultant $\vec{\mathbf R}$ from the first tail to the last head, and estimate its magnitude and direction from the sketch. Choose the equations: (1.5) for the components of each vector, (1.10) for the components of the sum, (1.11) or (1.6) for its magnitude, (1.7) for its direction.
  • EXECUTE: (a) find $A_x$ and $A_y$ for every vector and record them in a table, converting any angle that is measured from some other reference into an angle from $+x$ (as in Example 1.6); (b) add the $x$-components algebraically, including signs, to get $R_x$, and the $y$-components to get $R_y$; (c) compute $R=\sqrt{R_x^2+R_y^2}$ and $\theta=\arctan(R_y/R_x)$.
  • EVALUATE: compare with the estimate from your sketch. The calculator's $\theta$ may be off by $180^\circ$; the drawing settles it (Example 1.7 shows this happening).

Section 1.9: Unit vectorsBirim vektörler

A unit vector is a vector of magnitude 1, with no units. Its only job is to point — to name a direction in space. A caret, or "hat", distinguishes it from an ordinary vector: $\hat{\imath}$, not $\vec{\imath}$.

In an $xy$-coordinate system, let $\hat{\imath}$ point along $+x$ and $\hat{\jmath}$ along $+y$. Then $A_x\hat{\imath}$ is a vector of magnitude $\lvert A_x\rvert$ along the $x$-axis, and any vector can be written as the sum of its two component vectors — equation (1.12):

$$\vec{\mathbf A}=A_x\hat{\imath}+A_y\hat{\jmath}.$$

This is a vector equation: each term, such as $A_x\hat{\imath}$, is a vector quantity, while $A_x$ alone is a number. The notation earns its keep immediately, because the sum of two vectors can now be written in a single line instead of two — equation (1.13):

$$\begin{aligned} \vec{\mathbf R}&=\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf B} \\ &=(A_x\hat{\imath}+A_y\hat{\jmath})+(B_x\hat{\imath}+B_y\hat{\jmath}) \\ &=(A_x+B_x)\hat{\imath}+(A_y+B_y)\hat{\jmath} \\ &=R_x\hat{\imath}+R_y\hat{\jmath}. \end{aligned}$$

That is exactly the content of equations (1.9), now as one vector equation rather than two component equations.

Three dimensions. Add $\hat{k}$ along $+z$. Equations (1.12) and (1.13) become (1.14) and (1.15) — the second key equation of this week:

Key equation 1.14
$$\vec{\mathbf A}=A_x\hat{\imath}+A_y\hat{\jmath}+A_z\hat{k}$$

Section 1.10: The scalar (dot) productSkaler (nokta) çarpım

Vector addition grew naturally out of combining displacements. Many other physical relationships are expressed by multiplying vectors — but vectors are not ordinary numbers, so ordinary multiplication does not apply. Two different products are defined, and they answer different physical questions. The scalar product gives a scalar; the vector product gives another vector.

The scalar product of $\vec{\mathbf A}$ and $\vec{\mathbf B}$ is written $\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}$ and, because of that notation, is also called the dot product. Draw the two vectors tail to tail; the angle $\phi$ between their directions runs from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$. Now project $\vec{\mathbf B}$ onto the direction of $\vec{\mathbf A}$: that projection is the component of $\vec{\mathbf B}$ along $\vec{\mathbf A}$, equal to $B\cos\phi$. (Components may be taken along any convenient direction, not only the $x$- and $y$-axes.) The scalar product is the magnitude of $\vec{\mathbf A}$ multiplied by that component — equation (1.16), and this week's third key equation:

Key equation 1.16
$$\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}=AB\cos\phi=\lvert\vec{\mathbf A}\rvert\,\lvert\vec{\mathbf B}\rvert\cos\phi$$

Equivalently, project $\vec{\mathbf A}$ onto $\vec{\mathbf B}$: $\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}=B(A\cos\phi)=AB\cos\phi$, the same number. The order does not matter — the scalar product obeys the commutative law, $\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}=\vec{\mathbf B}\cdot\vec{\mathbf A}$.

Sign. The scalar product is a scalar, and it may be positive, negative or zero:

Angle between them: $0^\circ$ (parallel)

  • $\cos\phi$: $+1$

  • $\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}$: $+AB$, the largest possible value

Angle between them: between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$

  • $\cos\phi$: positive

  • $\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}$: positive

Angle between them: $90^\circ$ (perpendicular)

  • $\cos\phi$: $0$

  • $\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}$: zero — always, for any magnitudes

Angle between them: between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$

  • $\cos\phi$: negative

  • $\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}$: negative

Angle between them: $180^\circ$ (antiparallel)

  • $\cos\phi$: $-1$

  • $\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}$: $-AB$

The scalar product of two perpendicular vectors is always zero, and this is the usual way to test for perpendicularity.

Components. The chosen Cartesian basis vectors $\hat{\imath}$, $\hat{\jmath}$ and $\hat{k}$ have magnitude 1 and are mutually perpendicular (arbitrary unit vectors need not be), so from equation (1.16) — equations (1.17):

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\imath}\cdot\hat{\imath}&=\hat{\jmath}\cdot\hat{\jmath} \\ &=\hat{k}\cdot\hat{k} \\ &=(1)(1)\cos0^\circ \\ &=1 \\ \hat{\imath}\cdot\hat{\jmath}&=\hat{\imath}\cdot\hat{k} \\ &=\hat{\jmath}\cdot\hat{k} \\ &=(1)(1)\cos90^\circ \\ &=0. \end{aligned}$$

Expanding $\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}$ in components gives nine terms (equation 1.18), of which the six mixed ones vanish, leaving equation (1.19) — the fourth key equation:

Key equation 1.19
$$\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}=A_xB_x+A_yB_y+A_zB_z$$

The scalar product of two vectors is the sum of the products of their respective components. Together, (1.16) and (1.19) give the standard way to find the angle between two vectors whose components are known: compute the product from (1.19), the magnitudes from (1.11), and solve $\cos\phi=(\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B})/AB$.

Where it is going. In Week 05 the scalar product defines the work done by a constant force: $W=\vec{\mathbf F}\cdot\vec{\mathbf s}$. A force perpendicular to the displacement does no work — which is the row "$90^\circ$ → zero" in the table above, read as physics.

Problem. Find the scalar product $\vec{\mathbf A}\cdot\vec{\mathbf B}$ of two vectors in the $xy$-plane with magnitudes $A=4.00$ and $B=5.00$, where $\vec{\mathbf A}$ is at $53.0^\circ$ and $\vec{\mathbf B}$ at $130.0^\circ$, both measured from $+x$ toward $+y$.

Problem. Find the angle between the vectors

$$\begin{aligned} \vec{\mathbf A}&=2.00\hat{\imath}+3.00\hat{\jmath}+1.00\hat{k} \\ \text{and} \\ \vec{\mathbf B}&=-4.00\hat{\imath}+2.00\hat{\jmath}-1.00\hat{k}. \end{aligned}$$

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Section 1.10 continued: The vector (cross) productVektörel (çapraz) çarpım

The vector product of $\vec{\mathbf A}$ and $\vec{\mathbf B}$ is written $\vec{\mathbf A}\times\vec{\mathbf B}$ and is also called the cross product. As the name says, it is itself a vector. Chapter 10 uses it for torque and angular momentum (Week 11 of this course), and Chapters 27–28 for magnetic fields and forces.

Draw the two vectors tail to tail; they define a plane. The vector product is defined to be perpendicular to that plane — perpendicular to both $\vec{\mathbf A}$ and $\vec{\mathbf B}$ — with magnitude given by equation (1.20), this week's fifth key equation:

Key equation 1.20
$$C=AB\sin\phi$$

where $\phi$ is again measured from $\vec{\mathbf A}$ toward $\vec{\mathbf B}$, taking the smaller of the two possible angles, so $0^\circ\le\phi\le180^\circ$ and $\sin\phi\ge0$; a magnitude is never negative.

Two consequences to memorise. When $\vec{\mathbf A}$ and $\vec{\mathbf B}$ are parallel or antiparallel ($\phi=0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$), $C=0$: the vector product of two parallel or antiparallel vectors is always zero, and in particular the vector product of any vector with itself is zero. When they are perpendicular, $\sin\phi=1$ and the magnitude is as large as it can be, $AB$.

Watch out

Caution — vector against scalar product. Do not confuse $AB\sin\phi$ with $AB\cos\phi$. Hold $A$ and $B$ fixed and turn one vector: when they are parallel the vector product vanishes and the scalar product is largest; when they are perpendicular the vector product is largest and the scalar product vanishes. The two products are complementary — which is precisely why physics needs both.

Direction: the right-hand rule. A plane has two perpendicular directions, one on each side. Which one is $\vec{\mathbf A}\times\vec{\mathbf B}$? Imagine rotating $\vec{\mathbf A}$ about the perpendicular line until it lines up with $\vec{\mathbf B}$, through the smaller angle. Curl the fingers of your right hand in that sense of rotation; your thumb points along $\vec{\mathbf A}\times\vec{\mathbf B}$.

Do the same for $\vec{\mathbf B}\times\vec{\mathbf A}$ — rotating $\vec{\mathbf B}$ into $\vec{\mathbf A}$ — and your thumb points the opposite way. So the vector product is not commutative; it is anticommutative, equation (1.21):

$$\vec{\mathbf A}\times\vec{\mathbf B}=-\vec{\mathbf B}\times\vec{\mathbf A}.$$

A geometric reading of the magnitude. $B\sin\phi$ is the component of $\vec{\mathbf B}$ perpendicular to $\vec{\mathbf A}$, so $\lvert\vec{\mathbf A}\times\vec{\mathbf B}\rvert$ is the magnitude of $\vec{\mathbf A}$ times the part of $\vec{\mathbf B}$ perpendicular to it — and equally the magnitude of $\vec{\mathbf B}$ times the part of $\vec{\mathbf A}$ perpendicular to it. Numerically it is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the two vectors (so half of it is the area of the triangle).

Components. The unit vectors are mutually perpendicular, and the vector product of any vector with itself is zero, so

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\imath}\times\hat{\imath}&=\hat{\jmath}\times\hat{\jmath} \\ &=\hat{k}\times\hat{k} \\ &=\vec{\mathbf 0}, \end{aligned}$$

and by equations (1.20), (1.21) and the right-hand rule — equations (1.22):

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\imath}\times\hat{\jmath}&=-\hat{\jmath}\times\hat{\imath} \\ &=\hat{k} \\ \hat{\jmath}\times\hat{k}&=-\hat{k}\times\hat{\jmath} \\ &=\hat{\imath} \\ \hat{k}\times\hat{\imath}&=-\hat{\imath}\times\hat{k} \\ &=\hat{\jmath}. \end{aligned}$$

Expanding $\vec{\mathbf A}\times\vec{\mathbf B}$ in components (equation 1.23) and collecting terms gives equation (1.24),

$$\vec{\mathbf A}\times\vec{\mathbf B}=(A_yB_z-A_zB_y)\hat{\imath}+(A_zB_x-A_xB_z)\hat{\jmath}+(A_xB_y-A_yB_x)\hat{k},$$

that is, the components of $\vec{\mathbf C}=\vec{\mathbf A}\times\vec{\mathbf B}$ are equations (1.25) — the sixth and last key equation of the week:

Key equation 1.25
$$\begin{aligned}C_x&=A_yB_z-A_zB_y\\C_y&=A_zB_x-A_xB_z\\C_z&=A_xB_y-A_yB_x\end{aligned}$$

The pattern is cyclic: $x\to y\to z\to x$. The same three numbers come out of the determinant

$$\vec{\mathbf A}\times\vec{\mathbf B}=\begin{vmatrix}\hat{\imath}&\hat{\jmath}&\hat{k}\\ A_x&A_y&A_z\\ B_x&B_y&B_z\end{vmatrix},$$

expanded along the first row with signs $+,-,+$. The middle sign is where errors happen: $-(A_xB_z-A_zB_x)=A_zB_x-A_xB_z$, which is the $C_y$ of equations (1.25). Distribute the minus sign over both terms in the bracket.

For two vectors in the $xy$-plane, $A_z=B_z=0$, and only the third component survives:

$$\vec{\mathbf A}\times\vec{\mathbf B}=(A_xB_y-A_yB_x)\hat{k},$$

pointing out of the page when the bracket is positive and into it when negative.

Right-handed axes. With $\hat{\imath}\times\hat{\jmath}=\hat{k}$ the coordinate system is called right-handed. Reverse the $z$-axis and every equation in (1.22) changes sign — a left-handed system. Physics uses right-handed systems throughout, and so does this course.

Two products answer different physical questions

Along a direction — the scalar product. It asks how much of one vector lies along the other. Draw a force acting on a cart and the cart's displacement, then project the force onto the displacement. For a constant force, the parallel part determines its work: $W=\vec{\mathbf F}\cdot\Delta\vec{\mathbf r}$, a scalar, developed in Week 05. A force perpendicular to the motion does no work at all — the $\cos90^\circ=0$ row of the table in §1.10, so the normal force does zero work on a block sliding along a fixed surface. A moving support can do work: the perpendicularity must hold for the displacement in the chosen frame.

About a point — the vector product. It measures the perpendicular effect, and keeps track of its orientation. Draw a door: the hinge, the position arrow $\vec{\mathbf r}$ from hinge to handle, and your push $\vec{\mathbf F}$. Push straight at the hinge (antiparallel to $\vec{\mathbf r}$) and the door does not turn — $\sin180^\circ=0$. Push perpendicular to the door and the turning effect is as large as that force can make it. The torque $\vec{\boldsymbol\tau}=\vec{\mathbf r}\times\vec{\mathbf F}$ points along the hinge axis, not in the direction your hand moves; that is what a vector product does with a direction. Predict before calculating. Keep $A$, $B$ fixed and rotate one vector from parallel to perpendicular to the other. What happens to the two products?

Compare the operations

The scalar product falls from $AB$ to zero; the magnitude of the vector product rises from zero to $AB$. Reversing the order leaves the scalar product unchanged and reverses the vector product. A single force can therefore do zero work along one displacement while producing a non-zero torque about some chosen point — the two products are answering different questions about the same force. Work and torque are both "force times length", yet they are not interchangeable: report work in joules and torque in newton-metres, and never add one to the other.

"Outer product" is a different mathematical operation and is not another name for the three-dimensional cross product. Choose the product by the physical question, never by the units alone.

$$C=AB\sin\phi=(6)(4)(\sin30^\circ)=12.$$

For the component route, note that $\vec{\mathbf A}$ lies along $x$, so its only non-zero component is $A_x$; and $\vec{\mathbf B}$'s angle is measured from $+x$ toward $+y$, so equations (1.5) apply:

$$\begin{aligned} A_x&=6,\quad A_y \\ &=0,\quad A_z \\ &=0 \\ B_x&=4\cos30^\circ \\ &=2\sqrt3,\quad B_y \\ &=4\sin30^\circ \\ &=2,\quad B_z \\ &=0. \end{aligned}$$

so again $\vec{\mathbf C}=12\hat{k}$.

EVALUATE. Both methods agree, and both agree with the limiting check. Which is more convenient depends on what you are given: magnitudes and an angle favour equation (1.20), components favour equations (1.25).

Key concept. The vector product of two vectors is a third vector, perpendicular to both. You can get it either from the two magnitudes, the angle and the right-hand rule, or from the components.

Worked example 1.12

Vectors on the roof (the chapter's Bridging Problem)

Problem. An air-conditioning unit is fastened to a roof that slopes at $35^\circ$ above the horizontal. Its weight is a force $\vec{\mathbf F}$ on the unit directed vertically downward. So that the unit does not crush the roof tiles, the component of its weight perpendicular to the roof must not exceed $425\ \mathrm N$. (One newton is the SI unit of force, approximately 0.2248 lbf.) (a) What is the maximum allowed weight of the unit? (b) If the fasteners fail, the unit slides $1.50\ \mathrm m$ down the roof before stopping against a ledge. How much work does the weight force do on the unit during that slide, if the unit has the weight found in (a)? The work done by a force $\vec{\mathbf F}$ on an object that undergoes displacement $\vec{\mathbf s}$ is $W=\vec{\mathbf F}\cdot\vec{\mathbf s}$.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

IDENTIFY and SET UP. Known: the slope angle $\beta=35^\circ$, the limit on one component of the weight, $F_\perp^{\max}=425\ \mathrm N$, and the slide distance $s=1.50\ \mathrm m$. Target in (a): the magnitude $F$ of the weight vector. Target in (b): the scalar $W$, for which the component of the weight along the slope is what matters.

Draw the roof and choose axes that are not horizontal and vertical: let $+x$ run down the slope and $+y$ perpendicular to the roof surface (Example 1.6(b) established that any mutually perpendicular pair will do). The weight points vertically down, so in these axes it has a component down the slope and a component into the roof.

The angle to use — the step that decides the problem. The weight is vertical and the $y$-axis is perpendicular to a surface tilted by $\beta$ from the horizontal, so the angle between the weight vector and the inward normal is $\beta$ itself, not $90^\circ-\beta$. Sketch it: as the roof flattens, $\beta\to0$ and the whole weight presses into the roof. Hence

$$F_\perp=F\cos\beta,\qquad F_\parallel=F\sin\beta.$$

Symbolic answers.

$$\begin{aligned} F^{\max}&=\frac{F_\perp^{\max}}{\cos\beta} \\ W&=\vec{\mathbf F}\cdot\vec{\mathbf s} \\ &=F_\parallel s \\ &=F^{\max}s\sin\beta \\ &=F_\perp^{\max}\,s\tan\beta. \end{aligned}$$

Limiting check. A flat roof, $\beta\to0$: then $\cos\beta\to1$, so $F^{\max}\to425\ \mathrm N$ — the whole weight bears on the tiles, as it must — and $\tan\beta\to0$, so $W\to0$, because a flat roof gives the sliding displacement no component along the weight. A vertical wall, $\beta\to90^\circ$: $F^{\max}\to\infty$ within this restricted model: the weight has no normal component, so this constraint alone no longer supplies an upper bound. Real fasteners and structural limits still apply; infinite allowable weight is not a physical prediction.

Numbers with units. The textbook reports two significant figures because the angle is given only as $35^\circ$; extra displayed intermediate digits below are for checking the arithmetic.

$$\begin{aligned} F^{\max}&=\frac{425\ \mathrm N}{\cos35^\circ} \\ &=\frac{425\ \mathrm N}{0.8192} \\ &=5.2\times10^2\ \mathrm N, \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} W&=(425\ \mathrm N)(1.50\ \mathrm m)(\tan35^\circ) \\ &=4.5\times10^2\ \mathrm N\cdot\mathrm m. \end{aligned}$$

(Equivalently: $F_\parallel=(518.8\ \mathrm N)(\sin35^\circ)=297.6\ \mathrm N$ and $W=(297.6\ \mathrm N)(1.50\ \mathrm m)=446\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}$ — the same number, as the displayed-intermediate convention promises, to the figures being reported.)

Interpret, and the two checks the textbook asks for. First: is any component larger in absolute value than the vector itself? $F_\perp=425\ \mathrm N$ and $F_\parallel=298\ \mathrm N$ against $F=519\ \mathrm N$ — no, and that is impossible in principle, since a component is a leg of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the vector (Figure 1.1). Second: the scalar product can be computed two ways. By components, with $\vec{\mathbf s}=(1.50\ \mathrm m)\hat{\imath}$ and $\vec{\mathbf F}=(297.6\hat{\imath}+425\hat{\jmath})\ \mathrm N$, equation (1.19) gives $W=(297.6)(1.50)+(425)(0)=446\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}$. By magnitudes and the angle, equation (1.16), the angle between the vertical weight and the down-slope displacement is $90^\circ-35^\circ=55^\circ$, so $W=(518.8\ \mathrm N)(1.50\ \mathrm m)\cos55^\circ=446\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}$. They agree — and notice that the perpendicular component contributed nothing to the work, which is the physical content of §1.10 and the whole of Week 05.

Ağırlık düşey olduğundan dik bileşen $F\cos\beta$, eğim boyunca bileşen $F\sin\beta$'dir — açıyı çizimden oku, ezberden değil. $F^{\max}=F_\perp^{\max}/\cos\beta=5.2\times10^2$ N; iş yalnızca eğim boyunca bileşenden gelir: $W=F_\perp^{\max}s\tan\beta=4.5\times10^2$ N·m. VP1.7.1 — the displacement that closes the loop

Problem. A fourth vector $\vec{\mathbf D}$ is added to the three displacements of Example 1.7 so that the total is zero: $\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf B}+\vec{\mathbf C}+\vec{\mathbf D}=\vec{\mathbf 0}$. Find the magnitude and direction of $\vec{\mathbf D}$.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Predict. This is the walk back from the buried keys to the centre of the field, so before calculating anything: same length as the resultant, opposite direction.

Symbolic route. Adding $-(\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf B}+\vec{\mathbf C})$ to both sides, $\vec{\mathbf D}=-\vec{\mathbf R}$, so $D_x=-R_x$ and $D_y=-R_y$ by equations (1.8) with $c=-1$.

Limiting check. If the three displacements already closed the loop, $\vec{\mathbf R}=\vec{\mathbf 0}$ and $\vec{\mathbf D}$ would be zero too.

Numbers. $R_x=-7.99\ \mathrm m$ and $R_y=9.92\ \mathrm m$, so $D_x=+7.99\ \mathrm m$, $D_y=-9.92\ \mathrm m$:

$$\begin{aligned} D&=\sqrt{(7.99)^2+(9.92)^2}\ \mathrm m \\ &=12.7\ \mathrm m \\ \theta&=\arctan\frac{-9.92}{7.99} \\ &=-51^\circ \\ &=309^\circ, \end{aligned}$$

in the fourth quadrant — and this time the calculator's answer needs no $180^\circ$ repair, because $D_x>0$. Sketch it and see why: the signs of the components put the vector down and to the right, which is where $-51^\circ$ points.

Interpret. $\vec{\mathbf D}$ has the same magnitude as $\vec{\mathbf R}$ and a direction exactly $180^\circ$ from it ($309^\circ-129^\circ=180^\circ$), as the prediction said. A set of vectors summing to zero is a closed polygon when drawn head to tail — a fact that becomes the condition for equilibrium in Week 12.

VP1.7.3 — scaling one of the vectors

Problem. For the same three vectors, (a) find the components of $\vec{\mathbf T}=\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf B}+2\vec{\mathbf C}$, and (b) its magnitude and direction.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Predict. $\vec{\mathbf C}$ points due south, so doubling it drags the resultant southward. The original $\vec{\mathbf R}$ had $R_y=+9.92\ \mathrm m$ and $C_y=-17.80\ \mathrm m$, so one extra $\vec{\mathbf C}$ should push $T_y$ negative — moving the answer from the second quadrant to the third.

Symbolic route. By equations (1.8), $2\vec{\mathbf C}$ has components $2C_x$, $2C_y$; then equations (1.10) as usual: $T_x=A_x+B_x+2C_x$, $T_y=A_y+B_y+2C_y$.

Limiting check. Setting the multiplier back to 1 must return $\vec{\mathbf R}=(-7.99,\ 9.92)\ \mathrm m$. It does.

Numbers. (a) $T_x=38.37-46.36+2(0.00)=-7.99\ \mathrm m$ — unchanged, since $\vec{\mathbf C}$ has no $x$-component — and $T_y=61.40-33.68+2(-17.80)=-7.88\ \mathrm m$.

(b)

$$\begin{aligned} T&=\sqrt{(-7.99)^2+(-7.88)^2}\ \mathrm m \\ &=11.2\ \mathrm m \\ \arctan\frac{-7.88}{-7.99}&=+44.6^\circ\ \text{(calculator)}, \end{aligned}$$

and both components are negative, which is the third quadrant, so the true direction is $\theta=180^\circ+44.6^\circ=225^\circ$. The calculator was off by $180^\circ$ again — and this time it gave a positive angle, which is how the error hides: two minus signs cancelled inside the ratio.

VP1.7.4 — finding the missing leg

Problem. A hiker makes the displacement $\vec{\mathbf A}$ of Example 1.7 ($72.4\ \mathrm m$, $32.0^\circ$ east of north). She then makes a second displacement, after which she is $38.0\ \mathrm m$ from her starting point in a direction $37.0^\circ$ west of north from it. Find the magnitude and direction of the second displacement.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Predict. She went well to the north-east and ended up much closer to home and to the west of the start, so the second leg must head back south-west, and it must be long — comparable to $\vec{\mathbf A}$ itself.

Symbolic route. With $\vec{\mathbf A}$ the first leg, $\vec{\mathbf L}$ the second and $\vec{\mathbf P}$ the final position measured from the start, $\vec{\mathbf A}+\vec{\mathbf L}=\vec{\mathbf P}$, hence $\vec{\mathbf L}=\vec{\mathbf P}-\vec{\mathbf A}$: a vector subtraction, equation (1.4), done in components.

Limiting check. If the hiker ended where she started, $\vec{\mathbf P}=\vec{\mathbf 0}$ and $\vec{\mathbf L}=-\vec{\mathbf A}$ — the walk straight back, which is VP1.7.1 in miniature.

Numbers. "$37.0^\circ$ west of north" is $37.0^\circ$ from north toward west, so the angle from $+x$ is $90.0^\circ+37.0^\circ=127.0^\circ$:

$$\begin{aligned} P_x&=(38.0\ \mathrm m)\cos127.0^\circ \\ &=-22.87\ \mathrm m \\ P_y&=(38.0\ \mathrm m)\sin127.0^\circ \\ &=30.34\ \mathrm m, \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} L_x&=-22.87-38.37 \\ &=-61.24\ \mathrm m \\ L_y&=30.34-61.40 \\ &=-31.06\ \mathrm m, \end{aligned}$$

in the third quadrant — south-west, as predicted.

Interpret. She walked $72.4+68.7=141.1\ \mathrm m$ to finish $38.0\ \mathrm m$ from home. Note the structure of the problem: displacement from the start is a vector equation, and any one of its three vectors can be the unknown. $$\begin{aligned} F_y&=\frac{26.0\ \mathrm J-(-12.0\ \mathrm N)(4.00\ \mathrm m)}{5.00\ \mathrm m} \\ &=\frac{26.0+48.0}{5.00}\ \mathrm N \\ &=14.8\ \mathrm N, \end{aligned}$$

5. Calculate step by step, with units.

Include the zero z-components. $\vec A=(4.00,7.00,0)$ and $\vec B=(5.00,-2.00,0)$.

Expand all three components in the order A × B.

$$\begin{aligned} C_x&=A_yB_z-A_zB_y\\ &=(7.00)(0)-(0)(-2.00)=0,\\ C_y&=A_zB_x-A_xB_z\\ &=(0)(5.00)-(4.00)(0)=0,\\ C_z&=A_xB_y-A_yB_x\\ &=(4.00)(-2.00)-(7.00)(5.00)\\ &=-8.00-35.0=-43.0. \end{aligned}$$

Assemble the vector and take its magnitude.

$$\vec A\times\vec B=\boxed{-43.0\hat k},$$ $$|\vec A\times\vec B|=\sqrt{0^2+0^2+(-43.0)^2}=\boxed{43.0}.$$

Use the drawing for an independent direction check. Viewed from $+z$, turning from A toward B through the smaller angle is clockwise. The right-hand rule therefore gives $-z$, into the page. The magnitude is nonnegative; it is the z-component that is negative. Finally, $AB\sin\phi=\sqrt{65}\sqrt{29}\sin(82.0565\ldots^\circ)=43$, agreeing with the component calculation.

6. Interpret and check. The magnitude is positive, the z-component negative. An independent identity checks Exercises 1.40 and 1.42: $6^2+43^2=(65)(29)=1885$.

Y&F Exercise 1.43 — three angles from scalar products

Problem. Find the angle for each pair: (a) A=(−2.00,6.00), B=(2.00,−3.00); (b) A=(3.00,5.00), B=(10.00,6.00); (c) A=(−4.00,2.00), B=(7.00,14.00).

Source: Young & Freedman, University Physics, 15th ed., Chapter 1, Exercise 1.43. Givens retained; question restated and solution written for this course. Try on paper before revealing the route.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Solution.

Open drawing at full size

Drawing for the solution. Vector plots use equal coordinate scales; schematic models are identified in the drawing.

1. Read the drawing and predict. Sketch each pair tail to tail. Pair (a) is almost opposite; (b) points broadly together; (c) may be perpendicular.

2–3. Choose the relation and rearrange symbolically. For every pair, $\cos\phi=(A_xB_x+A_yB_y)/(AB)$, with $0\le\phi\le180^\circ$.

4. Check before substituting. A negative numerator implies obtuse; zero implies 90° for nonzero inputs; positive implies acute.

5. Calculate step by step, with units.

For each pair, use $\phi=\cos^{-1}[(\vec A\cdot\vec B)/(AB)]$. The inverse cosine gives the smaller angle between the vectors, from 0° to 180°.

(a) A = (−2.00, 6.00), B = (2.00, −3.00).

$$\begin{aligned} \vec A\cdot\vec B&=(-2)(2)+(6)(-3)=-22,\\ A&=\sqrt{(-2)^2+6^2}=\sqrt{40},\\ B&=\sqrt{2^2+(-3)^2}=\sqrt{13},\\ \cos\phi&=\frac{-22}{\sqrt{40}\sqrt{13}}=-0.9647638\ldots,\\ \phi&=164.744881\ldots^\circ\approx\boxed{165^\circ}. \end{aligned}$$

The negative dot product means obtuse; the first drawing shows almost opposite arrows.

(b) A = (3.00, 5.00), B = (10.00, 6.00).

$$\begin{aligned} \vec A\cdot\vec B&=(3)(10)+(5)(6)=60,\\ A&=\sqrt{3^2+5^2}=\sqrt{34},\\ B&=\sqrt{10^2+6^2}=\sqrt{136},\\ \cos\phi&=\frac{60}{\sqrt{34}\sqrt{136}}=\frac{60}{68},\\ \phi&=28.072487\ldots^\circ\approx\boxed{28.1^\circ}. \end{aligned}$$

The positive dot product means acute, consistent with the second drawing.

(c) A = (−4.00, 2.00), B = (7.00, 14.00).

$$\begin{aligned} \vec A\cdot\vec B&=(-4)(7)+(2)(14)=-28+28=0,\\ A&=\sqrt{20},\qquad B=\sqrt{245},\\ \cos\phi&=\frac{0}{\sqrt{20}\sqrt{245}}=0,\\ \phi&=\boxed{90.0^\circ}. \end{aligned}$$

Both magnitudes are nonzero, so the zero dot product establishes perpendicularity. A supplementary check is that the slopes are −1/2 and 2: their product is −1.

6. Interpret and check. The inverse cosine returns the angle between vectors directly. Unlike arctan of a component ratio, there is no 180° quadrant correction here.

Drawing for the solution. Vector plots use equal coordinate scales; schematic models are identified in the drawing.

1. Read the drawing and predict. The smaller angle is 120°, not 60°. A → B is clockwise viewed from +z; B → A is counter-clockwise.

2–3. Choose the relation and rearrange symbolically. $|\vec A\times\vec B|=AB\sin120^\circ$; use the right-hand rule for the vector sign.

4. Check before substituting. Changing the order preserves the spanned parallelogram area and reverses its normal.

5. Calculate step by step, with units.

Find the angle between the vectors, not either angle to an axis. A is at +60.0° and B at −60.0°, so the smaller separation is

$$\phi=60.0^\circ-(-60.0^\circ)=120.0^\circ.$$

(a) Magnitude of A × B.

$$\begin{aligned} |\vec A\times\vec B|&=AB\sin\phi\\ &=(2.80\ \mathrm{cm})(1.90\ \mathrm{cm})\sin120.0^\circ\\ &=(5.32)(0.8660254\ldots)\ \mathrm{cm^2}\\ &=4.60725515\ldots\ \mathrm{cm^2}\\ &\approx\boxed{4.61\ \mathrm{cm^2}}. \end{aligned}$$

$$\boxed{\vec A\times\vec B=-4.61\hat k\ \mathrm{cm^2}}\quad\text{(into the page)}.$$

(b) Reverse the order. The parallelogram area remains the same, but the direction reverses:

$$\boxed{\vec B\times\vec A=+4.61\hat k\ \mathrm{cm^2}}\quad\text{(out of the page)}.$$

Both magnitudes are 4.61 cm². Check the first sign using the components from Exercise 1.33:

$$\begin{aligned} C_z&=A_xB_y-A_yB_x\\ &=(1.400)(-1.64544827\ldots)\\ &\quad-(2.42487113\ldots)(0.950)\\ &=-4.60725515\ldots\ \mathrm{cm^2}. \end{aligned}$$

The component check confirms both the sign and the area interpretation.

Y&F Exercise 1.47 — recover a vector from its cross product

Problem. $\vec A$ is 8.0 m along −x. The product A×B is $+16.0\hat k\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and B has no x-component. Find B.

Solution.

Draw and predict. A points left. For the right-hand rule to give +z, B must point downward in the xy-plane. First verify that no z-component can hide in the data.

Choose and rearrange. Write $\vec A=(-a,0,0)$ and $\vec B=(0,B_y,B_z)$. Then $\vec A\times\vec B=(0,aB_z,-aB_y)$.

Y&F Exercise 1.48 — equal magnitudes of two different products

Problem. Two nonzero vectors make angle θ. (a) At 30.0°, which product has larger magnitude, the dot or cross product? (b) At which angles between 0° and 180° are their magnitudes equal?

Source: Young & Freedman, University Physics, 15th ed., Chapter 1, Exercise 1.48. Givens retained; question restated and solution written for this course. Try on paper before revealing the route.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Solution.

Draw and predict. Sketch cosine and sine. Dot-product magnitude uses absolute cosine; overlooking the absolute value loses an answer in quadrant II.

Choose and rearrange. $|\vec A\cdot\vec B|=AB|\cos\theta|$, $|\vec A\times\vec B|=AB\sin\theta$. Cancel the nonzero AB to compare.

Check before substituting. At 0° the dot magnitude is AB and cross is zero; at 90° the reverse holds; at 180° dot magnitude returns to AB.

Calculate with units.

$$\theta=30^\circ:\quad|\cos\theta|=\sqrt3/2>1/2=\sin\theta,$$ $$\begin{aligned} |\cos\theta|&=\sin\theta\Rightarrow\cos^2\theta \\ &=\sin^2\theta, \end{aligned}$$ $$\theta=45^\circ\quad\text{or}\quad135^\circ.$$

Interpret. The dot magnitude is larger at 30°. At 135° the dot product is negative, but its magnitude equals the cross-product magnitude. If either input vector is zero, both products vanish and an angle need not be defined.

The opening question: (iii) 18 km. Take $+x$ east and $+y$ north. The velocity has magnitude $v=15\ \mathrm{km/h}$ at $\theta=37^\circ$ from $+x$, so by equations (1.5) its northward component is $v_y=v\sin\theta=(15\ \mathrm{km/h})(\sin37^\circ)=9.0\ \mathrm{km/h}$. In 2.0 h the storm therefore moves $(9.0\ \mathrm{km/h})(2.0\ \mathrm h)=18\ \mathrm{km}$ north. The tempting wrong answer is 30 km — that is the total distance travelled, $(15)(2.0)$, not its northward part. Only the component along a direction counts for displacement along that direction, which is §1.8 in one line.

Entry: "A distance of 5 m" identifies a quantity, a number and a unit; "5" alone identifies nothing (§1.3).

Exit: For magnitude $A$ at angle $\theta$ from $+x$, $A_x=A\cos\theta$ and $A_y=A\sin\theta$, and $A=\sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2}$. Walking 3 m out and 3 m back gives a path length of 6 m and a displacement of zero: displacement compares endpoints (§1.7).
