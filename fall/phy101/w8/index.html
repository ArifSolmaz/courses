Learning Objectives

By the end of this session you will be able to:

  1. Define gravitational ($U_g=mgy$) and elastic ($U_s=\tfrac12kx^2$) potential energy relative to a stated zero
  2. Distinguish conservative from non-conservative forces and explain why $U$ is defined only for the former
  3. Apply conservation of mechanical energy, $K_i+U_i=K_f+U_f$, with a consistent reference height
  4. Include dissipative work with $K_i+U_i+W_{\rm other}=K_f+U_f$ without counting gravity twice
  5. Use energy methods for springs, ramps and vertical launches, checking a limiting case before substituting
  6. Build intuition through interactive energy diagrams and simulations

After the midterm: put gravitational work into an energy account

Recall $W_{\rm net}=K_f-K_i$. For a vertical displacement with upward positive, gravity does $$W_g=-mg(y_f-y_i)=mgy_i-mgy_f.$$ Define gravitational potential energy $U_g=mgy$ relative to one chosen zero height. Then $W_g=U_{g,i}-U_{g,f}$. Substitute into work–energy and add $U_{g,f}$ to both sides: $$K_i+U_{g,i}=K_f+U_{g,f}.$$ We have reorganized the same physics. Do not count gravitational work a second time after including $U_g$.

A spring stores $U_s=\tfrac12kx^2$, with $x$ measured from its natural length. This is the triangular area under the applied-force-versus-extension graph. If other forces such as friction do work, $$\boxed{\;K_i+U_i+W_{\rm other}=K_f+U_f\;}$$ Friction usually makes $W_{\rm other}<0$ for the chosen moving system. Mechanical energy decreases while energy transfers to thermal/internal forms.

TÜRKÇE

Önce sıfır yüksekliği seç. $mgh$ yalnızca seçtiğin referansa göre anlamlıdır; iki durum arasında aynı referansı koru.

Potential energy describes a configuration, not a push

Choose the system and reference before writing an energy account. Gravitational potential energy belongs to the object–Earth configuration; elastic potential energy belongs to the deformed spring. A change of reference adds the same constant to every value of $U$ and changes no prediction.

Geometry. Sketch the straight line $U_g=mgy$ and the bowl $U_s=kx^2/2$. Their heights represent energy, not force. Along one coordinate, a conservative force is the negative slope of the potential-energy curve:

$$F_x=-\frac{dU}{dx}.$$

A positive slope means a force toward decreasing $x$; a horizontal tangent means zero force, not necessarily zero velocity. For the spring, the slope is $kx$, so the restoring force is $-kx$. This one-dimensional slope is sufficient here; vector-calculus operators are not required.

Predict. Add $20\,\mathrm J$ to every value on a potential-energy graph. Does the motion change? Compare doubling a spring compression with doubling a near-Earth lifting height.

Read differences and shape

Neither potential differences nor slopes change when a constant is added, so the motion is unchanged. Spring energy increases fourfold with doubled compression; gravitational potential-energy increase doubles with doubled height for fixed mass and uniform $g$.

Transfer. A spring bumper trades motion for deformation; a raised process vessel stores energy through its height. A position log alone does not reveal dissipated energy: compare initial and final $K+U$ and account for work by other forces. Never include both $\Delta U_g$ and gravity's work as separate transfers in the same account.

Potansiyel enerjinin yüksekliği kuvvet değildir; negatif eğimi kuvveti verir. Referans değişir, enerji farkı değişmez.

Worked example 8.2

Example 1 -- Spring launcher

A spring ($k=500\,\mathrm{N/m}$) is compressed by $0.2\,\mathrm m$ and launches a $0.1\,\mathrm{kg}$ ball vertically. How high does the ball go?

Paper solution / Kâğıt üzerinde çözüm. Take the ball's initially compressed position as height zero, neglect spring mass and drag, and let the ball start and finish at rest. The spring initially stores $U_s=\tfrac12(500)(0.20)^2=10\,\mathrm J$. At the highest point all of this is gravitational energy: $$10=(0.10)(9.81)h=0.981h.$$ Divide both sides by $0.981\,\mathrm N$: $h=10/0.981=10.2\,\mathrm m$ above the initial compressed position.

The reference point matters. If the ball separates when the vertical spring reaches its natural length, it has already risen $0.20\,\mathrm m$. Its kinetic energy at separation is $10-mg(0.20)=9.80\,\mathrm J$, giving $v=\sqrt{2(9.80)/0.10}=14.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. It rises another $10.2-0.20=9.99\,\mathrm m$ above separation. A calculation that uses all $10\,\mathrm J$ as launch kinetic energy ignores the small gravitational gain during spring expansion.

TÜRKÇE

“Ne kadar yükselir?” sorusunda yüksekliğin nereden ölçüldüğünü mutlaka yaz. Yay açılırken de top yukarı çıkar ve yerçekimi potansiyel enerjisi artar.

ŞEKİL 8.2

Yay enerjisindeki ½ neden var? Yayı tutmak için gereken kuvvet uzamayla doğrusal artar; depolanan enerji kesikli dikdörtgenin değil bir üçgenin alanıdır: kx² değil ½kx². Uzama iki katına çıkınca enerji dört katına çıkar.

Worked example 8.3

Example 2 -- Block on incline with friction

A $4\,\mathrm{kg}$ block starts at the top of a $2\,\mathrm m$ high incline (angle $25^\circ$) with initial speed $1\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The kinetic friction coefficient is 0.15. What is the speed at the bottom?

Work it by stages / Aşama aşama çözüm. Use the bottom as $U_g=0$. The friction force acts along the ramp, so its work uses ramp length, not vertical height:

$$\begin{aligned}s&=\frac{h}{\sin25^\circ}=\frac{2.0}{\sin25^\circ}=4.73\,\mathrm m,\\N&=mg\cos25^\circ=35.6\,\mathrm N,\qquad f_k=\mu_kN=5.33\,\mathrm N,\\W_f&=-f_ks=-(5.33)(4.73)=-25.2\,\mathrm J.\end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned}\frac12mv_f^2&=\frac12mv_i^2+mgh+W_f\\&=2.0+78.5-25.2=55.2\,\mathrm J,\\v_f^2&=\frac{2(55.2\,\mathrm J)}{4.0\,\mathrm{kg}}=27.6\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2},\\v_f&=\boxed{5.26\,\mathrm{m/s}}.\end{aligned}$$

Friction lowers the result below the frictionless $\sqrt{1^2+2(9.81)(2)}=6.34\,\mathrm{m/s}$. TR: Önce normal kuvvet, sonra sürtünme kuvveti, sonra sürtünme işi bulunur. Yatay olmayan yolda $N=mg$ yazmak doğru değildir.

Worked example 8.4

Set a pendulum release angle

Problem. A sensor pod on a light $0.800\,\mathrm m$ tether must reach $1.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at its lowest point when released from rest. Find the release angle from vertical, neglecting drag and pivot friction. Would doubling the pod mass change the angle?

Picture and principle. Draw the pod at angle $\theta$ and at the bottom. The height difference is $h=L(1-\cos\theta)$. Conservation $mgh=mv^2/2$ gives $\theta=\cos^{-1}[1-v^2/(2gL)]$; mass cancels.

Limiting check. As target speed tends to zero, the release angle tends to zero. The cosine argument must remain between $-1$ and $1$.

Numbers and units. $$\theta=\cos^{-1}\!\left[1-\frac{(1.20\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2(9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(0.800\,\mathrm m)}\right]=24.7^\circ.$$

Interpret. Doubling mass leaves the angle unchanged in this model. Both potential and kinetic energy increase by the same factor.

TÜRKÇE

Yükseklik farkı ip uzunluğu değil, $L(1-\cos\theta)$ değeridir. Enerji denkleminde kütle sadeleşir.

Worked example 8.5

Choose a spring with enough stroke

Problem. A horizontal spring launcher must give a $0.150\,\mathrm{kg}$ slider a speed of $1.80\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at the spring’s natural length. Compare springs of $120$ and $240\,\mathrm{N/m}$ if available compression is at most $5.00\,\mathrm{cm}$. Treat the spring constants as three-significant-figure specifications and neglect losses.

Picture and principle. Draw the initially compressed spring and the slider at natural length. From $kx^2/2=mv^2/2$, required compression is $x=v\sqrt{m/k}$. Convert the stroke limit to metres before comparing.

Limiting check. A stiffer spring needs less compression for the same energy; doubling stiffness divides the required compression by $\sqrt2$.

Numbers and units. $$x_{120}=(1.80)\sqrt{0.150/120}\,\mathrm m=6.36\,\mathrm{cm},\quad x_{240}=4.50\,\mathrm{cm}.$$

Interpret. Only the stiffer spring fits within the available stroke. The problem specifies energy transfer, not a constant spring force.

TÜRKÇE

Yay kuvveti sıkışmayla değişir; enerji $kx^2/2$ olur. Daha sert yay aynı enerjiyi daha kısa mesafede depolar.

Worked example 8.6

Size a vertical spring bumper

Problem. A $0.400\,\mathrm{kg}$ test mass is released $0.100\,\mathrm m$ above an uncompressed vertical spring. Compression must not exceed $0.0600\,\mathrm m$. Find the minimum spring stiffness, neglecting losses, and explain why the fall height alone is not enough.

Picture and principle. Draw release, first contact, and maximum compression. The mass falls a total $h+x$, not just $h$. At maximum compression its speed is zero, so $mg(h+x)=kx^2/2$ and $k_{\min}=2mg(h+x_{\max})/x_{\max}^2$.

Limiting check. Even when released just touching the spring ($h=0$), gravity still does work during compression; the required stiffness is not zero.

Numbers and units. $$k_{\min}=\frac{2(0.400\,\mathrm{kg})(9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(0.100+0.0600)\,\mathrm m}{(0.0600\,\mathrm m)^2}=349\,\mathrm{N/m}.$$

Interpret. This is a dynamic compression limit. Equating $kx=mg$ would find a static equilibrium instead.

TÜRKÇE

Kütle yayı sıkıştırırken de aşağı iner. En büyük sıkışma, statik denge uzaması değildir.

Worked example 8.7

Upgrade a suspension without increasing its spring energy

Problem. A spring extends $15.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ under a gently added $0.300\,\mathrm{kg}$ sensor. An upgrade doubles the total hanging mass to $0.600\,\mathrm{kg}$. Choose a replacement spring so its static stored energy stays equal to the original value, and find its new static extension. Assume Hooke’s law and negligible spring mass.

Picture and principle. Draw each sensor at static equilibrium: $kx=mg$. Substituting $x=mg/k$ into $U=kx^2/2$ gives $U=m^2g^2/(2k)$. Equal energy therefore requires $k_2=k_1(m_2/m_1)^2$. First calibrate $k_1=m_1g/x_1$, then use $x_2=m_2g/k_2$.

Limiting check. If the mass is unchanged, both stiffness and extension stay unchanged. Doubling mass requires four times the stiffness to preserve this particular energy target.

Numbers and units.

$$k_1=\frac{(0.300\,\mathrm{kg})(9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2})}{0.0150\,\mathrm m}=196\,\mathrm{N/m},\quad k_2=4(196.2)\,\mathrm{N/m}=785\,\mathrm{N/m}.$$

The new extension is $x_2=(0.600)(9.81)/784.8\,\mathrm m=7.50\,\mathrm{mm}$. Both spring energies are $0.0221\,\mathrm J$.

Interpret. Doubling stiffness would preserve the old extension, but would double the energy. “Same extension” and “same stored energy” are different design requirements.

TÜRKÇE

Statik dengede $x=mg/k$ yazıp enerji denklemine yerleştir. Aynı uzamayı korumak ile aynı enerjiyi korumak farklı yay seçimleri gerektirir.

Worked example 8.8

Separate a round trip’s energy loss from height

Problem. A $0.800\,\mathrm{kg}$ test cart returns to the same point and height after one circuit. Its speed falls from $1.60$ to $1.10\,\mathrm{m/s}$. No motor supplies energy. Find the mechanical energy dissipated and test the claim that gravity caused a net energy loss over the circuit.

Picture and principle. Draw the closed route with identical start and end heights. Since $\Delta U_g=0$, dissipated energy is $E_{\rm loss}=K_i-K_f=m(v_i^2-v_f^2)/2$. Gravity’s net work is $-\Delta U_g=0$.

Limiting check. If the return speed equals the launch speed, this measurement implies zero net mechanical loss, whatever the hills along the route.

Numbers and units. $$E_{\rm loss}=\tfrac12(0.800\,\mathrm{kg})[(1.60)^2-(1.10)^2]\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}=0.540\,\mathrm J.$$

Interpret. Gravity exchanges kinetic and potential energy along the route but cannot explain this net loss. Resistance can.

Model responses / Örnek yanıtlar — open after trying

Entry: Net work equals the change in kinetic energy: $W_{\rm net}=K_f-K_i$. An equation relates physical quantities generally; substitution inserts the particular givens with units.

Exit: Choose the ramp bottom as zero height. Then $U_f=0$, and $K_i+mgh-f_ks=K_f$. Gravity is already represented by $mgh$, so adding another $+mgh$ as gravitational work would count the same transfer twice. The full numerical ramp solution appears above.

Visual prediction: For a vertical launch from zero height with no drag, initially $K_i=\tfrac12mv_0^2$ and $U_i=0$. At the peak $K=0$ and $U=\tfrac12mv_0^2$. On returning to launch height, $U=0$ and kinetic energy returns to its initial value. Their sum remains constant.
