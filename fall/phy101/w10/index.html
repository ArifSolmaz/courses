10.1Before you startBaşlamadan önce

🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  1. Define and relate angular position, velocity, and acceleration for rigid-body rotation
  2. Apply the rotational kinematic equations (constant angular acceleration) to solve problems
  3. Calculate the moment of inertia for simple shapes and composite bodies
  4. Use the parallel-axis theorem to find moments of inertia about arbitrary axes
  5. Analyze rolling motion and pulley-rope systems using rotational dynamics
  6. Compare rotational and translational kinetic energy for different geometries

Joining this lessonDerse buradan başlayanlar

Start from familiar motion and energy equations; no prior Python is required. For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis:

  • $\tau_{\rm net}=I\alpha$: net torque changes angular speed. $I$ measures how mass is distributed about the chosen axis; a solid disk has $I=\tfrac12MR^2$.
  • With constant $\alpha$, $\omega=\omega_0+\alpha t$ and $\theta=\omega_0t+\tfrac12\alpha t^2$. Convert RPM using $\omega=\mathrm{RPM}\times2\pi/60$.
  • Rotational energy is $K_{\rm rot}=\tfrac12I\omega^2$. Rolling without slipping also requires $v=R\omega$ and includes translational energy $\tfrac12Mv^2$.

Quick entry check: For $I=0.20\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ and constant net torque $0.60\,\mathrm{N\,m}$, $\alpha=0.60/0.20=3.0\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$. Starting from rest, two seconds gives $\omega=6.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. This result assumes the quoted torque is the net torque after any resistance.

The same physics, three application questions:

Perspective Use the same torque–inertia model
Mechatronics Engineering Compare wheel shapes or motor torques to reach the required speed in a given time.
Computer Engineering Compare an angular-speed trace or model prediction with the expected slope $\alpha=\tau_{\rm net}/I$.
Chemical Engineering Estimate the torque a motor must supply to bring a stirrer or centrifuge rotor to its working speed in a given time.

Before the equations: rotation uses familiar algebraDönmede tanıdık cebir

No programming is needed for the required calculations. Match the familiar linear quantity to its rotational partner:

Linear motion Rotation Meaning
$v=v_0+at$ $\omega=\omega_0+\alpha t$ Rate changes steadily
$F=ma$ $\tau=I\alpha$ Net action causes acceleration
$K=\tfrac12mv^2$ $K_{\rm rot}=\tfrac12I\omega^2$ Energy of motion

Unit bridge: $120\,\mathrm{RPM}$ means 120 revolutions per minute, so $120\times(2\pi)/60=4\pi=12.6\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Convert before calculating energy. Radians have no physical dimension, but writing rad helps identify an angle.

Rolling bridge: Without slipping, $v=R\omega$, so $\omega=v/R$. Substitute this into BOTH energy terms: $$Mgh=\tfrac12Mv^2+\tfrac12I(v/R)^2 =\tfrac12\left(M+I/R^2\right)v^2.$$ Multiply by 2, divide by $M+I/R^2$, then take the square root. If $I=\beta MR^2$, the result is $v=\sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+\beta}}$. Rotation uses some of the energy; the speed is smaller than the sliding value $\sqrt{2gh}$.

Pulley bridge: Translating masses obey $\sum F=ma$; the pulley obeys $\sum\tau=I\alpha$. The connection is $a=R\alpha$. A massive accelerating pulley generally needs unequal tensions to produce torque.

Pause: Doubling $R$ at fixed mass quadruples $I$ for the same shape. But in ideal rolling from the same height, $I/(MR^2)=\beta$ stays the same, so the final speed does not change.

How this week is workedBu hafta nasıl çalışılır

Every problem, worked example and experiment in this course follows the same six steps. Step 4 is the one added in September 2026, and it is the one that separates a physicist from a calculator operator.

draw → choose a law → rearrange to the symbolic answer → check a limiting case → substitute with units → interpret

  1. Symbolic answer first. Isolate the unknown in symbols, before any number appears.
  2. One limiting case. Push a parameter to a value whose answer you already know — a mass to zero, an angle to $90^\circ$, a friction coefficient to zero — and confirm the formula behaves sensibly. If it does not, the algebra is wrong and you have just caught it for free, before wasting arithmetic on it.
  3. Then numbers, with units carried through every line.

Why bother: the symbolic route is less arithmetic, not more, and it is where the marks are. A correct symbolic answer with a slip in the final multiplication is nearly full credit; a correct number with no derivation is not.

10.2Concepts, demonstrations and worked examplesKonular, gösterimler ve çözümlü örnekler

Before you move a slider: Predict which has larger inertia: ring or disk at equal mass and radius. Explain why rod comparisons also require its length and axis.

Rotation begins with angle, not torque

A rigid body keeps its shape. Every point shares the same angular displacement $\Delta\theta$, angular velocity $\omega$, and angular acceleration $\alpha$, although points farther from the axis move farther.

One revolution is $2\pi$ radians. Convert 120 RPM: $$120\frac{\mathrm{rev}}{\mathrm{min}}\times\frac{2\pi\,\mathrm{rad}}{1\,\mathrm{rev}}\times\frac{1\,\mathrm{min}}{60\,\mathrm s} =4\pi=12.6\,\mathrm{rad/s}.$$ For a point 0.20 m from the axis, $v=r\omega=0.20(12.6)=2.51$ m/s. A point at half that radius has half the tangential speed but the same $\omega$.

Today's scope: describe rotation and calculate moment of inertia. Next week we use torque to explain how rotation changes. TR: Bu hafta dönmenin kinematiği ve kütlenin eksene göre dağılımını öğreniyoruz; torkla ivmelendirme sonraki haftada.

From Translation to Rotation — The Big Analogy

Everything you learned about linear motion has a rotational twin:

Linear Quantity Symbol Rotational Quantity Symbol
Position $x$ Angular position $\theta$
Velocity $v$ Angular velocity $\omega$
Acceleration $a$ Angular acceleration $\alpha$
Mass $m$ Moment of inertia $I$
Force $F$ Torque $\tau$
Momentum $p = mv$ Angular momentum $L = I\omega$
Kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$ Rotational KE $\frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$
Newton's 2nd law $F = ma$ Rotational 2nd law $\tau = I\alpha$

Think of it this way: Mass tells you how hard it is to change linear motion. Moment of inertia tells you how hard it is to change rotational motion. But unlike mass, $I$ depends on where the mass is relative to the axis.

Angular Kinematics

Definitions

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis:

$$\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}, \quad \alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}$$

Constant Angular Acceleration Equations

# Equation Missing variable
1 $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$ $\theta$
2 $\theta = \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\alpha t^2$ $\omega$ (final)
3 $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta$ $t$
4 $\theta = \frac{1}{2}(\omega_0 + \omega)t$ $\alpha$

These are identical in structure to the linear equations — just replace $x \to \theta$, $v \to \omega$, $a \to \alpha$.

Connection Between Linear and Angular

For a point at distance $r$ from the rotation axis:

$$s = r\theta, \quad v_{\text{tan}} = r\omega, \quad a_{\text{tan}} = r\alpha, \quad a_{\text{cent}} = \omega^2 r$$

Use a unit conversion before the kinematics. At $120\,\mathrm{rev/min}$:

$$\omega_0=120\,\frac{\mathrm{rev}}{\mathrm{min}}\frac{2\pi\,\mathrm{rad}}{1\,\mathrm{rev}}\frac{1\,\mathrm{min}}{60\,\mathrm s}=4\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s}.$$

If it then brakes uniformly to rest in $4.0\,\mathrm s$, $$\alpha=\frac{\omega_f-\omega_0}{t}=\frac{0-4\pi}{4}=-\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s^2},\qquad\Delta\theta=\frac{4\pi+0}{2}(4)=8\pi\,\mathrm{rad}=4\,\mathrm{rev}.$$

Here $\theta$ in the table means angular displacement from the initial orientation; otherwise write $\theta-\theta_0$. TR: Negatif ivme burada frenlemeyi gösterir. Süre boyunca hız sıfıra yaklaşırken dönme açısı yine pozitif birikir.

Moment of Inertia

Definition

For a system of particles: $$I = \sum_i m_i r_i^2$$

For a continuous body: $$I = \int r^2 \, dm$$

where $r$ is the perpendicular distance from the rotation axis.

Standard Moments of Inertia

Shape Axis $I$
Thin ring (mass $M$, radius $R$) Through center, perpendicular $MR^2$
Solid disk / cylinder Symmetry axis through center, perpendicular to the circular face $\frac{1}{2}MR^2$
Solid sphere Through center $\frac{2}{5}MR^2$
Thin-walled hollow sphere Through center $\frac{2}{3}MR^2$
Thin rod (length $L$) Through center, perpendicular $\frac{1}{12}ML^2$
Thin rod (length $L$) Through end, perpendicular $\frac{1}{3}ML^2$

Parallel-Axis Theorem

$$I = I_{\text{cm}} + Md^2$$

where $d$ is the distance between the center-of-mass axis and the new parallel axis.

Physical insight: The further the mass is from the axis, the larger $I$ is. A ring has ALL its mass at distance $R$, so $I_{\text{ring}} = MR^2$. A disk spreads mass from 0 to $R$, so $I_{\text{disk}} = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$ — only half as much!

Compare equal mass and size numerically. Let $M=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $R=0.50\,\mathrm m$, so $MR^2=0.50\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$:

Shape, center axis Substitution $I\ (\mathrm{kg\,m^2})$
Thin ring $1(0.50)$ 0.50
Solid disk $\frac12(0.50)$ 0.25
Solid sphere $\frac25(0.50)$ 0.20

Doubling every radial distance multiplies $r^2$ by four; doubling mass only doubles $I$. TR: Kütle eşitken eylemsizlik momenti farklı olabilir. Eksenin yeri ve kütlenin o eksene uzaklığı mutlaka belirtilmelidir. “Hollow sphere” satırı ince küresel kabuk modelidir.

Worked example 10.1

moving the same mass changes inertia

Two small masses, each 0.50 kg, are mounted on a light rod, each 0.20 m from its central rotation axis. Treat them as particles. $$I=\sum mr^2=0.50(0.20)^2+0.50(0.20)^2=0.0400\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}.$$ Move both to 0.40 m without changing their masses: $$I_{\rm new}=2[0.50(0.40)^2]=0.160\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}=4I.$$ The distance from the axis is squared, so doubling it quadruples the inertia. The total mass is still 1.00 kg.

Check: $\mathrm{kg}\times\mathrm{m^2}$ is the unit of inertia. The $r$ in the formula is distance from the specified rotation axis, not distance from an arbitrary end of the rod. TR: Eksenin yerini çizmeden eylemsizlik momenti hesaplama.

Worked example 10.2

🔧 Worked Example 1: Angular Kinematics

Problem: A grinding wheel starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at $\alpha = 3.0\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$ for 8 seconds, then coasts at constant angular velocity for 10 seconds, then decelerates uniformly to rest in 20 seconds. Find: (a) The maximum angular velocity (b) The angular deceleration in phase 3 (c) The total angle turned through

Paper solution: one row per phase. Positive means the original direction of rotation. In phase 1, $\omega=0+3(8)=24\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $\theta_1=\tfrac12(3)(8^2)=96\,\mathrm{rad}$. In phase 2, $\alpha=0$, so $\theta_2=\omega t=24(10)=240\,\mathrm{rad}$.

For phase 3, solve $\omega_f=\omega_i+\alpha_3t$: $0=24+20\alpha_3$. Subtract 24 and divide by 20: $\alpha_3=-1.20\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$. Since acceleration is constant, the average angular speed is $(24+0)/2=12\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $\theta_3=12(20)=240\,\mathrm{rad}$.

The total angle is $96+240+240=576\,\mathrm{rad}$, or $576/(2\pi)=91.7$ revolutions. The negative acceleration reduces positive speed; the angle during braking is still positive.

Organize the three phases numerically. Keep one unit per column:

Phase Duration $\mathrm{(s)}$ $\omega_i\ \mathrm{(rad/s)}$ $\alpha\ \mathrm{(rad/s^2)}$ $\omega_f\ \mathrm{(rad/s)}$ $\Delta\theta\ \mathrm{(rad)}$
Speed up 8 0 3.0 24 96
Coast 10 24 0 24 240
Brake 20 24 −1.2 0 240

$$\alpha_3=\frac{\omega_f-\omega_i}{t_3}=\frac{0-24}{20}=-1.20\,\mathrm{rad/s^2},\qquad\theta_\mathrm{total}=96+240+240=576\,\mathrm{rad}.$$

Worked example 10.3

Find a rotation rate from the required rim acceleration

Problem. A disk-shaped space station has diameter $175\,\mathrm{m}$ and rotates uniformly about its central axis, perpendicular to the disk. Find the rotation rate in revolutions per minute that gives points on its rim a centripetal acceleration of $g/2$. Use $g=9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Picture and principle. View the station along its rotation axis. A rim point traces a circle of radius $r$, half the diameter. Its speed can remain constant while its velocity direction changes. That change requires an inward acceleration:

$$a_c=\frac{v^2}{r}=\omega^2r,$$

where $v=\omega r$ and $\omega$ is angular speed in radians per second. The required radius and acceleration are

$$r=\frac{175}{2}\,\mathrm{m}=87.5\,\mathrm{m},\qquad a_c=\frac{9.80}{2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}=4.90\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

First isolate the angular speed symbolically by dividing by radius and taking the positive square root:

$$\omega^2=\frac{a_c}{r}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\omega=\sqrt{\frac{a_c}{r}}=\sqrt{\frac{4.90\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}{87.5\,\mathrm{m}}}=0.237\ldots\,\mathrm{rad/s}.$$

One revolution corresponds to $2\pi$ radians. Therefore,

$$n=\omega\left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{rev}}{2\pi\,\mathrm{rad}}\right)\left(\frac{60\,\mathrm{s}}{1\,\mathrm{min}}\right)=\boxed{2.26\,\mathrm{rev/min}}.$$

Meaning and check. This is about one revolution every $26.6\,\mathrm{s}$. Substituting the unrounded angular speed into $\omega^2r$ returns $4.90\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Doubling the required acceleration would increase angular speed by $\sqrt2$, not by two, because acceleration depends on the square of angular speed. The acceleration vector points toward the axis at every instant.

The radius stays fixed during this comparison; changing it would change the required rotation rate.

Convert revolutions before calculating angular braking

Problem. A grinding wheel of mass $1.15\,\mathrm{kg}$ and diameter $22.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ spins counterclockwise at $20.0\,\mathrm{rev/s}$. When switched off, it slows uniformly to rest in $80.0\,\mathrm{s}$. Find its signed angular acceleration and the number of revolutions completed while stopping. Take counterclockwise as positive.

Reasoning and algebra. A full revolution is $2\pi$ radians. Convert the initial angular speed before using the radian-based acceleration equation:

$$ \omega_i=(20.0\,\mathrm{rev/s}) \frac{2\pi\,\mathrm{rad}}{1\,\mathrm{rev}} =40\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s}. $$

Because the acceleration is constant, the change in angular speed is divided by the elapsed time:

Key equation 10.1
$$ \begin{aligned} \alpha&=\frac{\omega_f-\omega_i}{t} =\frac{0-40\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s}}{80.0\,\mathrm{s}} =-\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}\\ &=-1.57\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}. \end{aligned} $$

For the angle, use average angular speed. Uniform slowing makes that average the arithmetic mean of the endpoint speeds:

Key equation 10.2
$$ \begin{aligned} \Delta\theta&=\frac{\omega_i+\omega_f}{2}t =\frac{40\pi+0}{2}(80.0)=1600\pi\,\mathrm{rad},\\ N&=\frac{\Delta\theta}{2\pi\,\mathrm{rad/rev}} =800\text{ revolutions}. \end{aligned} $$

Check. The average rotation rate is $10.0\,\mathrm{rev/s}$, so $10.0\times80.0=800$. The acceleration is clockwise, opposite the still-counterclockwise rotation. The wheel's mass and diameter are unnecessary for this kinematics calculation; torque would require additional inertia information.

açıklama. $20.0$ değerini doğrudan radyan/saniye sanmıyoruz; önce $2\pi$ ile çarpıyoruz. Negatif açısal ivme, bu örnekte dönmenin yavaşladığını gösterir. Tekerlek duruncaya kadar dönme yönü değişmez. Tur sayısını bulmak için toplam radyanı yeniden $2\pi$'ye böleriz.

Use rim speed to find angular acceleration

Problem. A $3.45\,\mathrm{kg}$ centrifuge starts from rest and accelerates uniformly for $100\,\mathrm{s}$. At full speed, a point $8.00\,\mathrm{cm}$ from its axis moves at $150\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find the angular acceleration and the number of revolutions completed during the acceleration.

Reasoning and algebra. The given $150\,\mathrm{m/s}$ is a linear speed of one point, not the rotation rate. Convert the radius to metres and apply $v=r\omega$:

$$ \begin{aligned} r&=8.00\,\mathrm{cm}=0.0800\,\mathrm{m},\\ \omega_f&=\frac{v_f}{r} =\frac{150\,\mathrm{m/s}}{0.0800\,\mathrm{m}} =1875\,\mathrm{rad/s}. \end{aligned} $$

Since $\omega_i=0$, constant angular acceleration gives

Key equation 10.3
$$ \alpha=\frac{\omega_f-\omega_i}{t} =\frac{1875\,\mathrm{rad/s}}{100\,\mathrm{s}} =18.8\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}. $$

The angular displacement follows from the average angular speed:

Key equation 10.4
$$ \begin{aligned} \Delta\theta&=\tfrac12(\omega_i+\omega_f)t =\tfrac12(1875)(100)=93750\,\mathrm{rad},\\ N&=\frac{93750}{2\pi}=14900\,\mathrm{rev} \approx1.49\times10^4\text{ revolutions}. \end{aligned} $$

Interpretation and check. The final rate is about $298\,\mathrm{rev/s}$, while the average rate during the uniform increase is half that. Multiplying this average by $100\,\mathrm{s}$ reproduces the turn count. The large result is consistent with the unusually high stated rim speed; no extra factor of $100$ should survive the centimetre-to-metre conversion. The mass does not enter these kinematic relations.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Önce doğrusal hızdan açısal hıza geçiyoruz: aynı $v$ için yarıçap küçüldükçe $\omega$ büyür. $8\,\mathrm{cm}$ yerine $8\,\mathrm{m}$ yazmak sonucu yüz kat değiştirir. Başlangıçta durgun olduğu için ortalama açısal hız, son açısal hızın yarısıdır; toplam turu son hızla çarparak bulamayız.

The second revolution takes less time

Problem. A $1.25\,\mathrm{kg}$ ball begins rolling from rest with constant angular acceleration. Its first complete revolution takes $3.60\,\mathrm{s}$. Find the time required for its next complete revolution. Measure all elapsed times from the instant the ball is released.

Reasoning and algebra. Let $t_1$ be the time at which one revolution is complete and $t_2$ the time at which two revolutions are complete. The requested interval is $t_2-t_1$, not $t_2$. Starting from rest gives

$$ \theta=\tfrac12\alpha t^2. $$

At the first milestone, $\theta_1=2\pi\,\mathrm{rad}$. Rearrange to identify the constant acceleration:

$$ \alpha=\frac{2\theta_1}{t_1^2} =\frac{4\pi}{(3.60)^2}\,\mathrm{rad/s^2} =0.970\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}. $$

After two revolutions the total angle is $4\pi\,\mathrm{rad}$. Dividing the two displacement equations avoids unnecessary intermediate rounding:

$$ \frac{\theta_2}{\theta_1} =\frac{t_2^2}{t_1^2}=2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_2=\sqrt{2}\,t_1. $$

Therefore

$$ \begin{aligned} \Delta t_{\text{second}}&=t_2-t_1 =(\sqrt{2}-1)(3.60\,\mathrm{s})\\ &=\boxed{1.49\,\mathrm{s}}. \end{aligned} $$

Interpretation and check. Two revolutions have finished at $t_2=5.09\,\mathrm{s}$, so the second occupies the interval from $3.60$ to $5.09\,\mathrm{s}$. It is shorter because the ball is already rotating when that interval begins and continues to speed up. The two intervals cover equal angles, but they do not have equal average angular speeds.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Zamanın sıfır noktasını ikinci tur başında değiştirmedik. $t_2$, toplam iki turun süresidir; istenen ikinci turun süresi için $t_1$'i çıkardık. Sabit ivmede yolun zamanın karesiyle artması, eşit yolların giderek daha kısa sürede alınmasını açıklar.

Source: Young/Freedman, University Physics with Modern Physics, 14th Global Edition, Chapter 9, §9.2 Problems, question 11 (09_testbank.doc). Original givens retained; wording and worked explanation expanded.

Moving a rotation axis changes the moment of inertia

Problem. A slender, uniform $100\,\mathrm{cm}$ rod serves as a metre stick. Compare two parallel axes perpendicular to the rod: one passes through the $50\,\mathrm{cm}$ mark and the other through the $30\,\mathrm{cm}$ mark. Find $I_{30}/I_{50}$, the ratio of their moments of inertia.

Reasoning and algebra. Picture the rod lying horizontally, with both rotation axes pointing perpendicular to the page. The first axis passes through the centre of mass. The distance separating the axes is

$$ d=50\,\mathrm{cm}-30\,\mathrm{cm}=20\,\mathrm{cm}=0.200\,\mathrm{m}. $$

For a uniform slender rod, its central moment of inertia is $I_{50}=ML^2/12$. The parallel-axis theorem supplies the shifted value:

$$ I_{30}=I_{50}+Md^2. $$

Form the requested ratio before inserting a mass. The common mass cancels:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{I_{30}}{I_{50}} &=\frac{ML^2/12+Md^2}{ML^2/12}\\ &=1+\frac{Md^2}{ML^2/12} =1+\frac{12d^2}{L^2}\\ &=1+\frac{12(0.200\,\mathrm{m})^2}{(1.00\,\mathrm{m})^2} =\boxed{1.48}. \end{aligned} $$

Interpretation and check. The shifted-axis inertia is $48\%$ larger, approximately $1.5$ times the central value. This must exceed one because $Md^2$ is positive. If the second axis moved back to the centre, $d$ would become zero and the ratio would become one. No numerical mass is needed for a ratio involving the same rod.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. $d$, eksenin cetvel üzerindeki $30\,\mathrm{cm}$ konumu değildir; iki eksen arasındaki $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ uzaklıktır. Pay ve paydada bulunan kütleyi sadeleştiriyoruz. Kare alma işlemi hem uzaklığa hem birime uygulanır; bu nedenle son oran birimsizdir.

Source: Young/Freedman, University Physics with Modern Physics, 14th Global Edition, Chapter 9, §9.2 Problems, question 13 (09_testbank.doc). Original givens retained; wording and worked explanation expanded.

Convert RPM to rad/s and explain how moving mass away from the axis changes I.

Write one sentence naming the physical principle and one line of algebra you can now explain. TR: Sonuca nasıl ulaştığını bir cümleyle anlat; emin olmadığın ilk adımı işaretle.

Model responses / Örnek yanıtlar — open after trying

Entry: At constant angular acceleration, $\omega=\omega_0+\alpha t$ and $\Delta\theta=\omega_0t+\alpha t^2/2$ have the same algebraic form as the linear motion equations.

Exit: Multiply RPM by $2\pi/60$ to obtain rad/s; the 120 RPM example gives $4\pi$ rad/s. In $I=\sum mr^2$, moving every mass to twice its axis distance multiplies every contribution by four, so it multiplies $I$ by four.

Visual prediction: At equal $M,R$, a ring has $MR^2$ and a disk has $MR^2/2$: the ring's inertia is twice the disk's. A rod has $ML^2/12$ about its centre or $ML^2/3$ about its end. Its length and chosen axis are needed before comparing it to the ring.
