Before the equations: signs and two stagesİşaretler ve iki aşama

The required work is a diagram, a few equations, and a calculator. Python is optional verification. Draw before and after pictures; choose right/east as positive before substituting any velocities.

Momentum has a sign: $p=mv$. Kinetic energy has no directional sign: $K=\tfrac12mv^2$. A $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ object at $-3\,\mathrm{m/s}$ has $p=-6\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$ and $K=+9\,\mathrm J$.

Algebra for objects that stick: $$m_1v_1+m_2v_2=(m_1+m_2)v_f,$$ $$v_f=\frac{m_1v_1+m_2v_2}{m_1+m_2}.$$ Divide the whole numerator by the total mass. For $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $+3\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $-3\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $v_f=[6-3]/3=+1\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The plus sign gives the direction.

Do not mix stages. In a bullet–block collision, momentum is conserved during the short impact if external impulse is negligible. Kinetic energy decreases. During the later upward swing, mechanical energy is conserved if drag is negligible; gravity then changes momentum. Use a new equation for each stage.

In 2D, write two momentum equations, one for $x$ and one for $y$. Only after finding $v_x,v_y$ calculate $v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$ and the angle. Units: $1\,\mathrm{N\,s}=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$; these are impulse/momentum units, not joules.

Pause and explain: Could a collision conserve momentum but lose kinetic energy? Yes: two objects can stick while energy becomes deformation, sound, and heat.

Linear Momentum

Definition

Momentum is the product of an object's mass and velocity:

Key equation 9.1
$$\;\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\;$$
Quantity Symbol SI Unit Nature
Momentum $\vec{p}$ $\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$ Vector
Mass $m$ kg Scalar
Velocity $\vec{v}$ m/s Vector

Analogy: The Freight Train vs. The Sports Car

Think of momentum as "how hard it is to stop something." A slow freight train (huge mass, small velocity) can have the same momentum as a fast sports car (small mass, huge velocity). Equal momentum means equal stopping impulse. Equal stopping time would require equal average force; equal stopping distance instead depends on kinetic energy, which can differ.

Newton's Second Law in Momentum Form

Key equation 9.2
$$\;\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}\;$$

When mass is constant: $\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m\vec{a}$ (the familiar form).

A signed comparison / İşaretli karşılaştırma. Choose right as positive:

Object Mass Velocity Momentum Kinetic energy
A$2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$$+3.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$$+6.0\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$$9.0\,\mathrm J$
B$1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$$-6.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$$-6.0\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$$18.0\,\mathrm J$

Together their momenta cancel, but their energies add: $p_\mathrm{total}=0$ and $K_\mathrm{total}=27\,\mathrm J$. TR: Toplam momentumun sıfır olması, cisimlerin durduğu ya da sistemin kinetik enerjisinin sıfır olduğu anlamına gelmez.

Impulse-Momentum Theorem

Impulse is the change in momentum produced by a force acting over a time interval:

Key equation 9.3
$$\;\vec{J} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec{F}\, dt = \Delta\vec{p} = m\vec{v}_f - m\vec{v}_i\;$$

For the average net force over the interval, $\vec J=\vec F_{\mathrm{avg}}\Delta t$. For a constant net force this is simply $\vec J=\vec F\Delta t$. Time is a scalar; this multiplication is not a dot product.

Engineering Insight

This is why car airbags and crumple zones work! By increasing $\Delta t$, the average force on the occupant decreases for the same momentum change. The signed area under the $F$–$t$ curve (impulse) stays the same. A longer duration reduces the average force; the peak force also depends on the pulse shape. For a triangular pulse, $J=\frac12F_{\mathrm{peak}}\Delta t$, so $F_{\mathrm{peak}}=2J/\Delta t$.

ŞEKİL 9.1

Aynı kütleyi durdurmanın iki yolu. İki eğrinin altındaki alanlar eşittir, çünkü momentum değişimi her iki durumda da aynıdır — ama çarpışmayı dört kat uzatmak tepe kuvveti dörtte bire düşürür. Hava yastığının bütün mühendislik içeriği budur.

Impulse accumulates force over time

Multiplying velocity by positive mass gives the momentum vector: $\vec{p}=m\vec{v}$. Integrating net force over time gives its change:

$$\vec{J}=\int\vec{F}_{\rm net}\,dt=\Delta\vec{p}.$$

Geometry. On an $F_x$–$t$ graph, signed area gives $J_x$. Compare this with Week 05: an $F_x$–$x$ area gives work. Force times seconds gives $\mathrm{N\,s}=\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$; force times metres gives joules. The axis label decides the meaning of the area.

Predict. Two identical incoming packages are stopped without rebound. A soft stop takes twice as long as a hard stop. Must the stopping impulse double?

Separate change from rate

Both packages have the same momentum change, so the net impulses are equal. The average net stopping force halves when stopping time doubles. The peak force only halves if the pulse shapes are otherwise scaled copies. Rebound would require a larger momentum change than stopping at rest at the same incoming speed.

System matters. During a short collision, internal forces exchange momentum between the objects. Their impulses cancel in the total; external impulse determines whether total momentum is conserved. Kinetic energy can decrease while total momentum remains constant. Equal and opposite momenta can sum to zero while both objects have positive kinetic energy.

Transfer. A robot's bumper, a force sensor logging an impact and pellets striking a collector all require signed momentum and a stated system. A short collision can justify neglecting some external impulses, but duration alone is not proof: compare their size with the momentum change.

Conservation of Momentum

For an isolated system (no external forces), total momentum is conserved:

$$\vec{p}_{\text{total, before}} = \vec{p}_{\text{total, after}}$$

For two objects in 1D:

Key equation 9.4
$$\;m_1 v_{1i} + m_2 v_{2i} = m_1 v_{1f} + m_2 v_{2f}\;$$

Types of Collisions

Type Momentum Conserved? KE Conserved? Objects Stick? Example
Perfectly ElasticYesYesNoBilliard balls, Newton's cradle
InelasticYesNo (KE lost)NoCar crash with bounce
Perfectly InelasticYesNo (max KE lost)YesBullet in block
ExplosiveYesNo (KE gained)SeparateExplosion, spring release

Elastic Collision Formulas (1D)

$$v_{1f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2} v_{1i} + \frac{2m_2}{m_1 + m_2} v_{2i}$$

$$v_{2f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2} v_{1i} + \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2} v_{2i}$$

Perfectly Inelastic Collision Formula (1D)

Key equation 9.5
$$\;v_f = \frac{m_1 v_{1i} + m_2 v_{2i}}{m_1 + m_2}\quad\text{(they stick together)}\;$$

Worked example 9.1

the two carts stick together

A 2.0 kg cart moves right at 3.0 m/s and hits a stationary 1.0 kg cart. They stick. Choose right positive and neglect external impulse during the short collision. $$m_1v_{1i}+m_2v_{2i}=(m_1+m_2)v_f,$$ $$2(3)+1(0)=3v_f\Rightarrow v_f=2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}\text{ right}.$$ Initial kinetic energy is $K_i=\tfrac12(2)(3^2)=9$ J; final energy is $K_f=\tfrac12(3)(2^2)=6$ J. Momentum stays 6 kg m/s, while 3 J transfers to internal energy. Momentum conservation alone does not make a collision elastic.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Head-on Elastic Collision

A $2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ ball moving at $5.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ collides head-on elastically with a $3.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ ball initially at rest. Find the final velocities.

Paper solution. Choose the initial direction as positive. Momentum conservation gives $2(5)+3(0)=2v_1+3v_2$, or $10=2v_1+3v_2$. For a one-dimensional elastic collision, separation speed equals approach speed: $v_2-v_1=5$, so $v_2=v_1+5$.

Substitute into momentum: $10=2v_1+3(v_1+5)=5v_1+15$. Subtract 15 from both sides: $-5=5v_1$. Divide by 5: $v_1=-1\,\mathrm{m/s}$, then $v_2=4\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The first ball rebounds.

Check momentum: $2(-1)+3(4)=10\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$. Check energy: initial $\tfrac12(2)(5^2)=25\,\mathrm J$; final $\tfrac12(2)(1^2)+\tfrac12(3)(4^2)=1+24=25\,\mathrm J$.

Worked example 9.2

Example 2: Perfectly Inelastic Collision (Ballistic Pendulum)

A $10\,\mathrm g$ bullet traveling at $400\,\mathrm{m/s}$ embeds itself in a $2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ wooden block hanging from a string. How high does the block+bullet rise?

Paper solution: split the event into impact and swing. Convert $10\,\mathrm g$ to $0.010\,\mathrm{kg}$. During the brief impact, neglect external horizontal impulse: $$m_bu=(M+m_b)V\quad\Rightarrow\quad V=\frac{(0.010)(400)}{2.01}=1.99\ \mathrm{m/s}.$$ After impact, the combined mass swings upward. Now use energy: $$\tfrac12(M+m_b)V^2=(M+m_b)gh.$$ Cancel the same total mass on both sides, then divide by $g$: $h=\frac{V^2}{2g}=\frac{(1.99)^2}{19.62}\approx0.202\,\mathrm m$.

The initial bullet energy is $800\,\mathrm J$, but immediately after impact the motion energy is only $3.98\,\mathrm J$. Most becomes internal energy during embedding. Applying energy conservation to the impact would therefore predict a much too large height.

Worked example: Force on a dropped sample vial (Chemical Engineering)

Situation. A $0.0300\,\mathrm{kg}$ vial slips from $h=0.500\,\mathrm m$ onto a hard bench and stops without rebound in $\Delta t=2.00\,\mathrm{ms}$.

Predict. Is the average stopping force closer to $1$, $10$ or $100$ times the vial's weight?

Symbolic. Impact speed $v=\sqrt{2gh}$; impulse $J=\Delta p=mv$; average force $\bar F=mv/\Delta t$ (gravity's $mg\,\Delta t$ is negligible here).

Limiting check. $\Delta t\to\infty$ gives $\bar F\to0$: a soft landing spreads the same impulse over a long time.

Numbers. $v=\sqrt{2(9.81)(0.500)}=3.13\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $J=(0.0300)(3.13)=0.0940\,\mathrm{N\,s}$, $\bar F=\dfrac{0.0940}{0.00200}=47.0\,\mathrm N$, about $160$ times the weight $0.294\,\mathrm N$. A mat stretching the stop to $20.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ gives $4.70\,\mathrm N$.

Interpret. The impulse is fixed by the drop; only the stopping time decides whether the glass survives.

İtme düşüş yüksekliğiyle sabittir; durma süresi on kat uzarsa ortalama kuvvet on kat düşer.

Worked example: Collision force from accelerometer samples (Computer Engineering)

Situation. A $0.250\,\mathrm{kg}$ device's accelerometer samples at $1.00\,\mathrm{kHz}$ ($\Delta t=1.00\,\mathrm{ms}$). During a knock the readings are $0,\,20,\,40,\,60,\,40,\,20,\,0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Predict. Is the average force during the knock equal to the peak force?

Symbolic. Impulse is the area under $F$–$t$: $J=m\sum a_i\Delta t=m\,\Delta v$. Peak force $F_{\max}=ma_{\max}$; average force $\bar F=J/T$ over the pulse length $T$.

Limiting check. All-zero samples give $J=0$; a triangular pulse always has $\bar F=\tfrac12F_{\max}$.

Numbers. $\Delta v=(180\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(0.00100\,\mathrm s)=0.180\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $J=(0.250)(0.180)=0.0450\,\mathrm{N\,s}$, $F_{\max}=(0.250)(60.0)=15.0\,\mathrm N$, $\bar F=\dfrac{0.0450}{0.00600}=7.50\,\mathrm N$.

Interpret. Summing samples times $\Delta t$ is the impulse; a slower sampler that missed the $60\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ peak would underestimate both $J$ and the speed change.

TÜRKÇE

İtme, ivme örneklerinin toplamı çarpı örnekleme aralığıdır. Üçgen darbe için ortalama kuvvet tepe kuvvetin yarısıdır.

Worked example 9.3

Include gravity in a landing impulse

Problem. A $0.250\,\mathrm{kg}$ instrument falls from rest through $0.200\,\mathrm m$. A pad brings it to rest in $0.0300\,\mathrm s$. Estimate the average upward contact force during stopping; neglect drag and treat the stopping time as measured.

Picture and principle. Draw weight downward and pad force upward. Impact speed is $v=\sqrt{2gh}$. During contact, $(F_{\rm pad,avg}-mg)\Delta t=mv$, taking upward positive. Thus $F_{\rm pad,avg}=m\sqrt{2gh}/\Delta t+mg$.

Limiting check. For the same impact speed, shorter stopping time requires a larger average contact force. Contact force exceeds net stopping force by $mg$.

Numbers and units. $$v=\sqrt{2(9.81)(0.200)}\,\mathrm{m/s}=1.98\,\mathrm{m/s},\quad F_{\rm pad,avg}=\frac{(0.250)\sqrt{2(9.81)(0.200)}}{0.0300}\,\mathrm N+(0.250)(9.81)\,\mathrm N=19.0\,\mathrm N.$$

Interpret. The average does not reveal the peak force. A pad can satisfy an average-load requirement while exceeding a peak-load limit.

TÜRKÇE

İtme hesabı net kuvveti verir. Temas kuvvetini bulurken ağırlığı da hesaba kat; ortalama kuvvet tepe kuvvet değildir.

Worked example 9.4

Reject an impossible collision log

Problem. On a level air track, a $0.400\,\mathrm{kg}$ cart at $0.900\,\mathrm{m/s}$ hits a stationary $0.600\,\mathrm{kg}$ cart. Afterward the first is recorded at $-0.100\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and the second at $+0.800\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Test momentum conservation, then infer the second velocity consistent with it and the corresponding energy loss.

Picture and principle. Draw signed velocity arrows before and after. With negligible external horizontal impulse, $m_1u_1=m_1v_1+m_2v_2$, so $v_2=m_1(u_1-v_1)/m_2$. Evaluate kinetic energy separately; it need not be conserved.

Limiting check. If cart one is unchanged, cart two must remain at rest under this isolated two-cart model.

Numbers and units. $$p_i=0.360\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s},\quad p_{f,\rm log}=0.440\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}.$$ The consistent value is $v_2=(0.400)(0.900+0.100)/0.600=0.667\,\mathrm{m/s}$; then $K_i-K_f=0.0267\,\mathrm J$.

Interpret. The original log needs an external impulse or a measurement error. The corrected isolated collision loses kinetic energy and is physically compatible with a passive impact.

TÜRKÇE

Önce işaretli momentum toplamlarını karşılaştır. Momentum korunumu, kinetik enerjinin de korunacağı anlamına gelmez.

Worked example 9.5

Calibrate a pendulum catcher

Problem. A $0.0200\,\mathrm{kg}$ pellet embeds in a $0.480\,\mathrm{kg}$ suspended catcher. Their combined centre of mass then rises $0.0300\,\mathrm m$. A lab report claims that 90% of the pellet’s initial kinetic energy becomes gravitational potential energy. Test the claim by inferring the launch speed and the fraction of energy retained after embedding. Assume the short collision has negligible external horizontal impulse and the subsequent swing has negligible losses.

Picture and principle. Draw two stages. During embedding, $mu=(M+m)V$. Thus the retained energy fraction is $\eta=[(M+m)V^2/2]/(mu^2/2)=m/(M+m)$. During the swing, $(M+m)V^2/2=(M+m)gh$. Combining gives $u=(M+m)\sqrt{2gh}/m$.

Limiting check. As the rise tends to zero the inferred speed tends to zero. A heavier catcher needs a faster pellet for the same rise.

Numbers and units. $$V=\sqrt{2(9.81)(0.0300)}\,\mathrm{m/s}=0.767\,\mathrm{m/s},\quad u=\frac{0.480+0.0200}{0.0200}\sqrt{2(9.81)(0.0300)}\,\mathrm{m/s}=19.2\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$ The retained fraction is $\eta=0.0200/(0.480+0.0200)=0.0400=4.00\%$.

Interpret. The 90% claim is false: only 4.00% remains as mechanical energy after embedding. Energy is conserved in the swing but not in the embedding collision. Applying one energy equation across both stages gives the wrong speed.

TÜRKÇE

Çarpışmada momentum, sonraki salınımda mekanik enerji korunur. İki aşamayı tek bir enerji korunumu denklemiyle birleştirme.

Worked example 9.6

Arrange a diagonal docking motion

Problem. Two carts latch together on a horizontal air table. Cart A has mass $0.300\,\mathrm{kg}$ and travels east at $0.800\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Cart B has mass $0.500\,\mathrm{kg}$ and approaches northward. Choose B’s speed so the combined cart moves northeast at exactly $45.0^\circ$, and find the combined speed.

Picture and principle. Draw perpendicular incoming momenta. After latching, $V_x=m_Au_A/(m_A+m_B)$ and $V_y=m_Bu_B/(m_A+m_B)$. A $45^\circ$ direction requires equal components, so $u_B=m_Au_A/m_B$.

Limiting check. If B has zero incoming momentum, the final motion is purely eastward. Equal final components require equal incoming momenta, not equal speeds.

Numbers and units. $$u_B=\frac{(0.300)(0.800)}{0.500}\,\mathrm{m/s}=0.480\,\mathrm{m/s},\quad V_x=V_y=0.300\,\mathrm{m/s},\quad |\vec{V}|=\sqrt2(0.300)\,\mathrm{m/s}=0.424\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

Interpret. The heavier incoming cart needs the lower speed. Momentum conservation sets the direction even though latching dissipates kinetic energy.

TÜRKÇE

Son yönü hızlar değil momentum bileşenleri belirler. Kütleler farklıysa eşit momentum için hızlar eşit olmaz.

Worked example 9.7

Specify a recoil-safe release

Problem. Two platforms of masses $2.00$ and $3.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ initially rest on an air track. An internal spring pushes them apart with relative separation speed $0.750\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find each ground-frame velocity, taking the lighter platform’s motion as positive.

Picture and principle. Draw opposite velocity arrows. Momentum gives $m_1v_1+m_2v_2=0$; the relative-speed condition gives $v_1-v_2=u$. Solve: $v_1=m_2u/(m_1+m_2)$ and $v_2=-m_1u/(m_1+m_2)$.

Limiting check. Equal masses recoil with equal and opposite velocities. A much heavier platform moves very little.

Numbers and units. $$v_1=\frac{3.00(0.750)}{2.00+3.00}\,\mathrm{m/s}=+0.450\,\mathrm{m/s},\quad v_2=-0.300\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

Interpret. The relative speed belongs to the pair; assigning it to either platform would violate momentum conservation. The spring supplies the gained kinetic energy.

TÜRKÇE

Bağıl hız iki hızın farkıdır. Hafif platform daha hızlı gider; iki platformun momentumu eşit büyüklükte ve zıt yönlüdür.
