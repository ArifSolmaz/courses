Course Description

This calculus-based physics course teaches the prediction of mechanical systems through Python-enhanced simulations. Given any mechanical system (falling, sliding, rotating, oscillating), students learn to draw the diagram, identify the right principle (Newton's laws, energy conservation, momentum conservation), write the equation, predict the outcome, and verify with simulation.

The course follows a consistent methodology: Diagram → Principle → Equation → Prediction → Verify. Each week includes a Lecture notebook and a Problem Lab notebook delivered via Google Colab.

Learning Outcomes

Upon successful completion of this course, the student will be able to:

  1. Perform dimensional analysis and vector decomposition as the foundation for all physics predictions.
  2. Describe and predict 1D and 2D motion using kinematic equations and graphical analysis.
  3. Apply Newton's laws and free body diagrams to predict the motion of systems with multiple forces.
  4. Use energy and momentum conservation as shortcut principles for predicting outcomes of complex interactions.
  5. Analyze rotational systems using torque, moment of inertia, and angular momentum conservation.
  6. Model simple harmonic motion and predict resonance conditions in oscillating systems.
  7. Describe wave propagation, interference, and standing wave patterns using superposition principles.
  8. Complete a capstone project that models a mechanical system, predicts its behavior, and verifies with simulation.

Where the Notebooks Fit

The weekly notebooks and their problem sets are not directly graded. They are how you prepare: the quizzes, midterm, and final all draw on the same problems and the same workflow, so the notebook you skip is the question you lose marks on later.

  • Independent modelling: produce the diagram, equation, prediction, verification, and limitation yourself
  • Correctness: predictions match expected outcomes; simulations verify analytical solutions
  • Methodology: follow Diagram → Principle → Equation → Prediction → Verify
  • Reasoning: systematic physical argument, not formula-plugging

Worked Solutions (released after each set closes)

After a problem set is due, a Python Solution Lab notebook is published for that week — solutions/Week_XX_Python_Solutions.ipynb. Each contains one fully worked problem at every difficulty level (L1 Basic, L2 Intermediate, L3 Challenge), following the same Diagram → Principle → Equation → Prediction → Verify workflow the quizzes and exams expect of you.

  • Each solution states the problem, names the governing principle, and gives the hand prediction you should make before running anything
  • Results are then verified by a second independent route — energy versus forces, symbolic versus numerical, closed form versus simulation
  • Parameters sit at the top of every cell, so you can change one value and re-run to see how the answer responds

These are worth very little if you read them before attempting the problem. Attempt first, predict on paper, then compare.

Modeling Evidence Requirements

  • Units: numerical answers must include appropriate units
  • Assumptions: students must state the model and simplifying assumptions used
  • Verification: calculations should be checked by code, graph, limiting case, or physical intuition when appropriate
  • Interpretation: plots and simulations must be explained in physical language

Course Policies

Attendance

Regular attendance is expected. Students who miss more than 30% of classes may be denied the right to take the final exam, per university regulations. Attendance at the separate physics laboratory course is tracked by that course.

Quizzes

Quizzes are run online and open for a stated window. A missed window scores zero, since the answers are visible once it closes — so sit each quiz while it is open rather than asking for an extension afterwards. If illness or a documented emergency prevents this, contact the instructor before the window closes wherever possible. Weekly notebooks carry no submission deadline: they are practice, not a graded hand-in.

Academic Honesty

Quizzes and exams must be your own work. Practice notebooks are for learning and you are encouraged to work through them together — the rules below apply to assessed work:

  • Consulting another person, a shared document, or an AI assistant during an online quiz
  • Copying from another student, or supplying answers to one, during a quiz or exam
  • Any material or device not explicitly permitted in the exam room

AI Tool Usage

AI assistants (ChatGPT, Copilot, etc.) may be used as learning aids to understand concepts, debug code, and review reasoning. Quizzes and exams are closed to AI assistance. You must be able to explain units, assumptions, equations, plots, and any code you submit. Work that you cannot explain or modify may lose credit even if the final answer is correct.

Live Checks in Class

The instructor may ask you to change a parameter, explain units, identify a force, interpret a graph, or predict what happens if an assumption changes. These are teaching checks, not a graded component — but they are the same moves the quizzes and exams ask for, so treat them as rehearsal.
