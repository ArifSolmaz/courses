Course Description

This calculus-based physics course teaches the prediction of mechanical systems through physical models, explicit calculations and optional visual checks. Given any mechanical system (falling, sliding, rotating, oscillating), students learn to draw the diagram, identify the right principle (Newton's laws, energy conservation, momentum conservation), reach a symbolic answer, check it in a limiting case, predict the outcome with units, and verify it — in the laboratory against measured data, or against a supplied simulation.

The course follows a consistent methodology: Diagram → Principle → Symbolic answer → Limiting check → Numbers with units → Interpret. There is one notebook per week, following the lecture schedule; each contains the explanations, worked examples and a three-level problem set for its topic. Eight laboratory sessions follow the departmental Deney column. Review and midterm weeks are protected, and the midterm and final are common examinations.

Learning Outcomes

Upon successful completion of this course, the student will be able to:

  1. Perform dimensional analysis and vector decomposition as the foundation for all physics predictions.
  2. Describe and predict 1D and 2D motion using kinematic equations and graphical analysis.
  3. Apply Newton's laws and free body diagrams to predict the motion of systems with multiple forces.
  4. Use energy and momentum conservation as shortcut principles for predicting outcomes of complex interactions.
  5. Analyze rotational systems using torque, moment of inertia, and angular momentum conservation (angular momentum is taught within Week 11; precession and vector treatment are optional extension material).
  6. Use force and torque balance to solve equilibrium and centre-of-mass problems.
  7. Model periodic motion using spring–mass and small-angle-pendulum assumptions.
  8. Take a physical measurement, state it with an honest uncertainty, and decide whether a measured result agrees with a prediction (laboratory requirement; not examined in the common exams).
  9. Optional extension: interpret resonance, waves and standing-wave patterns using appropriate physical models.
  10. Optional synthesis: model a mechanical system and compare its predictions with data or a supplied simulation.

Review buffers and the final four weeks

Use 10–15-minute prerequisite checks within Weeks 03, 08 and 10 and short joining recaps in Weeks 10–13. Keep Week 06 for problem solving and summary; use the official holiday-adjusted meeting arrangement in the 29 October week. These pauses do not add teaching weeks.

The course is delivered to Mechatronics Engineering, Computer Engineering and Chemical Engineering students together. Weeks 10–13 pose the same physics as application questions from all three departments. Writing Python is optional.

One notebook per week, plus laboratory briefs and optional extensions

Each week has exactly one notebook: the concepts with worked algebra, interactive checks with a prediction prompt before each one, worked examples, and the problem set graded L1 (core) / L2 / L3. Every problem shows its answer so you can check yourself; the full worked solution is released later, so write your own attempt first — symbolic answer, one limiting-case check, then numbers.

Eight laboratory briefs follow the departmental Deney column, each opening with a prediction made before the apparatus is switched on. A final-review notebook covers Weeks 01–13 for the common final. Six optional notebooks (circular motion, angular momentum, resonance, waves and sound, review and mini-projects, and gravitation and orbits) are listed on the course dashboard; none is a calendar week and none is examinable.

Laboratory / Laboratuvar

Eight sessions, from the departmental schedule

The Deney column of the departmental schedule sets the experiments and their order, shown in the calendar above. Shared technique — reading uncertainty from an instrument, repeated measurements, propagation, significant figures, linearising a curve to read a slope, and deciding whether two numbers agree — lives in one place: the measurement and uncertainty toolkit.

Experiments run deliberately behind the lectures: the Week 04 experiment measures Week 02 physics, and the Week 05 experiment measures Week 03 physics. The one exception is the simple pendulum in the Week 11 session, whose lecture treatment is Week 13 — Lab 11 therefore carries a self-contained primer, and Week 11 a short preview.

How reports are marked

Reports are graded on the quality of your reasoning about your own data, never on how close you land to the accepted value. A result that disagrees, with the discrepancy quantified in standard deviations and a named systematic error whose sign you can justify, earns full marks. A report that quotes the textbook value without data to support it does not.

Every brief opens with a prediction made before the apparatus is switched on, stated symbolically with a limiting-case check. Laboratory analysis technique is a laboratory requirement and is not examined in the common midterm or final; the physics being measured is.

Course Delivery & Learning Contract

What Students Can Expect Every Week

  • Protected class time: the 3-hour session starts and finishes according to the official timetable, with a visible agenda and planned breaks.
  • Clear explanation: each major idea follows explain → worked example → prediction → test → interpretation.
  • Questions and voice: questions are welcome throughout; checkpoints reserve explicit time for think-pair-explain, misconceptions, and open questions.
  • Professional purpose: the course is delivered to Mechatronics, Computer and Chemical Engineering students and repeatedly connects concepts to mechanical systems, sensing and simulation, and process equipment such as pumps, stirrers and pipe supports, with physically defensible computation.
  • Exam alignment: notebooks label the reasoning moves rehearsed on assessments. Exams use the same verbs and standards with new values or contexts.
  • Materials: weekly notes, runnable examples, core practice, feedback checkpoints, optional extensions, and references are provided from the start of the term.

Where the Notebooks Fit

The weekly notebooks and their problem sets are not directly graded. They are how you prepare: the quizzes, midterm, and final all draw on the same problems and the same workflow, so the notebook you skip is the question you lose marks on later.

  • Independent modelling: produce the diagram, equation, prediction, verification, and limitation yourself
  • Correctness: predictions match expected outcomes; simulations verify analytical solutions
  • Methodology: follow Diagram → Principle → Equation → Prediction → Verify
  • Reasoning: systematic physical argument, not formula-plugging

Worked Solutions (released after each set closes)

After a problem set closes, its Complete Worked Physics Solutions notebook is released as solutions/Week_XX_Python_Solutions.ipynb. It covers all ten numbered problems and their subparts at L1, L2, and L3: 140 problems across the weekly and extension notebooks, plus the three mini-project walkthroughs. The weekly notebooks already contain a answer for every problem, so you can check yourself; the solution file adds the complete working and optional Python.

  • Follow the principle, equation, algebraic rearrangement, substitution with units, and physical interpretation before using a calculator or demonstration.
  • English explanations include short Turkish support and explicit assumptions.
  • Python is an optional verification tool; writing code is not required to understand or reproduce the hand-worked solutions.

These are worth very little if you read them before attempting the problem. Attempt first, predict on paper, then compare.

Modeling Evidence Requirements

  • Units: numerical answers must include appropriate units
  • Assumptions: students must state the model and simplifying assumptions used
  • Verification: calculations should be checked by code, graph, limiting case, or physical intuition when appropriate
  • Interpretation: plots and simulations must be explained in physical language

Course Policies

Attendance

Regular attendance is expected. Students who miss more than 30% of classes may be denied the right to take the final exam, per university regulations.

Quizzes

Quizzes are run online and open for a stated window. A missed window scores zero, since the answers are visible once it closes — so sit each quiz while it is open rather than asking for an extension afterwards. If illness or a documented emergency prevents this, contact the instructor before the window closes wherever possible. Weekly notebooks carry no collection deadline: they are private practice, not a graded hand-in.

Academic Honesty

Quizzes and exams must be your own work. Practice notebooks are for learning and you are encouraged to work through them together — the rules below apply to assessed work:

  • Consulting another person, a shared document, or an AI assistant during an online quiz
  • Copying from another student, or supplying answers to one, during a quiz or exam
  • Any material or device not explicitly permitted in the exam room

AI Tool Usage

AI assistants (ChatGPT, Copilot, etc.) may be used as learning aids to understand concepts, debug code, and review reasoning. Quizzes and exams are closed to AI assistance. You must be able to explain units, assumptions, equations, plots, and any code you demonstrate. Work that you cannot explain or modify may lose credit even if the final answer is correct.

Live Checks in Class

The instructor may ask you to change a parameter, explain units, identify a force, interpret a graph, or predict what happens if an assumption changes. These are teaching checks, not a graded component — but they are the same moves the quizzes and exams ask for, so treat them as rehearsal.
