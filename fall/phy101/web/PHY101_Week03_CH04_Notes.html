Newton’s laws of motion

Three statements published in 1687 still describe how every car, planet and thrown ball moves. This lesson lets you push on things and watch the laws work: predict what will happen, run the simulation, then explain what you saw.

Four ideas you need first

A force is a push or a pull that one object exerts on another. It has a magnitude (measured in newtons, N) and a direction, so it is a vector. Every force has an agent: if you cannot name what is pushing or pulling, it is not a force.

The net force ΣF is the vector sum of all forces acting on one object. Newton’s laws are statements about the net force, never about a single force in isolation.

Mass is not weight. Mass m (kg) measures how much an object resists changes in its motion; it is the same everywhere. Weight w = mg is the gravitational force on the object; it changes with g.

The three laws at a glance

1

Inertia

With zero net force, velocity stays constant. Motion does not need a cause; change of motion does.

 2

ΣF = ma

Net force produces acceleration in its own direction, in proportion to the force and in inverse proportion to the mass.

 3

Action and reaction

Forces come in pairs acting on different bodies: equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, same type.

Time. About 75–90 minutes for all three laws with discussion; each law section is a self-contained 20–25 minute block if you prefer three shorter sessions.

Learning objectives. Students should be able to (1) state the three laws in words and symbols; (2) draw a free-body diagram and translate it into ΣF = ma component equations; (3) distinguish mass from weight and net force from an individual force; (4) identify third-law pairs and explain why they never cancel; (5) solve single-object problems with friction, inclines and apparent weight.

Warm-up with the playground. Ask students to make the block move at constant velocity with μ = 0.3. They must first exceed static friction, then reduce F to exactly μmg. The readout showing ΣF = 0 while the block keeps moving is the first law in one picture.

Newton’s first law

Inertia: motion needs no cause

A body keeps doing what it is already doing—resting, or moving in a straight line at constant speed—until a net force makes it change. The surprising half is the second one: moving does not require a push.

The law

If the net external force on a body is zero, its velocity is constant: a body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed.

In symbols: ΣF = 0 if and only if a = 0, in an inertial reference frame.

What the law really says

For two thousand years the common-sense view, going back to Aristotle, was that moving things need something to keep them moving: stop pushing a cart and it stops. Galileo saw through this. The cart stops because friction acts on it, not because its motion “runs out”. Remove the friction and the cart would roll forever. Newton made this the starting point of mechanics: the natural state of a body is not rest, it is constant velocity, and rest is just the special case v = 0.

The property that makes a body resist changes in its velocity is called inertia, and mass is its measure. A full shopping trolley is harder to start, harder to stop and harder to steer than an empty one. Nothing about this involves gravity: the same trolley would be just as hard to start in orbit.

Read the law as a test rather than a description: if you see velocity changing, in magnitude or in direction, a net force is acting; if velocity is constant, the forces balance. A car turning a corner at a steady 50 km/h is accelerating, because its direction changes, so a net force (friction from the road) must be acting on it.

One fine print: the law holds in inertial reference frames, frames that are not themselves accelerating. Inside a braking bus you seem to be thrown forward with no force acting on you. Seen from the pavement, nothing pushes you forward: you simply keep your velocity while the bus slows beneath you.

Misconception to unlearn

“A moving object must have a force acting in the direction of motion.” No. A coasting bicycle, a puck on ice and a spacecraft between planets all move with no forward force on them. If you ever write “force of motion” on a free-body diagram, cross it out: there is no agent exerting it.

Newton’s second law

Force changes motion: ΣF = ma

The first law says a net force changes velocity. The second law says by how much, and in which direction: the acceleration points along the net force, grows with the force and shrinks with the mass.

The law

The acceleration of a body is directly proportional to the net force acting on it, inversely proportional to its mass, and in the direction of the net force.

ΣF = ma   ⇔   ΣFx = max,  ΣFy = may

The newton is defined by this law: 1 N is the net force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s², so 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Reading the equation

It is a vector equation. The acceleration has the direction of the net force, not of any single force and not of the velocity. A ball thrown upward is slowing down because its acceleration (down, from gravity) points opposite to its velocity (up). The two directions agree only when something speeds up along a straight line.

Mass is the constant of proportionality. Push with twice the force and you get twice the acceleration; push the same force on twice the mass and you get half. The simulation above lets you test both statements with numbers, and the ratio F/a is the same for a cart whatever force you use. That ratio is the mass.

Weight is a force, so it obeys the law too. Near Earth’s surface the only force on a freely falling object is gravity, and all objects fall with the same acceleration g ≈ 9.81 m/s². Hence the gravitational force on a mass m must be w = mg. A heavier object is pulled harder, but it also has more inertia, and the two effects cancel exactly.

Where it comes from. Newton actually wrote the law in terms of momentum, p = mv: the net force equals the rate of change of momentum, ΣF = dp/dt. For constant mass this reduces to ΣF = ma; the general form is what you need for rockets, which lose mass as they burn fuel.

Free-body diagrams: from picture to equations

Every second-law problem follows the same path. Isolate one object. Draw each force acting on it (and only on it) from a point: name the force, its agent and its direction. Choose axes so that the acceleration lies along one of them. Write ΣF = ma for each axis. Solve. Then check: do the units work, and does the answer behave sensibly in limiting cases?

The incline below is the classic example. Tilting the axes to run along the slope and perpendicular to it means the acceleration is purely along x, and only the weight needs to be split into components.

Two misconceptions to unlearn

“The force points the way the object is moving.” Force points the way the acceleration does. At the top of its flight a thrown ball is momentarily at rest, yet the net force on it is its full weight, pointing down. If the force were zero there, the ball would stay up.

“Heavier things fall faster.” In air, sometimes, because of drag. Without air, every mass falls with the same g: the extra pull on a heavier object is exactly used up overcoming its extra inertia.

Newton’s third law

Forces come in pairs

You cannot touch without being touched. Whenever one body pushes or pulls on another, the second pushes or pulls back on the first with a force of exactly the same size in the opposite direction. The two forces act on different bodies, which is why they never cancel.

The law

If body A exerts a force on body B, then body B exerts a force on body A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

FA on B = −FB on A

The force arrows always have the same length. The velocities do not: each skater’s speed is FΔt/m, so the lighter skater moves faster, in exactly the inverse ratio of the masses. The total momentum stays at zero, a first glimpse of momentum conservation.

How to identify a pair

Name every force with the sentence “A exerts a force on B”. Its third-law partner is the force you get by swapping the names: “B exerts a force on A”. If the partner you have written down does not fit this swap, it is not the partner.

  • Equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, at every instant.
  • Same type: gravity pairs with gravity, contact with contact, tension with tension.
  • They act on different bodies, so they never appear together on one free-body diagram, and they can never cancel each other.

The last point resolves the classic puzzle. A horse pulls a cart forward; the cart pulls the horse backward with equal force. Why does anything move? Because the horse’s motion is decided only by the forces on the horse: the cart’s backward pull and the ground’s forward push on its hooves. If the ground pushes harder than the cart pulls, the horse accelerates. The cart’s motion likewise depends only on the forces on the cart.

Find the partner

The most common wrong pair

A book rests on a table. Its weight (Earth pulls the book down) and the normal force (the table pushes the book up) are equal and opposite, but they are not a third-law pair: both act on the book, and one is gravitational while the other is a contact force. They are balanced because the book is in equilibrium, which is the first law, not the third. The true partners are: the book pulls Earth up; the book pushes the table down.

Why anything moves at all

Every form of propulsion is a third-law story. You walk by pushing the ground backward; the ground pushes you forward. A car’s tires push backward on the road and the road pushes the car forward: the engine turns the wheels, but it is friction from the road that accelerates the car. A swimmer pushes water backward. A rocket throws exhaust gas backward at high speed, and the gas pushes the rocket forward; no air is needed to “push against”, which is why rockets work in empty space.

The law also says that a heavy truck and a small car exert equal forces on each other during a collision. The car suffers more because the same force produces a much larger acceleration on the smaller mass, ΣF = ma again.

Putting the laws to work

Applications and problem solving

The laws are short; using them well is a routine. This section fixes the routine, then applies it to an elevator ride, a crate on a rope and an Atwood machine, with practice problems to finish.

The routine

  1. Pick one object and sketch it alone. Decide what it touches (contact forces) and what pulls on it at a distance (gravity).
  2. Draw the free-body diagram. One arrow per force, each labelled with type and agent: “normal force from floor”, “tension from rope”. No “force of motion”, no partner forces (they act on other objects).
  3. Choose axes so that the acceleration lies along one axis, and state the direction you call positive.
  4. Write ΣF = ma per axis, resolving any force that is not along an axis into components.
  5. Solve algebraically first, then substitute numbers.
  6. Check: units, sign, and the limiting cases (angle to zero, friction to zero, equal masses).

Apparent weight: predict, then ride

A bathroom scale reads the normal force it exerts on you, not your weight. Standing in an accelerating elevator, the two differ. With “up” positive, ΣFy = N − mg = ma, so the scale reads N = m(g + a). What matters is the acceleration, not the direction of travel.

Worked examples

Reveal the steps one at a time and try to write the next one before you show it.

Example 1: a crate pulled by a rope at an angle

A 10.0 kg crate is dragged along a horizontal floor by a rope that makes 30° with the floor. The tension in the rope is 40.0 N and the coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.20. Find the crate’s acceleration.

  1. Free-body diagram Four forces on the crate: weight mg = 98.1 N straight down; normal force N straight up; tension T = 40.0 N at 30° above the floor; kinetic friction fk along the floor, opposite the motion.
  2. Axes x along the floor in the direction of motion, y upward. The crate stays on the floor, so ay = 0 and the only unknown acceleration is ax = a.
  3. Vertical equation ΣFy = N + T sin 30° − mg = 0, so N = 98.1 N − 20.0 N = 78.1 N. The rope carries part of the weight, so the floor pushes up less than mg.
  4. Friction fk = μkN = 0.20 × 78.1 N = 15.6 N. Using mg instead of N here is the most common error in this problem.
  5. Horizontal equation ΣFx = T cos 30° − fk = 34.6 N − 15.6 N = 19.0 N = ma, so a = 19.0 N / 10.0 kg = 1.90 m/s².
  6. Check N/kg is m/s². With no friction the result would be T cos 30°/m = 3.46 m/s², larger, as it should be. With the rope horizontal, N = mg, friction rises to 19.6 N and a = 2.04 m/s²: pulling at an angle trades horizontal pull for reduced friction.

Example 2: the Atwood machine

Blocks of 2.00 kg and 3.00 kg hang from the two ends of a light string passing over a frictionless, massless pulley. Find the acceleration of the blocks and the tension in the string.

  1. Two free-body diagrams Each block feels its weight downward and the tension T upward. A light string over a frictionless, massless pulley has the same tension throughout, so the same T appears in both diagrams.
  2. Coordinates The heavier block goes down and the lighter one up. Take “positive” as the direction each block actually moves: up for m1 = 2.00 kg, down for m2 = 3.00 kg. Because the string does not stretch, both have the same acceleration magnitude a.
  3. Second law for each block Block 1: T − m1g = m1a. Block 2: m2g − T = m2a.
  4. Eliminate the tension Adding the two equations: (m2 − m1)g = (m1 + m2)a, so a = (1.00 kg)(9.81 m/s²)/(5.00 kg) = 1.96 m/s².
  5. Find the tension From block 1: T = m1(g + a) = 2.00 kg × 11.77 m/s² = 23.5 N. (The symbolic result is T = 2m1m2g/(m1 + m2).)
  6. Check The tension lies between the two weights, 19.6 N and 29.4 N; it must, to pull the light block up and let the heavy one down. If the masses were equal the formulas give a = 0 and T = mg, which is the equilibrium you would expect.

Practice

Work each one on paper with a free-body diagram before opening the answer. Use g = 9.81 m/s².

1. A 1200 kg car accelerates uniformly from rest to 20.0 m/s in 8.00 s on a level road. What net force acts on it?

a = Δv/Δt = 2.50 m/s², so ΣF = ma = 1200 kg × 2.50 m/s² = 3.00 × 10³ N, forward. This is the net force; it is supplied by friction from the road on the tires.

2. A 60.0 kg student stands on a scale in an elevator that accelerates downward at 1.50 m/s². What does the scale read?

With up positive, a = −1.50 m/s²: N = m(g + a) = 60.0 kg × 8.31 m/s² = 499 N. The scale dial, which is calibrated in kilograms by dividing by g, would show about 50.8 kg. The same reading occurs whether the elevator is moving down and speeding up or moving up and slowing down.

3. A block is already sliding down a 25.0° incline where the coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.300. Find its acceleration.

Along the slope: mg sin θ − μkmg cos θ = ma, so a = g(sin 25.0° − 0.300 cos 25.0°) = 9.81 × (0.4226 − 0.2719) = 1.48 m/s², down the slope. The mass cancels. You can check this value with the incline simulation in the second-law section.

4. Two boxes of 3.00 kg and 5.00 kg sit side by side, touching, on a frictionless floor. A horizontal 16.0 N push is applied to the 3.00 kg box. Find the acceleration of the boxes and the force the 3.00 kg box exerts on the 5.00 kg box.

Treat both boxes as one 8.00 kg object: a = 16.0 N / 8.00 kg = 2.00 m/s². Now isolate the 5.00 kg box: the only horizontal force on it is the push from the 3.00 kg box, so that contact force is 5.00 kg × 2.00 m/s² = 10.0 N. Check with the 3.00 kg box: 16.0 N − 10.0 N = 6.00 N = 3.00 kg × 2.00 m/s². The 5.00 kg box pushes back on the 3.00 kg box with 10.0 N: a third-law pair.

Instructor notes: applications (25 min)

Elevator. Before running the trip, have students sketch the scale reading against time for an upward trip, then compare with the graph. Most draw the reading above mg for the whole upward ride; the cruise phase corrects this. Follow with the downward trip and the cable cut.

Worked examples. Reveal one step, hide the screen, and ask the class to produce the next step on paper. The two deliberate traps are N = mg in Example 1 and “the heavier block has a larger acceleration” in Example 2.

Practice problem 4 is a good exit ticket: it needs the second law twice (system, then subsystem) and the third law once.
