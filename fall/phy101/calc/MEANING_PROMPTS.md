# Calculator practice — explain before pressing keys

Use these alongside the existing 15 challenges. Before starting the timed round, ask students to draw the situation and predict the sign, direction or scale on paper. After closing submissions, discuss the physical reason, then play the existing calculator walkthrough. These are oral/paper prompts; the form and saved score format are unchanged.

Correct eligible answers earn 10 points; incorrect answers earn 0. There is no speed bonus. A written explanation helps teach and discuss reasoning; it is not proof that a student did not use outside help.

| Challenge | Before calculation | After submissions close |
| --- | --- | --- |
| W2-C1: square a negative | Draw negative initial velocity, positive acceleration and **positive signed displacement**. Is final speed greater than the initial speed magnitude? | The trolley first moves left, stops, then returns and reaches a point right of its start. The positive displacement is not path length. With positive net work over that displacement, final speed is larger. Squaring a signed velocity gives a nonnegative value. |
| W2-C2: numerator brackets | Predict the acceleration sign from the speed change and positive travel direction. | The complete change in squared speed belongs in the numerator. At fixed endpoints in speed, twice the distance needs half the constant acceleration. |
| W2-C3: km/h to m/s | Sketch a velocity–time triangle for braking. What happens to stopping distance if initial speed doubles at the same deceleration magnitude? | It quadruples; speed must first be expressed in units compatible with acceleration. Reaction distance is outside this question. |
| W3-C1: DEG/RAD | Draw horizontal and vertical launch components. State the height and drag assumptions behind the range equation. | For equal launch/landing heights and no drag, complementary angles share a range. The angle mode must match degrees; a negative range for these specified angles signals an error. |
| W3-C2: cliff flight time | Sketch the rise, return to launch height and further fall. Which component sets flight time? | The vertical component does. Landing below the launch point takes longer than returning to its height; the positive root describes the later landing event. Divide the entire numerator by gravity. |
| W3-C3: EXP | Predict whether a tonne-scale mass with acceleration of a few metres per second squared needs units, thousands or millions of newtons. | Mass scales the acceleration vector to give net force. EXP already means multiplying by a power of ten; an extra factor of ten changes the physical scale. |
| W4-C1: incline projections | Draw gravity and resolve it along/across the plane. Why is friction subtracted for downhill sliding? | It opposes the relative downhill slip. The acceleration is the signed imbalance divided by mass; the sign can differ from the current velocity. |
| W4-C2: inverse trig | Explain why a stationary block's friction grows as the incline is raised. | Sliding begins at the static limit, giving tan(angle) = coefficient in this model. This is sliding onset, assuming the block does not tip first. |
| W4-C3: Atwood fraction | Predict which side descends and what happens when the masses become equal. | The weight difference drives both masses, so the denominator contains their sum. The usual expression assumes a light rope and negligible pulley inertia as well as negligible axle friction. |
| W5-C1: obtuse angle | Draw the force projection along displacement and predict the work sign. | The projection is opposite displacement, so this force removes kinetic energy through negative work. A force can pull while an object continues moving against that pull. |
| W5-C2: spring square | Sketch the straight applied-force versus compression graph and identify its area. | The triangle gives one half times stiffness times compression squared. In Week 05 explain this as work needed to compress an ideal spring quasistatically; configuration energy is developed in Week 08. |
| W5-C3: prefixes and power | Same work in half the time: what changes? | Average power doubles; transferred work does not. Kilojoules must be converted when reporting joules per second in watts. |
| W6-C1: root of a sum | Sketch the two perpendicular forces tip-to-tail. Bound the resultant between the larger individual force and their scalar sum. | Components add as vectors; the resultant magnitude follows the right triangle. The root applies to the sum of squares, not each contribution separately. |
| W6-C2: two stages | Label the height loss and rough horizontal distance. Where does gravity do work and where does friction do negative work? | Use net work to find the change in kinetic energy over both stages. Check whether the block has enough kinetic energy to reach the stated endpoint; do not take a square root of a negative speed squared. |
| W6-C3: significant figures | Draw the parallel projection and estimate work before detailed calculation. | Keep full precision until the final report. Three significant figures describe the reported number; they do not justify rounding every intermediate operation. |

**TR:** Tuşlara basmadan önce şekli çiz, işareti veya değişim yönünü tahmin et. Gönderimler kapandıktan sonra fiziksel gerekçeyi açıklayıp tuş adımlarını göster. Bu rutin forma yeni alan eklemez ve dış yardım kullanımını tek başına engellemez.
