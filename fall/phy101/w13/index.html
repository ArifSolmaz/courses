Joining this lessonDerse buradan başlayanlar

Quick recap. Choose an equilibrium position and a positive direction. Newton's law $\sum F=ma$ and the linear spring force $F=-kx$ give $a=-(k/m)x$, $\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ and $T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}$. Here $x,A$ are in m, $m$ in kg, and $k$ in N/m. Without damping, $E=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12kx^2$ stays constant. The pendulum formula $T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}$ additionally assumes a small angle. These are models with conditions, not formulas for every repeating motion.

One system, shared engineering questions. A 0.50 kg instrument on a 200 N/m spring has $T=2\pi\sqrt{0.50/200}=0.314\,\mathrm{s}$. A mechatronics engineer can predict how a heavier instrument changes the vibration; doubling mass makes the period $\sqrt2$ times longer. A computer engineer interpreting its sensor trace can check that successive same-side peaks are about 0.314 s apart and that reported time units are seconds. A chemical engineer can treat the same model as a vibrating pipe or a pump mount and ask which spring stiffness keeps the period away from the pump’s cycle time. All three compare the same physical prediction with evidence; writing Python is optional.

Before we calculate: oscillation in plain languageÖnce temel fikir

Core route: understand the restoring force → use the period and energy formulas → read the graphs → attempt P1–P4 on paper. Differential-equation solvers and their Python implementation are optional demonstrations. You do not need to write a program to solve this week's core physics.

An equilibrium position has zero net force. A displacement $x$ is signed: right is positive, left negative. The spring force $F=-kx$ points back toward equilibrium; the minus sign describes direction. Amplitude $A$ is the largest distance from equilibrium and is positive. Period $T$ is seconds per cycle; frequency $f=1/T$ is cycles per second. Angular frequency $\omega=2\pi f$ uses radians per second. They describe the same repetition in different units.

Algebra bridge. A square root applies to the whole fraction:

$$\omega^2=\frac{k}{m}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

For the period, divide both sides by $2\pi$ before squaring:

$$T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{T}{2\pi}=\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left(\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)^2=\frac{m}{k}.$$

For exponential decay, first take the natural logarithm, then divide by the decay rate:

$$\frac{A(t)}{A(0)}=e^{-\gamma t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\ln\!\left(\frac{A(t)}{A(0)}\right)=-\gamma t,$$ $$t=\frac{\ln[A(0)/A(t)]}{\gamma}.$$

What makes motion periodic? / Hareket ne zaman periyodiktir?

Periodic motion repeats after a period $T$. Frequency is $f=1/T$. Simple harmonic motion is a special periodic motion: the restoring acceleration is proportional to displacement and opposite in direction, $a=-\omega^2x$.

Today we introduce spring–mass motion and the small-angle pendulum, then connect their displacement, velocity, acceleration and energy. The pendulum period $T=2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ will follow from the restoring torque and the small-angle assumption. Damping is an optional extension inside this notebook (the section marked “Optional” below); resonance is a separate extension notebook. Neither adds another dated teaching week.

What is Simple Harmonic Motion?

Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) is a type of periodic motion where the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement and acts in the opposite direction.

Key equation 13.1
$$\;F = -kx\;$$

This leads to the differential equation:

Key equation 13.2
$$\;m\ddot{x} + kx = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0,\qquad \omega=\sqrt{k/m}\;$$

where $\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ is the angular frequency.

The General Solution

Key equation 13.3
$$\;x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\;$$
Quantity Symbol Expression Unit
Amplitude $A$ max displacement $\mathrm{m}$
Angular frequency $\omega$ $\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ $\mathrm{rad/s}$
Period $T$ $\frac{2\pi}{\omega}$ $\mathrm{s}$
Frequency $f$ $1/T = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}$ $\mathrm{Hz}$
Phase constant $\phi$ from initial conditions rad

Analogy: SHM as Circular Motion

Think of SHM as the shadow of uniform circular motion. If a ball moves in a circle of radius $A$ with angular speed $\omega$, its projection on the x-axis traces out SHM. This is why we use sine and cosine functions!

Velocity and Acceleration

$$v(t) = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi)$$

$$a(t) = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2 x(t)$$

Key observation: Acceleration is always proportional and opposite to displacement.

Read one cycle as four physical statesBir çevrimi dört durumda oku

A separate class example uses $m=1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$, $k=16\,\mathrm{N/m}$ and release from $A=0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ at rest. Choose right positive and $t=0$ at the right endpoint.

$$\omega^2=\frac{k}{m}=16\,\mathrm{s^{-2}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega=4.0\,\mathrm{rad/s},\qquad T=\frac{2\pi}{4}=1.57\,\mathrm{s}.$$

$$v_{\max}=A\omega=(0.10)(4)=0.40\,\mathrm{m/s},\qquad a=-\omega^2x=-(16\,\mathrm{s^{-2}})x.$$

Think–Pair–Explain: Does zero velocity imply zero force at the endpoint? Worked explanation: No. At $x=+0.10\,\mathrm m$, $F=-kx=-(16)(0.10)=-1.60\,\mathrm N$, so acceleration is leftward even though velocity is momentarily zero. At equilibrium the reverse is possible: zero net force and nonzero velocity.

The calculus bridge: why a sine curve keeps returningNeden sinüs eğrisi

Simple harmonic motion is the one topic in this course where the calculus is not optional decoration — it is the reason the answer is a sine wave rather than something else. You can follow the whole argument with the two readings you already have: a derivative is a slope, an integral is an area.

The physics in one line. A spring pulls back in proportion to how far you have stretched it:

$$F = -kx \quad\text{and}\quad F = ma \quad\Longrightarrow\quad ma = -kx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = -\frac{k}{m}x.$$

Now read that last equation as a sentence, remembering that acceleration is the slope of the velocity graph, which is itself the slope of the position graph:

the curvature of the position graph is proportional to the position, with the opposite sign.

What kind of curve does that? We need a function whose second slope is a negative multiple of itself. Try a few and check:

Try $x(t) =$Its curvature behaves asDoes it work?
a straight line, $ct$curvature $0$, but $x \ne 0$✗
a parabola, $ct^2$curvature constant, $x$ is not✗
exponential growth, $e^{ct}$curvature $= +c^2 x$ — same sign✗ runs away
$\cos(\omega t)$curvature $= -\omega^2 x$ — opposite sign ✅✓

Only the sine and cosine family bends back towards zero in proportion to how far out it already is. So

Key equation 13.4
$$x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad \text{and matching } -\omega^2 = -\frac{k}{m} \text{ gives } \,\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\,$$

The oscillation is not an assumption — it is forced. Nothing was put in by hand except "the restoring force is proportional to the displacement". That single property makes the motion sinusoidal, fixes the frequency, and makes the period independent of the amplitude. Any system with a restoring force proportional to displacement oscillates this way: a spring, a small-angle pendulum, a floating object bobbing, a molecule in a crystal, an LC circuit next year. That is why this week's formulas keep reappearing for the rest of your degree.

The slopes, for reference. With $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$:

$$v = \frac{dx}{dt} = -A\omega\sin(\omega t+\phi), \qquad a = \frac{dv}{dt} = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t+\phi) = -\omega^2 x.$$

Three consequences you can read straight off these, and should check against the phase-space demonstration below rather than memorise:

  • $v$ is largest where $x = 0$ (the graph of $x$ is steepest at the centre) and zero at the turning points where $x = \pm A$.
  • $a$ is largest exactly where $x$ is largest, and points back towards the centre.
  • $v$ and $x$ are a quarter-cycle out of step — which is why the phase-space plot is an ellipse.

The Simple Pendulum

A pendulum of length $L$ with a mass $m$ swings under gravity. For small angles ($\theta \ll 1$ rad):

$$\ddot{\theta} + \frac{g}{L}\theta = 0$$

This is SHM with $\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}$ and period:

Key equation 13.5
$$\;T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\quad\text{(small angle)}\;$$
Key Property Spring-Mass Simple Pendulum
Restoring force $-kx$ $-mg\sin\theta \approx -mg\theta$
Angular frequency $\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ $\sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}$
Period depends on mass $m$, spring constant $k$ length $L$, gravity $g$
Period independent of amplitude (for SHM) mass, amplitude (small angle)

Note: The small-angle approximation $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ is accurate to within 1% for $|\theta|<14^\circ$.

Worked comparison: change length, keep the modelUzunluğu değiştir

For small swings, compare lengths $L_1=0.25\,\mathrm m$ and $L_2=1.00\,\mathrm m$ at the same $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

$$T_1=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{9.81}}=1.00\,\mathrm s,\qquad \frac{T_2}{T_1}=\frac{\sqrt{L_2/g}}{\sqrt{L_1/g}} =\sqrt{\frac{L_2}{L_1}}=\sqrt4=2.$$

Thus $T_2=2.01\,\mathrm s$. The ratio method cancels the common $2\pi$ and gravity before numbers are inserted. Doubling bob mass changes neither prediction under this model.

Think–Pair–Explain: A sensor reports a return every $T/2$. Has it measured a full cycle? Worked explanation: It may be detecting each crossing of equilibrium. Adjacent crossings have opposite directions, so a full period requires returning to the same position and direction, after $T$.

Energy in SHM

The total mechanical energy in SHM is conserved (no friction):

Key equation 13.6
$$\;E_{\text{total}} = K + U = \tfrac12 kA^2 = \text{constant}\;$$

At any instant:

$$\text{KE} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\sin^2(\omega t + \phi)$$

$$\text{PE} = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\cos^2(\omega t + \phi)$$

$$\text{KE} + \text{PE} = \frac{1}{2}kA^2$$

Think of it like water sloshing between two connected tanks: when one is full, the other is empty, but the total water never changes.

Worked energy accountAdım adım enerji hesabı

Let $m=0.80\,\mathrm{kg}$, $k=80\,\mathrm{N/m}$, $A=0.10\,\mathrm m$. Find the speed at $x=0.060\,\mathrm m$ without using time.

$$E=\frac12kA^2=\frac12(80)(0.10)^2=0.400\,\mathrm J,$$ $$U=\frac12kx^2=\frac12(80)(0.060)^2=0.144\,\mathrm J,$$ $$K=E-U=0.400-0.144=0.256\,\mathrm J.$$

Now isolate the unknown by applying the same operation to both sides:

$$\frac12mv^2=K \quad\Rightarrow\quad v^2=\frac{2K}{m} =\frac{2(0.256)}{0.80}=0.640\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad |v|=0.800\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

The square root gives speed, not direction; the object can pass this position going either way. Check the account: $0.256+0.144=0.400\,\mathrm J$.

Damped Oscillations

In the real world, friction and air resistance cause oscillations to die out. The equation of motion for a damped harmonic oscillator is:

$$m\ddot{x} + b\dot{x} + kx = 0$$

where $b$ is the damping coefficient. Defining $\gamma = \frac{b}{2m}$ and $\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$:

$$\ddot{x} + 2\gamma\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0$$

Three regimes:

Regime Condition Behavior
Underdamped $\gamma < \omega_0$ Oscillates with decaying amplitude
Critically damped $\gamma = \omega_0$ Fastest return without oscillation
Overdamped $\gamma > \omega_0$ Slow exponential return

For underdamped motion:

$$x(t) = A e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi)$$

where $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}$ is the damped angular frequency.

Adjust the damping coefficient and observe how the amplitude decays. For underdamping, the dashed $\pm A e^{-\gamma t}$ curves are turning-point decay guides. The initial-condition note below explains why they are not strict bounds on the motion.

A subtle initial-condition check. For release from rest, $x(0)=A$ and $v(0)=0$, the underdamped solution is

$$x(t)=A e^{-\gamma t}\left[\cos(\omega_d t)+\frac{\gamma}{\omega_d}\sin(\omega_d t)\right],\qquad \omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}.$$

The dashed $\pm A e^{-\gamma t}$ curves in the demonstration connect successive turning points; they are not strict bounds between those points. A true sinusoidal envelope has coefficient $C=A\sqrt{1+(\gamma/\omega_d)^2}$. Türkçe: başlangıç uzaması ile kosinüsün zarf katsayısı her zaman aynı değildir. Başlangıçta hızın sıfır olması sinüs terimini gerektirir. Kritik veya aşırı sönümde bu salınımlı formu kullanmayız.

Numerical Methods: Euler vs Analytical vs odeint

The Euler method is the simplest numerical ODE solver. For a second-order ODE rewritten as two first-order equations:

$$\dot{x} = v, \qquad \dot{v} = -\omega^2 x$$

The Euler update rule is:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n + v_n \cdot \Delta t$$ $$v_{n+1} = v_n + a_n \cdot \Delta t$$

This is like predicting tomorrow's weather using only today's rate of change. Simple but accumulates errors over time.

scipy.integrate.odeint uses adaptive step-size methods (LSODA) that are far more accurate.

Example 1: Spring-Mass System

A 2.0 kg block is attached to a spring with $k = 200\,\mathrm{N/m}$. It is pulled 0.1 m from equilibrium and released from rest. Find (a) the angular frequency, (b) the period, (c) the maximum speed, (d) the maximum acceleration.

Work it through on paper. Released from rest means $A=0.10\,\mathrm{m}$.

(a) Choose the law, then divide by mass. Newton plus Hooke gives $$ma=-kx\Rightarrow a=-\frac{k}{m}x=-\omega^2x,$$ $$\omega^2=\frac{k}{m}=\frac{200\,\mathrm{N/m}}{2.0\,\mathrm{kg}}=100\,\mathrm{s^{-2}} \Rightarrow \omega=10.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}.$$ (b) Convert angular frequency into a period. One cycle is $2\pi$ radians: $$T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=\frac{2\pi}{10.0}=0.628\,\mathrm s.$$ (c) Use energy at equilibrium. Multiply both sides by $2/m$, then take the positive square root for speed: $$\frac12mv_{\max}^2=\frac12kA^2 \Rightarrow v_{\max}^2=\frac{k}{m}A^2=100(0.10)^2=1.00\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2},$$ $$v_{\max}=1.00\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$ (d) Use the largest displacement. At an endpoint, $$|a|_{\max}=\frac{kA}{m}=\frac{200(0.10)}{2.0}=10.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

Interpretation: Speed is zero at an endpoint even though acceleration is greatest there. TR: Cisim durduğu anda kuvvet sıfır olmak zorunda değildir. Aşağıdaki hücre yalnızca bu aritmetiği kontrol eder.

Worked example 13.1

Example 2: Simple Pendulum Clock

A pendulum clock has a period of exactly 2.0 s (a "seconds pendulum"). Find the required length. What happens if the clock is taken to the Moon ($g_{\text{Moon}} = 1.62\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$)?

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Hand calculation. Start with $T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}$. Divide by $2\pi$, square, then multiply by $g$: $$T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\Rightarrow\frac{T}{2\pi}=\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \Rightarrow\left(\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)^2=\frac Lg,$$ $$L=g\left(\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)^2 =9.81\left(\frac{2.0}{2\pi}\right)^2=0.994\,\mathrm m.$$ The length stays unchanged on the Moon. Substitute its gravity: $$T_{\rm Moon}=2\pi\sqrt{0.994/1.62}=4.92\ \mathrm s.$$ You can also divide the period equations to get $T_{\rm Moon}/T_{\rm Earth}=\sqrt{g_{\rm Earth}/g_{\rm Moon}}=2.46$.

Interpretation: Each swing takes about 2.46 times longer, so an unchanged pendulum clock runs slow. These formulas assume small angles, a rigid support and negligible drag. TR: Ay'da daha zayıf yerçekimi, daha yavaş geri çağırıcı etki ve daha uzun periyot demektir.

Worked example 13.2

Example 3: Damped Oscillation Energy Loss

A damped oscillator has $m = 0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$, $k = 20\,\mathrm{N/m}$, $b = 0.4\,\mathrm{N\,s/m}$. Starting from $x_0 = 0.3\,\mathrm{m}$ at rest, after how many oscillations does the energy drop to half its initial value?

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

First define “energy drops to half.” The smooth weak-damping approximation uses

$$\gamma=\frac{b}{2m}=\frac{0.4}{2(0.5)}=0.400\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad \frac{E}{E_0}\approx e^{-2\gamma t}.$$

Set the ratio equal to one half, take logarithms, and divide by $-2\gamma$:

$$\frac12=e^{-0.8t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\ln\!\left(\frac12\right)=-0.8t,$$ $$t_{1/2}\approx\frac{\ln2}{0.8}=0.866\,\mathrm s.$$

Convert this time to a number of damped periods:

$$\omega_d=\sqrt{\frac{20}{0.5}-0.4^2}=6.31\,\mathrm{rad/s},$$ $$T_d=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_d}=0.995\,\mathrm s,\qquad n\approx\frac{0.866}{0.995}=0.870\text{ cycles}.$$

Precision note. Release from rest requires both terms in the displacement:

$$x(t)=x_0e^{-\gamma t}\left[\cos(\omega_dt)+\frac{\gamma}{\omega_d}\sin(\omega_dt)\right],$$ $$v(t)=-x_0e^{-\gamma t}\frac{\omega_0^2}{\omega_d}\sin(\omega_dt).$$

Use these values in $E=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12kx^2$. Solving the actual-energy condition $E/E_0=1/2$ gives $t=0.814\,\mathrm s$, or $0.818$ cycles. The smooth approximation above is a different calculation, so its crossing time need not agree exactly.

$$\frac{dE}{dt}=-bv^2\le0.$$

Energy decreases monotonically, while its loss rate varies through each swing.

Worked example 13.3

Find a period from the static stretch

Problem. An object attached to an ideal vertical spring stretches it by $\delta=6.4\,\mathrm{cm}$ when lowered slowly to equilibrium. For this periodic-motion extension, displace it slightly from that equilibrium and release it. Find its angular frequency and period without knowing its mass. Neglect damping and use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

1. Use the resting object to learn the spring-to-mass ratio. At equilibrium, upward spring force equals downward weight: $$k\delta=mg.$$ Divide both sides by $m\delta$, keeping the same operation on both sides: $$\frac{k}{m}=\frac{g}{\delta}. \qquad \delta=6.4\,\mathrm{cm}\times\frac{1\,\mathrm m}{100\,\mathrm{cm}} =0.064\,\mathrm m.$$

2. Describe motion around equilibrium. Let $y$ be downward displacement from the equilibrium position. The actual spring extension is $\delta+y$. Therefore, $$F_{\rm net}=mg-k(\delta+y)=-ky,$$ because $mg=k\delta$. Newton's law gives $\ddot y=-(k/m)y$, the SHM equation. Its angular frequency is positive: $$\omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}=\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} =\sqrt{\frac{9.81}{0.064}}=12.4\,\mathrm{rad/s}.$$

3. Convert angular frequency to a time per cycle. A full cycle corresponds to $2\pi$ radians, so $$T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=\frac{2\pi}{12.4} =0.508\,\mathrm s\approx\boxed{0.51\,\mathrm s}.$$

Check. $g/\delta$ has units $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$, so its square root has units $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. No mass value was needed: the measured equilibrium stretch already determines $k/m$.

Önce durgun dengeyi kullanarak $k/m$ oranını buluyoruz. Salınım koordinatı yayın doğal boyundan değil, denge noktasından ölçülür. Bu yüzden ağırlık ile başlangıçtaki yay kuvveti birbirini götürür; geriye $-ky$ kalır. Periyot için $\omega$ ile çarpmak yerine $2\pi$ değerini $\omega$'ya böleriz.

Worked example 13.4

Follow a vertical spring from release to equilibrium

Problem. Use the ideal spring whose static stretch is $\delta=0.064\,\mathrm m$. The attached object starts at rest where the spring is unstretched and is released. Find its displacement function, the first time it crosses equilibrium, and its speed there. Take downward as positive and measure $y$ from equilibrium. Ignore damping and use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

1. Translate the starting position into the chosen coordinate. The unstretched position is $\delta$ above equilibrium: $$y(0)=-\delta,\qquad v(0)=0,\qquad \omega=\sqrt{g/\delta}=12.4\,\mathrm{rad/s}.$$ Write the general SHM solution as $y=C\cos(\omega t)+D\sin(\omega t)$. At $t=0$, cosine is one and sine is zero, so $C=-\delta$. Differentiating gives $v(0)=D\omega=0$; since $\omega\ne0$, $D=0$. Thus $$\boxed{y(t)=-(0.064\,\mathrm m)\cos(12.38t)},\qquad v(t)=\delta\omega\sin(\omega t).$$ Here $t$ is in seconds. The amplitude about equilibrium is $0.064\,\mathrm m$.

2. Find the first crossing. Set $y=0$. The first positive solution of $\cos(\omega t)=0$ is $\omega t=\pi/2$, not $\pi$: $$t_{\rm cross}=\frac{\pi}{2\omega} =\frac{\pi}{2(12.4)}=0.127\,\mathrm s\approx\boxed{0.13\,\mathrm s}.$$

3. Evaluate the velocity at that instant. Since $\sin(\pi/2)=1$, $$v_{\rm cross}=\delta\omega=(0.064)(12.4) =\boxed{0.79\,\mathrm{m/s}\text{ downward}}.$$

Check. Crossing equilibrium takes one-quarter of a cycle. The net force and acceleration are zero there, while speed is greatest. Maximum physical spring extension is $\delta+\delta=0.128\,\mathrm m$; that is twice the amplitude measured from equilibrium.

Worked example 13.5

Read an oscillator speed from its energy

Problem. A bank problem specifies a spring constant of $52.9\,\mathrm{N/cm}$. For this extension, attach a $2.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ cart to that ideal horizontal spring on a frictionless track. Release it from rest at amplitude $A=2.00\,\mathrm{cm}$. Find the total energy, maximum speed, and speed when it is $1.00\,\mathrm{cm}$ from equilibrium. The cart stays attached to the spring throughout the motion.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

1. Convert both the stiffness and lengths. One metre contains 100 centimetres: $$k=52.9\,\frac{\mathrm N}{\mathrm{cm}} \times\frac{100\,\mathrm{cm}}{1\,\mathrm m} =5290\,\mathrm{N/m},\quad A=0.0200\,\mathrm m,\quad x=0.0100\,\mathrm m.$$ The stiffness number increases on conversion; multiplying the length by $0.01$ does not mean doing the same to a quantity measured per centimetre.

2. Start where all energy is elastic. At release, $v=0$, so $$E=\frac12kA^2=\frac12(5290)(0.0200)^2 =\boxed{1.06\,\mathrm J}.$$ At any position, conservation gives $$\frac12mv^2+\frac12kx^2=\frac12kA^2.$$ Subtract $kx^2/2$ and multiply by $2/m$: $$v^2=\frac{k}{m}(A^2-x^2),\qquad |v|=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}(A^2-x^2)}.$$

3. Insert the requested positions. At equilibrium, $x=0$: $$v_{\max}=A\sqrt{k/m}=0.0200\sqrt{5290/2.00} =\boxed{1.03\,\mathrm{m/s}}.$$ At $x=0.0100\,\mathrm m$: $$|v|=\sqrt{\frac{5290}{2.00}[(0.0200)^2-(0.0100)^2]} =\boxed{0.891\,\mathrm{m/s}}.$$

Check. At half the amplitude, elastic energy is one-quarter of the total, so kinetic energy is three-quarters. Speed is therefore $\sqrt{3/4}$ of its maximum, not one-half. Energy gives speed; the direction depends on which part of the cycle the cart is following.

Worked example 13.6

Halve the amplitude and compare energy, speed and period

Problem. The bank asks how reducing a spring's compression changes a dart's height. Use its spring-energy idea in an attached-cart model: an ideal spring has $k=200\,\mathrm{N/m}$ and a cart has $m=0.500\,\mathrm{kg}$. Compare releases from rest at amplitudes $A_1=0.100\,\mathrm m$ and $A_2=0.0500\,\mathrm m$ on a frictionless horizontal track. Find each total energy, maximum speed and period.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

1. Compare energies before inserting numbers. With zero speed at an endpoint, $$E=\frac12kA^2,\qquad \frac{E_2}{E_1}=\frac{\frac12kA_2^2}{\frac12kA_1^2} =\left(\frac{A_2}{A_1}\right)^2=\left(\frac12\right)^2=\frac14.$$ The common $k/2$ cancels. Numerically, $$E_1=\tfrac12(200)(0.100)^2=\boxed{1.00\,\mathrm J},\qquad E_2=\tfrac12(200)(0.0500)^2=\boxed{0.250\,\mathrm J}.$$

2. Convert the available energy into maximum speed. At equilibrium, $mv_{\max}^2/2=kA^2/2$. Cancel $1/2$, divide by $m$, then take the positive square root for speed: $$v_{\max}=A\sqrt{k/m}. \quad \sqrt{k/m}=\sqrt{200/0.500}=20.0\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.$$ Hence $v_{\max,1}=\boxed{2.00\,\mathrm{m/s}}$ and $v_{\max,2}=\boxed{1.00\,\mathrm{m/s}}$.

3. Compare the time per cycle. For an ideal linear spring, $$T=2\pi\sqrt{m/k}=2\pi\sqrt{0.500/200} =\boxed{0.314\,\mathrm s}\quad\text{for both releases}.$$ Amplitude does not appear in this expression. The smaller motion covers a shorter distance each cycle at proportionally smaller speeds.

Check. Halving amplitude gives one-quarter of the energy and one-half of the maximum speed, while the period stays the same. These statements assume the spring follows Hooke's law over both displacement ranges and damping is negligible.

Worked example 13.7

Estimate the period of a long swing

Problem. A bank problem uses a swing with chains $L=5.10\,\mathrm m$ long. For this periodic-motion extension, treat the rider and seat as a point mass at that distance below the pivot, with light inextensible chains. Release the swing from $5.0^\circ$ from vertical. Estimate its period and frequency using the small-angle model. Would doubling the rider's mass change the predicted period? Use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and neglect resistance.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

1. Identify the restoring torque. With angular displacement $\theta$ measured from the downward vertical, gravity produces a torque toward equilibrium: $$\tau=-mgL\sin\theta.$$ For a point mass, $I=mL^2$. Substitute into $I\ddot\theta=\tau$ and divide both sides by $mL^2$: $$mL^2\ddot\theta=-mgL\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot\theta=-\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta.$$ Mass cancels because both the restoring torque and rotational inertia are proportional to mass.

2. State the approximation. Convert the amplitude to radians: $$5.0^\circ\times\frac{\pi}{180^\circ}=0.0873\,\mathrm{rad}.$$ For small angles, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$, giving $\ddot\theta\approx-(g/L)\theta$. Comparing with SHM identifies $$\omega\approx\sqrt{g/L}=\sqrt{9.81/5.10} =1.39\,\mathrm{rad/s}.$$

3. Calculate time and cycles per second. $$T\approx2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} =2\pi\sqrt{\frac{5.10}{9.81}}=\boxed{4.53\,\mathrm s},$$ $$f=\frac1T=\frac1{4.53}=\boxed{0.221\,\mathrm{Hz}}.$$ Doubling mass leaves this model's period unchanged.

Check. $L/g$ has units $\mathrm{s^2}$, so its square root is a time. The period is one complete out-and-back cycle; the time from one extreme to the opposite extreme is about $T/2=2.27\,\mathrm s$. A longer chain gives a longer period because $T$ is proportional to $\sqrt L$.
