Before calculating: translate motion into an equation

Core route: sketch → choose a positive direction → list knowns with units → choose an equation → solve by hand → interpret the sign. Python is an optional way to inspect a graph, not a course prerequisite.

Symbol Meaning SI unit
$x_0$, $x$ initial and final position $\mathrm m$
$\Delta x=x-x_0$ signed displacement $\mathrm m$
$v_0$, $v$ initial and final velocity $\mathrm{m/s}$
$a$ change of velocity per second $\mathrm{m/s^2}$
$t$ elapsed time of the phase $\mathrm s$

Choose by the missing quantity. If time is not needed, use $v^2=v_0^2+2a\Delta x$. If final velocity is not needed, use $\Delta x=v_0t+\frac12at^2$. These equations require constant acceleration during the interval.

Algebra slowly: isolate elapsed time by applying the same operation to both sides:

$$\begin{aligned} v&=v_0+at,\\ v-v_0&=at &&\text{subtract }v_0,\\ t&=\frac{v-v_0}{a} &&\text{divide by }a\ne0. \end{aligned}$$

For a car slowing from $12\,\mathrm{m/s}$ to rest with $a=-3\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$,

$$t=\frac{0-12\,\mathrm{m/s}}{-3\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}=4\,\mathrm s.$$

Both numerator and denominator are negative, so the elapsed time is positive. The unit quotient is seconds.

Signs come from axes. With upward positive, free-fall acceleration is $a_y=-9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ both while rising and while falling. At the highest point, velocity is momentarily zero but acceleration is still downward. Speed is the nonnegative magnitude of velocity.

Square roots: $t^2=9\,\mathrm{s^2}$ has mathematical roots $\pm3\,\mathrm s$. A time measured after release uses $+3\,\mathrm s$. A quadratic height equation can instead have two valid positive roots, for the upward and downward crossings.

Review stop: explain why $v=-5\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $a=-2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ describe increasing speed in the negative direction. After one second, $v=-5+(-2)(1)=-7\,\mathrm{m/s}$: speed rose from $5$ to $7\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Acceleration with a minus sign does not always mean slowing down.

How this week is workedBu hafta nasıl çalışılır

Every problem, worked example and experiment in this course follows the same six steps. Step 4 is the one added in September 2026, and it is the one that separates a physicist from a calculator operator.

draw → choose a law → rearrange to the symbolic answer → check a limiting case → substitute with units → interpret

  1. Symbolic answer first. Isolate the unknown in symbols, before any number appears.
  2. One limiting case. Push a parameter to a value whose answer you already know — a mass to zero, an angle to $90^\circ$, a friction coefficient to zero — and confirm the formula behaves sensibly. If it does not, the algebra is wrong and you have just caught it for free, before wasting arithmetic on it.
  3. Then numbers, with units carried through every line.

Why bother: the symbolic route is less arithmetic, not more, and it is where the marks are. A correct symbolic answer with a slip in the final multiplication is nearly full credit; a correct number with no derivation is not.

Position, Displacement & Velocity

Key Definitions

Choose an origin and a positive direction before assigning signs. Here $x_i$ and $x_f$ are the positions at the beginning and end of an interval of duration $\Delta t>0$.

Quantity Symbol Definition SI unit
Position $x$ Location relative to the chosen origin $\mathrm{m}$
Displacement $\Delta x$ $x_f-x_i$; signed change of position $\mathrm{m}$
Distance $d$ Total path length; nonnegative $\mathrm{m}$
Average velocity $\bar v$ $\displaystyle\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$ $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$
Instantaneous velocity $v$ $\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}$; slope of the position graph $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$
Average acceleration $\bar a$ $\displaystyle\frac{v_f-v_i}{\Delta t}$ $\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$
Instantaneous acceleration $a$ $\displaystyle\frac{dv}{dt}$; slope of the velocity graph $\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$

Important Distinctions

  • Displacement vs Distance: Walking $3\,\mathrm m$ east and $3\,\mathrm m$ west gives $\Delta x=0$ and $d=6\,\mathrm m$. No motion gives zero distance, so distance is not always positive.
  • Speed vs Velocity: Speed is $|v|\ge0$; velocity includes direction.
  • Velocity vs Acceleration: Velocity describes change of position; acceleration describes change of velocity. A negative acceleration does not by itself mean slowing down.

Small example — one trip, two averages. A robot moves from $x=1\,\mathrm m$ to $x=5\,\mathrm m$, then back to $x=3\,\mathrm m$, in $6\,\mathrm s$ total.

$$ \begin{aligned} \Delta x&=3-1=2\,\mathrm m, & d&=(5-1)+(5-3)=6\,\mathrm m,\\ \bar v&=\frac{2\,\mathrm m}{6\,\mathrm s}=0.333\,\mathrm{m/s}, & \text{average speed}&=\frac{6\,\mathrm m}{6\,\mathrm s}=1.00\,\mathrm{m/s}. \end{aligned} $$

Analogy

A GPS locates the car. A speedometer reports speed, so a direction is needed to obtain velocity. Acceleration describes the velocity change; whether an accelerator or brake makes its component positive depends on the chosen axis and the direction of travel.

The Kinematic Equations (Constant Acceleration)

Use these equations only during an interval with constant acceleration. Let $t$ be time since that interval began, $x_0,v_0$ its initial values, and $x,v$ its final values.

Key equation 2.1
$$v=v_0+at$$
Key equation 2.2
$$x=x_0+v_0t+\tfrac12at^2$$
Key equation 2.3
$$v^2=v_0^2+2a(x-x_0)$$
Key equation 2.4
$$x-x_0=\tfrac12(v_0+v)t$$

There are only four, and the fourth follows from the first three. Each one is missing a different quantity, which is what makes the table below the fastest way to choose: find the quantity the problem does not give you and does not ask for, and use the equation that leaves it out.

Sign Convention

With right/up positive, $v>0$ means right/up and $v<0$ means left/down. When $v$ and $a$ have the same sign, speed increases; opposite signs mean speed decreases until a possible reversal. At $v=0$, inspect what acceleration does immediately afterward.

The calculus bridge: velocity is a slopeHız bir eğimdir

You have three kinematic equations and they only work when the acceleration is constant. Behind them sits one idea that always works, and you can read it straight off the graphs you have already drawn.

Velocity is the slope of the position graph.

$$v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \quad \text{over an interval}, \qquad v = \frac{dx}{dt} \quad \text{at an instant.}$$

The second expression is the first one with the interval shrunk until it is a single point. That is all $d/dt$ means: the slope of the graph right here, rather than the average slope across a stretch. Read the symbol as the sentence "the slope of $x$ against $t$" and it stops being intimidating.

Acceleration is the slope of the velocity graph, by exactly the same reasoning:

$$a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \text{the slope of } v \text{ against } t.$$

And going the other way, displacement is the area under the velocity graph:

$$\Delta x = \int v\,dt = \text{the signed area under } v \text{ against } t.$$

This is why the $v$–$t$ area demonstration in this notebook matters more than it looks: that area is the displacement, and area below the axis counts as motion backwards.

You wantRead this off the graphIn symbols
Velocity at an instantslope of the $x$–$t$ curve$dx/dt$
Acceleration at an instantslope of the $v$–$t$ curve$dv/dt$
Displacement over an intervalarea under the $v$–$t$ curve$\int v\,dt$
Change in velocityarea under the $a$–$t$ curve$\int a\,dt$

Where the familiar equations come from. If $a$ is constant, the $a$–$t$ graph is a flat line, so the area under it is just $a t$ — giving $v = v_0 + at$. The $v$–$t$ graph is then a straight line, and the area under a straight line is a rectangle plus a triangle: $v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2$, which is exactly $\Delta x$. The equations you are memorising are two areas. That is worth knowing, because when the acceleration is not constant the equations break but the areas and slopes do not.

Free Fall Simulator

Choose upward positive. In the ideal model, gravity supplies the constant acceleration $a_y=-g$, where $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ is a positive magnitude.

$$y=y_0+v_0t-\frac12gt^2,\qquad v_y=v_0-gt.$$

For an upward launch ($v_0>0$), $v_y=0$ at $t_{\rm up}=\frac{v_0}{g}$. The rise above launch is $\Delta h=\frac{v_0^2}{2g}$; the absolute maximum height is $y_{\max}=y_0+\Delta h$. Upward and downward flight times are equal only when comparing the same starting and ending heights.

Small check: With $v_0=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s}$, after $1\,\mathrm s$ the ball has $v_y=9.81-9.81(1)=0$, while $a_y=-9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ still. Its rise is $\frac12(9.81)(1^2)=4.91\,\mathrm m$.

Braking Distance Calculator

Separate the reaction interval (constant speed in this model) from braking (constant negative acceleration). Let $b=|a|>0$ be the braking deceleration magnitude.

$$d_{\rm reaction}=v_0t_r,\qquad d_{\rm brake}=\frac{v_0^2}{2b},\qquad d_{\rm stop}=d_{\rm reaction}+d_{\rm brake}.$$

Illustrative road condition Deceleration magnitude used for a classroom model
Dry asphalt $7$–$10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$
Wet asphalt $4$–$7\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$
Icy road $1$–$3\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$

These are illustrative ranges, not guarantees for a particular vehicle or road. Use the values supplied in each question.

Worked comparison: For $v_0=10\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $t_r=1\,\mathrm s$, and $b=5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, reaction distance is $10\,\mathrm m$ and braking distance is $100/10=10\,\mathrm m$: total $20\,\mathrm m$. Doubling speed gives $20+400/10=60\,\mathrm m$, not $40\,\mathrm m$.

Worked Examples

Worked example 2.1

Constant Acceleration

Problem: A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at $3\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

  • (a) What is its speed after $5\,\mathrm s$?
  • (b) How far has it traveled in $5\,\mathrm s$?
  • (c) How far has it traveled when it reaches $30\,\mathrm{m/s}$?

Hand calculation

Choose forward positive. The initial velocity is $v_0=0$ and acceleration is $a=+3\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Let $\Delta x$ denote displacement from the starting point.

(a) Substitute into the velocity equation:

$$\begin{aligned} v&=v_0+at,\\ &=0+(3\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(5\,\mathrm s)=15\,\mathrm{m/s}. \end{aligned}$$

(b) Square time before multiplying:

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta x&=v_0t+\frac12at^2,\\ &=0+\frac12(3\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(5\,\mathrm s)^2,\\ &=\frac12(3)(25)\,\mathrm m=37.5\,\mathrm m. \end{aligned}$$

The unit cancellation is $(\mathrm{m/s^2})(\mathrm{s^2})=\mathrm m$.

(c) Choose the equation without time. This is a new final event, when $v=30\,\mathrm{m/s}$:

$$\begin{aligned} v^2&=v_0^2+2a\Delta x,\\ (30\,\mathrm{m/s})^2&=0+2(3\,\mathrm{m/s^2})\Delta x,\\ \Delta x&=\frac{900\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}{6\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}=150\,\mathrm m. \end{aligned}$$

The last step divides both sides by $6\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. This event occurs at $t=v/a=10\,\mathrm s$.

Check: Doubling elapsed time from rest at constant acceleration quadruples distance: $150=4(37.5)$.

(c) şıkkında süre artık beş saniye değildir. Yeni son hıza ait denklemi kur; sonra iki tarafı aynı ivme terimine böl. Yolun birimi metre kalmalıdır.

Model answer / Örnek yanıt — open after trying

$$\begin{aligned} v_y(1\,\mathrm s)&=9.81-9.81(1)=0\,\mathrm{m/s},\\ a_y&=-9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}. \end{aligned}$$

For the return, use $y=0$ and SI units:

$$0=9.81t-4.905t^2=t(9.81-4.905t).$$

One factor must vanish: $t=0$ is launch, while $9.81-4.905t=0$ gives $t=2.0\,\mathrm s$, the return. Türkçe: Çarpımın sıfır olması iki kökü açıklar. Tepe anında hız sıfır olsa da aşağı yönlü ivme devam eder.

Your explanation / Açıklaman: write or discuss your reasoning in words, with an equation or sketch where useful.

Worked example 2.2

Free Fall

Problem: A ball is thrown straight up from the ground with $v_0=20\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

  • (a) How high does it go?
  • (b) How long until it returns to the ground?
  • (c) What is its velocity when it hits the ground?

Hand calculation with one sign convention

Take upward positive, $y_0=0$, $v_0=+20\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $a_y=-g=-9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Neglect air resistance. Let $H$ be the rise above the launch point.

(a) At the peak, set velocity to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} 0&=v_0^2-2gH,\\ 2gH&=v_0^2 &&\text{add }2gH\text{ to both sides},\\ H&=\frac{v_0^2}{2g} &&\text{divide both sides by }2g,\\ &=\frac{(20\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2(9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2})}=20.4\,\mathrm m. \end{aligned}$$

(b) Return means the height is zero again. Factor the time equation:

$$\begin{aligned} 0&=v_0t-\frac12gt^2=t\left(v_0-\frac12gt\right),\\ t&=0\quad\text{or}\quad v_0-\frac12gt=0,\\ t_{\rm return}&=\frac{2v_0}{g}=\frac{2(20\,\mathrm{m/s})}{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}=4.08\,\mathrm s. \end{aligned}$$

The zero root is the launch event; the positive root is the return event.

(c) Use the signed velocity equation:

$$v_y=v_0-gt_{\rm return}=20-9.81(4.08)\approx-20.0\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

The negative sign means downward. Impact speed is the magnitude, $20.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

Check: $t_{\rm peak}=\frac{v_0}{g}=2.04\,\mathrm s$ is half the full flight because launch and landing heights match. Gravity remains downward throughout.

TÜRKÇE

Tepe noktasında $v_y=0$ koyuyoruz, $a_y=0$ değil. Çarpanlara ayırma iki farklı olayı görünür yapar: atış ve yere dönüş. Son hızdaki eksi, süratin değil yönün bilgisidir.

Worked example 2.3

Braking Problem

Problem: A driver going $100\,\mathrm{km/h}$ sees a child $80\,\mathrm m$ ahead. Reaction time is $1\,\mathrm s$, and braking deceleration has magnitude $8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Does the car stop in time?

Hand calculation in two phases

Choose forward positive. First convert the initial speed; the conversion ratios cancel kilometres and hours:

$$v_0=100\,\frac{\mathrm{km}}{\mathrm h}\frac{1000\,\mathrm m}{1\,\mathrm{km}}\frac{1\,\mathrm h}{3600\,\mathrm s}=27.8\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

Reaction phase: Let $d_r$ be the distance before braking. Acceleration is zero during $t_r=1.0\,\mathrm s$:

$$d_r=v_0t_r=(27.8\,\mathrm{m/s})(1.0\,\mathrm s)=27.8\,\mathrm m.$$

Braking phase: Let $d_b$ be braking distance. Here $a=-8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and final $v=0$:

$$\begin{aligned} 0&=v_0^2+2ad_b,\\ 0&=772\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}-(16\,\mathrm{m/s^2})d_b,\\ (16\,\mathrm{m/s^2})d_b&=772\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2},\\ d_b&=\frac{772\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}{16\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}=48.2\,\mathrm m. \end{aligned}$$

Add the braking term to both sides, then divide by its positive coefficient. Finally add the two distances:

$$\begin{aligned} d_{\rm stop}&=d_r+d_b=27.8+48.2=76.0\,\mathrm m,\\ \text{remaining distance}&=80-76.0=4.00\,\mathrm m. \end{aligned}$$

Result: The car stops about $4.00\,\mathrm m$ before the child within the stated model. Keep unrounded values in the calculation.

Check: Count reaction distance as well as braking distance. The small positive margin depends on the supplied assumptions.

TÜRKÇE

Tepki sırasında araç ilerlemeye devam eder. Önce iki yol parçasını ayrı hesapla, sonra topla. Negatif ivmeyi denkleme işaretiyle yaz; pozitif durma yolunu bulmak için terimleri iki tarafa tutarlı taşı.

Worked example 2.4

Average velocity and average speed describe different things

Problem. A walker travels south at $2.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$ for $60.0\,\mathrm{min}$, then turns and walks $3000\,\mathrm{m}$ north in $25.0\,\mathrm{min}$. Find her average velocity and average speed over the complete trip. Include the direction of the average velocity.

Picture and principle. Draw one north–south axis with north positive. The first displacement is negative; the return displacement is positive. Average velocity uses signed displacement, while average speed uses total path length:

$$\bar v=\frac{\Delta x_1+\Delta x_2}{t_1+t_2},\qquad\overline{\text{speed}}=\frac{d_1+d_2}{t_1+t_2}.$$

Convert both durations before combining them with speeds in metres per second:

$$t_1=60.0(60)=3600\,\mathrm{s},\quad t_2=25.0(60)=1500\,\mathrm{s},\quad T=5100\,\mathrm{s}.$$

For the first leg, rearranging $v=\Delta x/t$ gives $\Delta x=vt$. Hence

$$\Delta x_1=(-2.00\,\mathrm{m/s})(3600\,\mathrm{s})=-7200\,\mathrm{m},$$

$$\Delta x_2=+3000\,\mathrm{m},\qquad\Delta x=-4200\,\mathrm{m}.$$

The complete-trip results are

$$\bar v=\frac{-4200\,\mathrm{m}}{5100\,\mathrm{s}}=-0.824\ldots\,\mathrm{m/s},$$

$$\overline{\text{speed}}=\frac{7200+3000}{5100}\,\mathrm{m/s}=\boxed{2.00\,\mathrm{m/s}}.$$

The average velocity is therefore $\boxed{0.824\,\mathrm{m/s}\text{ south}}$. The negative sign simply records the chosen north-positive axis.

Meaning and check. She finishes south of her starting point even though she spends part of the trip moving north. Her return-leg speed is $3000/1500=2.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$, so a total average speed of $2.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$ is consistent. Also, average speed must be at least the magnitude of average velocity: $2.00>0.824$.

TÜRKÇE

Ortalama hızın payında yer değiştirme vardır; dönüşte yapılan yol önceki yer değiştirmenin bir bölümünü götürür. Ortalama süratte ise iki yol uzunluğu da pozitiftir. “Güneye doğru” bilgisini ya negatif işaretle ya yön sözcüğüyle göster; ikisini fiziksel olarak çelişen biçimde kullanma.

Worked example 2.5

A dragster: final speed is twice the average speed

Problem. A dragster starts from rest and covers a quarter mile in $6.70\,\mathrm{s}$ with constant acceleration. Find its velocity at the finish line, reporting its speed in miles per hour. Take the forward track direction as positive and use $1\,\mathrm{mi}=1609.344\,\mathrm{m}$.

Picture and principle. On a velocity–time graph, the motion is a straight line from zero to $v_f$. The area under this line is a triangle and equals displacement:

$$d=\frac12tv_f.$$

The same relation follows from $d=\tfrac12at^2$ and $v_f=at$. Rearrange before substituting: multiply by two and divide by the elapsed time,

$$v_f=\frac{2d}{t}.$$

Convert the quarter-mile distance to SI units:

$$d=\frac14(1609.344\,\mathrm{m})=402\,\mathrm{m}.$$

Then calculate the finishing velocity:

$$v_f=\frac{2(402\,\mathrm{m})}{6.70\,\mathrm{s}}=120\ldots\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

To express the speed in miles per hour, cancel metres and seconds using conversion fractions:

$$v_f=120\ldots\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}\left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{mi}}{1609.344\,\mathrm{m}}\right)\left(\frac{3600\,\mathrm{s}}{1\,\mathrm{h}}\right)=\boxed{269\,\mathrm{mi/h}}.$$

Its velocity is forward along the track. The positive constant acceleration inferred from the same data is $a=v_f/t\approx17.9\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Meaning and check. The calculation $d/t$ alone gives the average speed, about $134\,\mathrm{mi/h}$, not the finishing speed. Starting from rest and increasing velocity linearly makes the average exactly half the final value. Reinsert the inferred acceleration into $d=\tfrac12at^2$ to recover $402\,\mathrm{m}$.

TÜRKÇE

Yol bölü zaman her durumda son hızı vermez. Bu harekette hız sıfırdan düzenli arttığı için ortalama hız $(0+v_f)/2$ olur. Denklemdeki $1/2$ katsayısı bu nedenle vardır. Önce $v_f$'yi yalnız bırakmak, sayıları yanlış sırayla çarpma riskini azaltır.

Worked example 2.6

Stopping distance includes the reaction interval

Problem. A car approaches a stop sign at $40.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The sign is $200\,\mathrm{m}$ away when the driver notices it. The driver takes $0.200\,\mathrm{s}$ to apply the brakes. Find the magnitude of the constant acceleration needed during braking to stop exactly at the sign. Assume the speed remains constant during the reaction interval.

Picture and principle. Split the road into two adjacent lengths: reaction distance followed by braking distance. During the first interval there is no braking acceleration. During the second, acceleration points opposite to the forward velocity. Choose forward as positive.

The reaction distance follows from constant-speed motion:

$$d_r=v_0t_r=(40.0\,\mathrm{m/s})(0.200\,\mathrm{s})=8.00\,\mathrm{m}.$$

Subtract it from the total available distance:

$$d_b=D-d_r=200-8.00=192\,\mathrm{m}.$$

Braking time is unknown, so use the constant-acceleration relation that eliminates time:

$$v_f^2=v_0^2+2ad_b.$$

Subtract $v_0^2$ and divide by $2d_b$ to isolate the signed acceleration:

$$a=\frac{v_f^2-v_0^2}{2d_b}=\frac{0^2-(40.0\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2(192\,\mathrm{m})}=-4.17\ldots\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

The requested magnitude is $\boxed{|a|=4.17\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}$. The minus sign in $a$ indicates that the acceleration opposes the chosen positive direction.

Meaning and check. From $v_f=v_0+at$, the braking time is $t_b=-v_0/a=9.60\,\mathrm{s}$. Its average speed is $20.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, so braking covers $20.0(9.60)=192\,\mathrm{m}$. Adding the reaction distance recovers $200\,\mathrm{m}$. Ignoring reaction would incorrectly give a smaller required deceleration, $4.00\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

TÜRKÇE

Tepki süresinde araç durmaz; fren uygulanana kadar aynı hızla yol alır. Önce bu yolu toplam mesafeden çıkarmalıyız. İvmenin büyüklüğü pozitif istenir, fakat hareket denkleminde yönü göstermek için $a$ negatiftir. Eksi işaretini hesap bitmeden silme.

Worked example 2.7

A delayed pursuit uses two different elapsed times

Problem. A car moving at a constant $30.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ passes a stationary police car. The police driver waits $1.00\,\mathrm{s}$, then accelerates uniformly from rest. Determine the acceleration needed to catch the moving car at a point $300\,\mathrm{m}$ from the police car's starting position.

Picture and principle. Put $x=0$ where the cars first pass and $x=300\,\mathrm{m}$ at the meeting point. Start a common clock at the passing event. Both vehicles must occupy the same position when they meet, but they have not been moving for the same length of time.

The first car moves uniformly, so its travel time to the meeting point satisfies

$$D=vt_{\mathrm{meet}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad t_{\mathrm{meet}}=\frac{D}{v}=\frac{300\,\mathrm{m}}{30.0\,\mathrm{m/s}}=10.0\,\mathrm{s}.$$

The police car accelerates only after the delay $\tau$:

$$t_p=t_{\mathrm{meet}}-\tau=10.0-1.00=9.00\,\mathrm{s}.$$

Starting from rest gives $D=\tfrac12at_p^2$. Multiply by two and divide by the square of the police travel time:

$$a=\frac{2D}{t_p^2}=\frac{2D}{(D/v-\tau)^2}=\frac{2(300\,\mathrm{m})}{(9.00\,\mathrm{s})^2}=\boxed{7.41\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}.$$

Meaning and check. The police car reaches the meeting point with speed $at_p\approx66.7\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Its average speed over acceleration is half that value, $33.3\,\mathrm{m/s}$; multiplying by $9.00\,\mathrm{s}$ gives $300\,\mathrm{m}$. Meanwhile the other car travels $30.0(10.0)=300\,\mathrm{m}$. Equal meeting positions do not require equal instantaneous velocities.

TÜRKÇE

İki araç için saat aynı anda başlatılabilir; fakat hareket süreleri farklıdır. Polis aracı ilk bir saniye beklediğinden ivmelenme süresi $10-1=9$ saniyedir. Denklemde on saniyeyi kullanmak, bekleme aralığında da hızlandığını varsaymak olur. Süre farkını sayısal işlemden önce açıkça yaz.

Worked example 2.8

One height can correspond to two times

Problem. A ball is hit straight upward at $30.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find its time to maximum height, maximum rise, and both times when it is $25.0\,\mathrm{m}$ above the launch point. Ignore air resistance and use $g=9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Picture and principle. Put $y=0$ at launch and choose upward positive. Gravity gives constant acceleration $a_y=-g$ throughout the flight, including the highest point. Thus

$$v_y=v_0-gt,\qquad y=v_0t-\frac12gt^2.$$

At the top only the vertical velocity is zero. Solving $0=v_0-gt_{\mathrm{top}}$ gives

$$t_{\mathrm{top}}=\frac{v_0}{g}=\frac{30.0}{9.80}\,\mathrm{s}=3.06\,\mathrm{s}.$$

Using $0=v_0^2-2gh$ and isolating height,

$$h=\frac{v_0^2}{2g}=\frac{(30.0\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2(9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2})}=\boxed{45.9\,\mathrm{m}}.$$

For $y=25.0\,\mathrm{m}$, move all terms to one side:

$$\frac12gt^2-v_0t+y=0.$$

The quadratic formula, with coefficients $g/2$, $-v_0$, and $y$, becomes

$$t=\frac{v_0\pm\sqrt{v_0^2-2gy}}{g}=\frac{30.0\pm\sqrt{30.0^2-2(9.80)(25.0)}}{9.80}\,\mathrm{s}.$$

Therefore $\boxed{t_1=0.995\,\mathrm{s}}$ on the way up and $\boxed{t_2=5.13\,\mathrm{s}}$ on the way down.

Meaning and check. The target height lies below the maximum, so two crossings are physically possible. The earlier root occurs before $3.06\,\mathrm{s}$ and the later root after it. Their midpoint equals $t_{\mathrm{top}}$, reflecting the up–down symmetry. Both roots satisfy the same height equation; neither should be discarded.

The positive discriminant also confirms two distinct crossings. A target higher than the maximum would give no real crossing time.

TÜRKÇE

İkinci dereceden denklemdeki iki kök burada iki farklı fiziksel olaydır. Top önce yükselirken, sonra inerken aynı yükseklikten geçer. Tepe noktasında hız sıfır olsa bile ivme sıfır değildir: yerçekimi aşağıya doğru etkisini sürdürür. Kökleri yorumlamak için tepeye çıkış süresiyle karşılaştır.

2.4Exit checkÇıkış kontrolü

Explain why a thrown ball has zero velocity but nonzero acceleration at its highest point.

Write one sentence naming the physical principle and one line of algebra you can now explain. TR: Sonuca nasıl ulaştığını bir cümleyle anlat; emin olmadığın ilk adımı işaretle.

Model responses / Örnek yanıtlar — open after trying

Entry: $36\,\mathrm{km/h}\times1000/3600=10\,\mathrm{m/s}$. A leftward velocity is negative if right is positive; a sign needs a stated axis.

Exit: At the highest point of a vertical throw, the instantaneous velocity is zero while gravity still gives $a_y=-9.81$ m/s². The velocity changes through zero; the ball does not stay there.

Visual prediction: With $v_0=5$ m/s and $a=-1$ m/s², set $v=5-t=0$, so the turning time is 5 s. Before that turn, $\Delta x=5(5)-5^2/2=12.5$ m and distance is also 12.5 m. If motion continues to 10 s, displacement is $5(10)-10^2/2=0$ but distance is $12.5+12.5=25$ m.
