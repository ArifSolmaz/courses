3.1Before you startBaşlamadan önce

Retrieval question: Resolve a velocity into horizontal and vertical components; recall $a_y=-g$ when upward is positive.

🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this session you will be able to:

  1. Decompose a velocity vector into horizontal and vertical components
  2. Derive and apply the equations of projectile motion (range, maximum height, time of flight)
  3. Predict where a projectile lands given initial speed and launch angle
  4. Compare ideal (vacuum) trajectories with air-resistance trajectories
  5. Optimize launch angle for maximum range and for hitting a specific target
  6. State Newton’s three laws and draw a free-body diagram showing every force on one chosen object, then write $\sum F_x=ma_x$ and $\sum F_y=ma_y$

Before calculating: two motions sharing one clock

Physics first: understand the diagram and solve the equations on paper. Use a trajectory plot to test your prediction; programming the plot is optional.

For ideal projectile motion, ignore air resistance, choose $+x$ horizontal and $+y$ upward, and write: $$\begin{aligned} v_{0x}&=v_0\cos\theta, & v_{0y}&=v_0\sin\theta,\\ x&=x_0+v_{0x}t, & y&=y_0+v_{0y}t-\frac12gt^2. \end{aligned}$$ Horizontal acceleration is zero; vertical acceleration is $-g$. The two directions have different equations but the same elapsed time.

A small algebra bridge: for a horizontal launch from height $h$, $0=h-\frac12gt^2$. Add $\frac12gt^2$ to both sides, multiply by 2, divide by $g$, then take the positive square root: $$\frac12gt^2=h\ \Rightarrow\ gt^2=2h\ \Rightarrow\ t^2=\frac{2h}{g}\ \Rightarrow\ t=\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}.$$ The units under the root are $\mathrm m/(\mathrm{m/s^2})=\mathrm{s^2}$, so the result is in seconds. Only after finding $t$ use $x=v_{0x}t$.

When the shortcut works: $T=\frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}$ and $R=\frac{v_0^2\sin(2\theta)}{g}$ assume landing and launch at the same height. For a cliff or hillside, use the position equations and the actual landing condition. At the peak, $v_y=0$; usually $v_x\ne0$.

Two roots: in $At^2+Bt+C=0$, keep the signs inside $t=\dfrac{-B\pm\sqrt{B^2-4AC}}{2A}$. Test each root against the time interval and the physical event; do not automatically discard the smaller root.

Review stop: two objects released horizontally at different speeds from the same height land simultaneously if both have zero initial vertical velocity. Explain this from the vertical equation before viewing a plot.

Theory: Projectile Motion

What is a Projectile?

A projectile moves under gravity after release; the launch push has ended. Our first model neglects air resistance and assumes constant $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Take $+x$ horizontal, $+y$ upward, and measure $\theta$ above $+x$. The initial point $(x_0,y_0)$ need not be the ground.

One object, two component equations, one shared time $t$:

Quantity Horizontal motion Vertical motion
Initial position $x_0$ $y_0$
Initial velocity component $v_{0x}=v_0\cos\theta$ $v_{0y}=v_0\sin\theta$
Acceleration $a_x=0$ $a_y=-g$
Velocity at time $t$ $v_x=v_{0x}$ $v_y=v_{0y}-gt$
Position at that same $t$ $x=x_0+v_{0x}t$ $y=y_0+v_{0y}t-\frac12gt^2$

$$\boxed{\;x=x_0+v_{0x}t,\qquad v_x=v_{0x}=v_0\cos\theta\;}$$

$$\boxed{\;y=y_0+v_{0y}t-\tfrac12gt^2,\qquad v_y=v_{0y}-gt\;}$$

Positions have units $\mathrm m$, velocities $\mathrm{m/s}$, accelerations $\mathrm{m/s^2}$, and time $\mathrm s$. Do not calculate different horizontal and vertical flight times for one landing event.

Small example — build both coordinates. Release a ball from $(0,2\,\mathrm m)$ with $v_0=10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at $30^\circ$. After $0.50\,\mathrm s$:

$$ \begin{aligned} v_{0x}&=10\cos30^\circ=8.66\,\mathrm{m/s}, &v_{0y}&=10\sin30^\circ=5.00\,\mathrm{m/s},\\ x&=0+(8.66)(0.50)=4.33\,\mathrm m,\\ y&=2+(5.00)(0.50)-\tfrac12(9.81)(0.50)^2\\ &=2+2.50-1.23=3.27\,\mathrm m,\\ v_y&=5.00-9.81(0.50)=0.095\,\mathrm{m/s}. \end{aligned} $$

Square time before multiplying the gravity term. The ball is almost at its apex but is still rising slightly. At the apex $v_y=0$, while $v_x=8.66\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $a_y=-g$ remain: neither the full velocity nor acceleration is zero.

Shortcuts and their conditions

Result: Time to apex

  • Formula: $t_{\rm apex}=\frac{v_{0y}}{g}$

  • Required interpretation: Upward initial component, $v_{0y}>0$

Result: Rise above launch

  • Formula: $\Delta H=\frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}$

  • Required interpretation: Not absolute height; $y_{\max}=y_0+\Delta H$

Result: Full flight time

  • Formula: $T=\frac{2v_{0y}}{g}$

  • Required interpretation: Landing at $y=y_0$, upward launch

Result: Horizontal range

  • Formula: $R=\frac{v_0^2\sin(2\theta)}{g}$

  • Required interpretation: Same launch/landing height, no drag

$$\boxed{\;R=\frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\quad\text{(same launch and landing height, no drag)}\;}$$

For a cliff or an inclined landing surface, solve the actual vertical landing equation for $t$ first, then use $x=x_0+v_{0x}t$.

Key Insight — Independence of Motion

A dropped ball and a horizontally launched ball released together from the same height have the same $y_0$, $v_{0y}=0$, and $a_y=-g$. Their vertical equations are identical, so they land together on level ground in this ideal model. Horizontal speed changes where they land, not when.

Think–Pair–Explain: From $h=4.91\,\mathrm m$, one ball is dropped and one is launched horizontally at $3\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Predict their flight times and horizontal distances. Model reasoning: $0=h-\frac12gt^2$ gives $t=\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}=1.00\,\mathrm s$ for both. Distances are zero and $(3)(1)=3.00\,\mathrm m$.

Trajectory Equation (Eliminating Time)

For $v_{0x}\ne0$, subtract $x_0$ and divide by $v_{0x}$:

$$ \begin{aligned} t&=\frac{x-x_0}{v_{0x}},\\ y-y_0&=v_{0y}\frac{x-x_0}{v_{0x}}-\frac g2\left(\frac{x-x_0}{v_{0x}}\right)^2\\ &=(x-x_0)\tan\theta-\frac{g(x-x_0)^2}{2v_0^2\cos^2\theta}. \end{aligned} $$

Setting $x_0=y_0=0$ gives the usual parabola equation. A vertical launch has $v_{0x}=0$; division by it is invalid, so keep the time equations instead.

Components are two descriptions of one motion

Picture. Draw one position arrow and project it onto horizontal and vertical axes. Both components belong to the same particle at the same time. Resolving a vector changes its description, not its physical motion. With uniform downward gravity and negligible drag, $a_x=0$ and $a_y=-g$; the equations separate because the force has no horizontal component.

A part leaving a horizontal conveyor keeps its horizontal velocity while gaining downward velocity. A camera tracking the part sees both coordinates evolve with one clock. A liquid parcel leaving a horizontal outlet follows the same model only if drag and interactions with the rest of the stream are negligible.

Predict. Release two identical parts from the same height with the same initial vertical velocity, but different horizontal speeds. Sketch their vertical positions at equal time intervals. Which lands first?

Explain before using a range formula

They land together under the stated assumptions. Their vertical equations and landing height are identical, so their flight times match. The faster part travels farther horizontally. Equal flight time need not hold if initial vertical velocity, landing height or aerodynamic forces differ.

Complementary Angles

For the same initial speed, equal launch/landing heights, and no drag, complementary launch angles $\theta$ and $90^\circ-\theta$ give equal ranges. The range depends on $\sin(2\theta)$:

$$\begin{aligned} \sin\bigl(2(90^\circ-\theta)\bigr)&=\sin(180^\circ-2\theta),\\ &=\sin(2\theta). \end{aligned}$$

The first line distributes the factor of two. The second uses the sine identity $\sin(180^\circ-\beta)=\sin\beta$.

For $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$, the range factors are $\sin60^\circ$ and $\sin120^\circ$, both $\sqrt3/2$. The higher launch has a longer flight time and a higher apex despite equal range.

Interactive: Vacuum vs Air Resistance

Real projectiles experience drag opposite their velocity relative to the air. This demonstration assumes still air and uses the quadratic model

$$\mathbf F_{\rm drag}=-\frac12C_d\rho A\lVert\mathbf v\rVert\mathbf v.$$

Here $C_d$ is a dimensionless drag coefficient, $\rho$ is air density in $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $A$ is cross-sectional area in $\mathrm{m^2}$, and $\mathbf v$ is velocity in $\mathrm{m/s}$. Its magnitude is $v=\lVert\mathbf v\rVert$. The product $v\mathbf v$ makes force magnitude proportional to speed squared; the minus sign reverses its direction.

Divide the force by mass $m$ and define the combined parameter $b$:

$$\begin{aligned} b&=\frac{C_d\rho A}{2m}, & [b]&=\mathrm{m^{-1}},\\ a_x&=-bv\,v_x, & a_y&=-g-bv\,v_y. \end{aligned}$$

Drag couples the two velocity components through $v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$. Therefore the simple constant-acceleration range formulas no longer apply. At zero velocity the drag force is zero; gravity remains.

Model answer / Örnek yanıt — open after trying

For fixed initial speed and equal launch/landing heights, $R$ depends on $\sin(2\theta)$, $H$ on $\sin^2\theta$, and $T$ on $\sin\theta$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{R_{60}}{R_{30}}&=\frac{\sin120^\circ}{\sin60^\circ}=1,\\ \frac{H_{60}}{H_{30}}&=\frac{(\sqrt3/2)^2}{(1/2)^2}=3,\\ \frac{T_{60}}{T_{30}}&=\frac{\sqrt3/2}{1/2}=\sqrt3. \end{aligned}$$

Subscripts identify the launch angle in degrees. The $60^\circ$ launch is higher and stays aloft longer, but its range is unchanged. Türkçe: Oran kurunca aynı başlangıç sürati ve yerçekimi çarpanları sadeleşir. Aynı menzil, aynı süre veya yükseklik demek değildir.

  • (c) Range

Hand calculation

Assume landing at launch height and no air resistance. Choose horizontal forward and vertical up positive. First split the initial velocity:

$$\begin{aligned} v_{0x}&=v_0\cos\theta=(20)\cos37^\circ=16.0\,\mathrm{m/s},\\ v_{0y}&=v_0\sin\theta=(20)\sin37^\circ=12.0\,\mathrm{m/s}. \end{aligned}$$

(a) Find the shared flight time $T$. Landing has zero vertical displacement:

$$\begin{aligned} 0&=v_{0y}t-\frac12gt^2=t\left(v_{0y}-\frac12gt\right),\\ T&=\frac{2v_{0y}}{g}=\frac{2(12.0\,\mathrm{m/s})}{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}=2.45\,\mathrm s. \end{aligned}$$

Factoring gives the launch root $t=0$ and the later landing root $T$; use the latter.

(b) Find rise $H$ by setting $v_y=0$ at the peak:

$$\begin{aligned} 0&=v_{0y}^2-2gH,\\ H&=\frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}=\frac{(12.0\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{19.6\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}=7.38\,\mathrm m. \end{aligned}$$

Add $2gH$ to both sides, then divide by $2g$.

(c) Use that same flight time in the horizontal equation:

$$R=v_{0x}T=(16.0\,\mathrm{m/s})(2.45\,\mathrm s)=39.2\,\mathrm m.$$

Check: Equal launch/landing heights give equal up/down times. At the peak the ball still moves horizontally at $16.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

TÜRKÇE

Önce iki hız bileşenini ayır. Düşey denklem süreyi verir; menzilde aynı süreyi kullanırız. Tepe için yalnızca düşey hız sıfırlanır. Ara sayıları hesap makinesinde tam hassasiyetle sakla.

Worked example 3.1

Example 2: Cliff Launch

A ball is thrown horizontally from a cliff $45\,\mathrm m$ high with $v_0=15\,\mathrm{m/s}$. How far from the base does it land?

Hand calculation

Set ground level to $y=0$ and choose upward positive. The release conditions are $y_0=45\,\mathrm m$, $v_{0y}=0$, and $v_{0x}=15\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Set $x_0=0$ at the cliff.

The vertical equation gives time:

$$\begin{aligned} 0&=y_0-\frac12gt^2,\\ \frac12gt^2&=y_0 &&\text{add the gravity term to both sides},\\ t^2&=\frac{2y_0}{g} &&\text{multiply by }2/g,\\ t&=\sqrt{\frac{2(45\,\mathrm m)}{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}}=3.03\,\mathrm s. \end{aligned}$$

Choose the positive square root because we want time after release. Now use the horizontal equation:

$$x=v_{0x}t=(15\,\mathrm{m/s})(3.03\,\mathrm s)=45.4\,\mathrm m.$$

Result: The ball lands about $45.4\,\mathrm m$ from the cliff base.

Check: Horizontal speed does not appear in the fall-time equation. Doubling it doubles the range while leaving fall time unchanged.

Newton’s laws: a first look / Newton yasalarına ilk bakış

The second half of this week introduces the three laws and one force diagram. Friction models, inclines and connected bodies follow next week.

The Three Laws

Use an inertial reference frame and a constant mass. A free-body diagram contains forces acting on the selected object, not forces it exerts on others.

Law: 1st (Inertia)

  • Meaning: Zero net force means constant velocity, including rest

  • Equation: $\sum\vec F=0\Rightarrow\vec a=0$

Law: 2nd (net force and acceleration)

  • Meaning: Net force determines acceleration

  • Equation: $\sum\vec F=m\vec a$

Law: 3rd (Action-Reaction)

  • Meaning: Interaction partners act on different objects

  • Equation: $\vec F_{A\to B}=-\vec F_{B\to A}$

Key equation 3.1
$$\;\sum\vec F=m\vec a\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum F_x=ma_x,\;\; \sum F_y=ma_y\;$$

Small example: A $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ cart has a $7\,\mathrm N$ force right and a $3\,\mathrm N$ force left. With right positive:

$$\sum F_x=7-3=4\,\mathrm N,\qquad a_x=\frac{4\,\mathrm N}{2\,\mathrm{kg}}=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

If both forces become $3\,\mathrm N$, the net force and acceleration are zero. A cart already moving right then continues right at constant velocity; zero net force does not mean zero velocity.

TÜRKÇE

$ma$'ya yalnızca büyük kuvveti değil, işaretli toplamı eşitliyoruz. Etki–tepki kuvvetleri farklı cisimlere etki eder; aynı cismin diyagramında birbirlerini götüren bir çift değildir.

A first force diagram: which interactions act on one object?

For today's Newton introduction, practise weight, support and an applied force. Draw only forces on the chosen object. A third-law partner acts on the other object.

A 2.0 kg cart on a horizontal frictionless track is pulled right by 6.0 N. Choose right and up positive. Vertical forces balance: $N-mg=0$, so $N=2.0(9.81)=19.6$ N. Horizontally, $$\sum F_x=ma_x\Rightarrow6.0=2.0a_x\Rightarrow a_x=3.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$ From rest, after 2.0 s its velocity is $v_x=0+(3.0)(2.0)=6.0$ m/s. The force on the cart causes its acceleration. The opposite force on the hand or rope does not cancel it on the cart's diagram.

Check: With the pull removed, the ideal cart keeps its velocity; zero force means zero acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity. TR: Bu hafta önce tek cismin kuvvetlerini ayırıyoruz. Sürtünmenin türleri ve eğik düzlem uygulamaları gelecek haftanın konusudur.

A force sum belongs to one selected system

A free-body diagram is an inventory of interactions on the selected object. Each force needs an agent: Earth, floor, cable or another object. Vector addition combines those forces; dividing their resultant by positive mass gives the acceleration vector. Velocity need not point along that resultant.

Picture. Isolate a lift passenger. Draw Earth pulling the passenger down and the floor pushing the passenger up. The passenger's push on the floor belongs on the floor's diagram. It is the reaction partner of the normal force, not an extra force on the passenger.

Predict. A lift travels upward at constant speed. Is the upward normal force greater than the passenger's weight?

Worked example 3.2

Place a chute under a moving belt

Problem. Parts leave a horizontal belt and fall $0.450\,\mathrm m$ into a chute $0.600\,\mathrm m$ forward. Neglect drag. Choose the belt speed and determine the impact direction so the chute can be aligned with the incoming motion.

Picture and principle. Draw a horizontal launch with downward drop $h$ and forward range $R$. Vertical motion gives $t=\sqrt{2h/g}$. Then $u=R/t$, $v_y=-gt$, and $\tan\phi=|v_y|/u=2h/R$, with $\phi$ below horizontal.

Limiting check. A larger drop at the same range requires a slower belt and a steeper impact direction.

Numbers and units. $$t=\sqrt{\frac{2(0.450\,\mathrm m)}{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}}=0.303\,\mathrm s,\quad u=1.98\,\mathrm{m/s},\quad \phi=\tan^{-1}(0.900/0.600)=56.3^\circ.$$

Interpret. The chute should follow the velocity at impact, not the launch direction. The idealised model omits bouncing and air drag.

Yatay hız sabittir, düşey hız yerçekimiyle değişir. Oluğun yönü, parçanın banttan çıkış yönüne değil çarpma anındaki hızına göre seçilir.

Worked example 3.3

Will the part clear the enclosure?

Problem. A test launcher at height $0.800\,\mathrm m$ gives a part velocity components $v_x=2.40\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_y=3.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$. An enclosure has a horizontal ceiling at $1.40\,\mathrm m$. Find the maximum height and the forward position where the part lands on the floor. Neglect drag.

Picture and principle. Sketch the launch, apex, ceiling and floor. At the apex $v_y=0$, so $y_{\max}=y_0+v_{y0}^2/(2g)$. For the floor use the positive root $t=[v_{y0}+\sqrt{v_{y0}^2+2gy_0}]/g$, then $x=v_xt$.

Limiting check. With zero upward launch component, the maximum height is simply the launch height.

Numbers and units. $$y_{\max}=0.800\,\mathrm m+\frac{(3.20\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2(9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2})}=1.32\,\mathrm m.$$ The flight lasts $0.845\,\mathrm s$ and lands at $2.03\,\mathrm m$.

Interpret. The part clears the ceiling by $0.0781\,\mathrm m$ in this point-particle model. Its actual dimensions would reduce that clearance.

TÜRKÇE

Worked example 3.4

Which gate catches the rising part?

Problem. A marker launches with components $(1.50,2.80)\,\mathrm{m/s}$ from a reference height. Two proposed optical gates are at that height plus $0.250\,\mathrm m$. Calculate their possible horizontal positions and identify the gate that sees the marker while rising.

Picture and principle. Draw a trajectory crossing the gate height twice. Solve $\Delta h=v_{y0}t-gt^2/2$: $t_\pm=[v_{y0}\pm\sqrt{v_{y0}^2-2g\Delta h}]/g$. The smaller root is on ascent; $x_\pm=v_xt_\pm$.

Limiting check. When $\Delta h=v_{y0}^2/(2g)$, the roots coincide at the apex. A larger height gives no real crossing.

Numbers and units. $$t_-=0.111\,\mathrm s,\qquad t_+=0.460\,\mathrm s.$$ Place the rising gate at $x_-=0.166\,\mathrm m$; the falling crossing is at $x_+=0.690\,\mathrm m$.

Interpret. Two algebraically valid roots represent different events. State which event the instrument should detect before choosing a root.

TÜRKÇE

İki kök iki farklı geçiş anıdır. Yükselişte ölçüm isteniyorsa küçük zaman kökü seçilir.

Worked example 3.5

Recover a launcher setting from a video

Problem. A part travels $1.10\,\mathrm m$ horizontally in $0.400\,\mathrm s$ and arrives $0.120\,\mathrm m$ below its launch point. A label says “horizontal launch.” Use the video measurements to test that label and infer the actual launch speed and angle. Neglect drag.

Picture and principle. Draw displacement components with upward positive. From $R=v_xt$ and $\Delta y=v_{y0}t-gt^2/2$, obtain $v_x=R/t$ and $v_{y0}=(\Delta y+gt^2/2)/t$. Then $u=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_{y0}^2}$ and $\theta=\tan^{-1}(v_{y0}/v_x)$.

Limiting check. A truly horizontal launch would have $\Delta y=-gt^2/2$ and inferred $v_{y0}=0$.

Numbers and units. $$v_x=2.75\,\mathrm{m/s},\quad v_{y0}=\frac{-0.120\,\mathrm m+(9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(0.400\,\mathrm s)^2/2}{0.400\,\mathrm s}=1.66\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$ Thus $u=3.21\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $\theta=31.1^\circ$ above horizontal.

Interpret. The label is inconsistent with the measurements: the part initially rises even though it finishes below its launch point.

Worked example 3.6

Choose a brake rating

Problem. A $12.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ inspection carriage moves at $1.40\,\mathrm{m/s}$. It must stop within $0.160\,\mathrm m$ after a brake engages. Compare constant horizontal brake-force options of $60.0\,\mathrm N$ and $90.0\,\mathrm N$, neglecting other horizontal forces.

Picture and principle. Draw the brake force opposite velocity; take forward as positive. Combining $0=v^2+2ad$ with $F_x=ma$ gives a required magnitude $F_{\min}=mv^2/(2d)$. For a candidate brake, $d=mv^2/(2F)$.

Limiting check. Doubling the force halves the stopping distance, whereas doubling speed requires four times the force for the same distance.

Numbers and units. $$F_{\min}=\frac{(12.0\,\mathrm{kg})(1.40\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2(0.160\,\mathrm m)}=73.5\,\mathrm N.$$ The two stopping distances are $0.196\,\mathrm m$ and $0.131\,\mathrm m$, respectively.

Interpret. Only the $90.0\,\mathrm N$ option meets this idealised requirement. This model begins at brake engagement; it contains no controller delay.

Worked example 3.7

Check the sign in a reversal log

Problem. A $0.750\,\mathrm{kg}$ actuator carriage changes velocity from $+0.400\,\mathrm{m/s}$ to $-0.250\,\mathrm{m/s}$ in $0.0800\,\mathrm s$. Its software reports a net force of $-1.41\,\mathrm N$. Test that value and identify the likely calculation error.

Picture and principle. Draw both velocity arrows on one signed axis. The average net force is $F_{\rm avg}=m(v_f-v_i)/\Delta t$. If acceleration is uniform, this is also the instantaneous net force throughout the interval.

Limiting check. If the initial and final velocities are identical, the average net force is zero. Equal speeds in opposite directions do not satisfy that condition.

Numbers and units. $$F_{\rm avg}=\frac{(0.750\,\mathrm{kg})[-0.250-(+0.400)]\,\mathrm{m/s}}{0.0800\,\mathrm s}=-6.09\,\mathrm N.$$ Using the unsigned speed difference gives $(0.750)(0.250-0.400)/0.0800=-1.41\,\mathrm N$.

Interpret. The software appears to subtract speeds instead of signed velocities. The large reversal is hidden by that mistake.

TÜRKÇE

Yön değişiminde hızların işaretleri korunmalıdır. Süratlerin farkı, hız değişiminin büyüklüğünü vermez.

Worked example 3.8

Find the allowable lift load

Problem. A vertical actuator can exert at most $160\,\mathrm N$ upward. Its moving platform has mass $3.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ and must accelerate upward at $0.800\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. What maximum payload mass can it lift in this model? Neglect other resistance.

Picture and principle. Draw the platform and payload as one system of total mass $M=m_p+m_L$. The upward force and downward weight obey $T-Mg=Ma$. Rearranging gives $m_L=T/(g+a)-m_p$.

Limiting check. At zero acceleration the force only supports weight. Requiring positive upward acceleration reduces the allowed payload.

Numbers and units. $$m_L=\frac{160\,\mathrm N}{(9.81+0.800)\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}-3.00\,\mathrm{kg}=12.1\,\mathrm{kg}.$$

Interpret. The force rating must support the platform itself as well as the payload. Ignoring gravity would grossly overestimate capacity.

Worked example 3.9

Detect extra mass from travel time

Problem. A horizontal air-bearing stage is driven from rest by a constant net force of $2.40\,\mathrm N$. It travels $0.600\,\mathrm m$ in $1.50\,\mathrm s$. Its expected moving mass is $3.00\,\mathrm{kg}$. Infer the actual moving mass and decide whether an extra fixture could explain the timing.

Picture and principle. Draw constant acceleration along the rail, with vertical forces balanced. Combining $x=at^2/2$ and $F=ma$ gives $m=Ft^2/(2x)$. The force is the net horizontal force, as stated.

Limiting check. At fixed force and distance, a longer travel time implies a larger mass; doubling time implies four times the mass.

Numbers and units. $$m=\frac{(2.40\,\mathrm N)(1.50\,\mathrm s)^2}{2(0.600\,\mathrm m)}=4.50\,\mathrm{kg}.$$ The inferred extra mass is $4.50-3.00=1.50\,\mathrm{kg}$.

Interpret. An added fixture is consistent with the data, but timing alone cannot distinguish extra mass from a smaller actual net force.

Worked example 3.10

Pull without unloading the guide

Problem. A smooth horizontal guide carries an $8.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ carriage. A cable pulls at $30.0^\circ$ above horizontal. The guide must retain a normal force of at least $50.0\,\mathrm N$. Find the largest permitted cable tension and the corresponding horizontal acceleration.

Picture and principle. Draw tension upward and forward, weight downward, and normal force upward. With no vertical acceleration, $N=mg-T\sin\theta$. Thus $T_{\max}=(mg-N_{\min})/\sin\theta$ and $a_x=T_{\max}\cos\theta/m$.

Limiting check. As the required normal force approaches the full weight, permitted upward cable force tends to zero. A horizontal cable does not unload the guide.

Numbers and units. $$T_{\max}=\frac{(8.00)(9.81)-50.0}{\sin30.0^\circ}\,\mathrm N=57.0\,\mathrm N,\quad a_x=\frac{(56.96\,\mathrm N)\cos30.0^\circ}{8.00\,\mathrm{kg}}=6.17\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

Interpret. Increasing tension helps horizontal acceleration but reduces contact force. Both requirements must be checked together.
