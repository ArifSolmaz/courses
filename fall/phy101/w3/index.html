3.1Before you startBaşlamadan önce

Retrieval question: Resolve a velocity into horizontal and vertical components; recall $a_y=-g$ when upward is positive.

🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this session you will be able to:

  1. Decompose a velocity vector into horizontal and vertical components
  2. Derive and apply the equations of projectile motion (range, maximum height, time of flight)
  3. Predict where a projectile lands given initial speed and launch angle
  4. Compare ideal (vacuum) trajectories with air-resistance trajectories
  5. Optimize launch angle for maximum range and for hitting a specific target
  6. State Newton’s three laws and draw a free-body diagram showing every force on one chosen object, then write $\sum F_x=ma_x$ and $\sum F_y=ma_y$

Before calculating: two motions sharing one clock

Physics first: understand the diagram and solve the equations on paper. Use a trajectory plot to test your prediction; programming the plot is optional.

For ideal projectile motion, ignore air resistance, choose $+x$ horizontal and $+y$ upward, and write: $$\begin{aligned} v_{0x}&=v_0\cos\theta, & v_{0y}&=v_0\sin\theta,\\ x&=x_0+v_{0x}t, & y&=y_0+v_{0y}t-\frac12gt^2. \end{aligned}$$ Horizontal acceleration is zero; vertical acceleration is $-g$. The two directions have different equations but the same elapsed time.

A small algebra bridge: for a horizontal launch from height $h$, $0=h-\frac12gt^2$. Add $\frac12gt^2$ to both sides, multiply by 2, divide by $g$, then take the positive square root: $$\frac12gt^2=h\ \Rightarrow\ gt^2=2h\ \Rightarrow\ t^2=\frac{2h}{g}\ \Rightarrow\ t=\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}.$$ The units under the root are $\mathrm m/(\mathrm{m/s^2})=\mathrm{s^2}$, so the result is in seconds. Only after finding $t$ use $x=v_{0x}t$.

When the shortcut works: $T=\frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}$ and $R=\frac{v_0^2\sin(2\theta)}{g}$ assume landing and launch at the same height. For a cliff or hillside, use the position equations and the actual landing condition. At the peak, $v_y=0$; usually $v_x\ne0$.

Two roots: in $At^2+Bt+C=0$, keep the signs inside $t=\dfrac{-B\pm\sqrt{B^2-4AC}}{2A}$. Test each root against the time interval and the physical event; do not automatically discard the smaller root.

Review stop: two objects released horizontally at different speeds from the same height land simultaneously if both have zero initial vertical velocity. Explain this from the vertical equation before viewing a plot.

How this week is workedBu hafta nasıl çalışılır

Every problem, worked example and experiment in this course follows the same six steps. Step 4 is the one added in September 2026, and it is the one that separates a physicist from a calculator operator.

draw → choose a law → rearrange to the symbolic answer → check a limiting case → substitute with units → interpret

  1. Symbolic answer first. Isolate the unknown in symbols, before any number appears.
  2. One limiting case. Push a parameter to a value whose answer you already know — a mass to zero, an angle to $90^\circ$, a friction coefficient to zero — and confirm the formula behaves sensibly. If it does not, the algebra is wrong and you have just caught it for free, before wasting arithmetic on it.
  3. Then numbers, with units carried through every line.

Why bother: the symbolic route is less arithmetic, not more, and it is where the marks are. A correct symbolic answer with a slip in the final multiplication is nearly full credit; a correct number with no derivation is not.

Theory: Projectile Motion

What is a Projectile?

A projectile moves under gravity after release; the launch push has ended. Our first model neglects air resistance and assumes constant $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Take $+x$ horizontal, $+y$ upward, and measure $\theta$ above $+x$. The initial point $(x_0,y_0)$ need not be the ground.

One object, two component equations, one shared time $t$:

Quantity Horizontal motion Vertical motion
Initial position $x_0$ $y_0$
Initial velocity component $v_{0x}=v_0\cos\theta$ $v_{0y}=v_0\sin\theta$
Acceleration $a_x=0$ $a_y=-g$
Velocity at time $t$ $v_x=v_{0x}$ $v_y=v_{0y}-gt$
Position at that same $t$ $x=x_0+v_{0x}t$ $y=y_0+v_{0y}t-\frac12gt^2$

$$\boxed{\;x=x_0+v_{0x}t,\qquad v_x=v_{0x}=v_0\cos\theta\;}$$

$$\boxed{\;y=y_0+v_{0y}t-\tfrac12gt^2,\qquad v_y=v_{0y}-gt\;}$$

Positions have units $\mathrm m$, velocities $\mathrm{m/s}$, accelerations $\mathrm{m/s^2}$, and time $\mathrm s$. Do not calculate different horizontal and vertical flight times for one landing event.

Small example — build both coordinates. Release a ball from $(0,2\,\mathrm m)$ with $v_0=10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at $30^\circ$. After $0.50\,\mathrm s$:

$$ \begin{aligned} v_{0x}&=10\cos30^\circ=8.66\,\mathrm{m/s}, &v_{0y}&=10\sin30^\circ=5.00\,\mathrm{m/s},\\ x&=0+(8.66)(0.50)=4.33\,\mathrm m,\\ y&=2+(5.00)(0.50)-\tfrac12(9.81)(0.50)^2\\ &=2+2.50-1.23=3.27\,\mathrm m,\\ v_y&=5.00-9.81(0.50)=0.095\,\mathrm{m/s}. \end{aligned} $$

Square time before multiplying the gravity term. The ball is almost at its apex but is still rising slightly. At the apex $v_y=0$, while $v_x=8.66\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $a_y=-g$ remain: neither the full velocity nor acceleration is zero.

Shortcuts and their conditions

Result Formula Required interpretation
Time to apex $t_{\rm apex}=\frac{v_{0y}}{g}$ Upward initial component, $v_{0y}>0$
Rise above launch $\Delta H=\frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}$ Not absolute height; $y_{\max}=y_0+\Delta H$
Full flight time $T=\frac{2v_{0y}}{g}$ Landing at $y=y_0$, upward launch
Horizontal range $R=\frac{v_0^2\sin(2\theta)}{g}$ Same launch/landing height, no drag

$$\boxed{\;R=\frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\quad\text{(same launch and landing height, no drag)}\;}$$

For a cliff or an inclined landing surface, solve the actual vertical landing equation for $t$ first, then use $x=x_0+v_{0x}t$.

Key Insight — Independence of Motion

A dropped ball and a horizontally launched ball released together from the same height have the same $y_0$, $v_{0y}=0$, and $a_y=-g$. Their vertical equations are identical, so they land together on level ground in this ideal model. Horizontal speed changes where they land, not when.

Think–Pair–Explain: From $h=4.91\,\mathrm m$, one ball is dropped and one is launched horizontally at $3\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Predict their flight times and horizontal distances. Model reasoning: $0=h-\frac12gt^2$ gives $t=\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}=1.00\,\mathrm s$ for both. Distances are zero and $(3)(1)=3.00\,\mathrm m$.

Trajectory Equation (Eliminating Time)

For $v_{0x}\ne0$, subtract $x_0$ and divide by $v_{0x}$:

$$ \begin{aligned} t&=\frac{x-x_0}{v_{0x}},\\ y-y_0&=v_{0y}\frac{x-x_0}{v_{0x}}-\frac g2\left(\frac{x-x_0}{v_{0x}}\right)^2\\ &=(x-x_0)\tan\theta-\frac{g(x-x_0)^2}{2v_0^2\cos^2\theta}. \end{aligned} $$

Setting $x_0=y_0=0$ gives the usual parabola equation. A vertical launch has $v_{0x}=0$; division by it is invalid, so keep the time equations instead.

Complementary Angles

For the same initial speed, equal launch/landing heights, and no drag, complementary launch angles $\theta$ and $90^\circ-\theta$ give equal ranges. The range depends on $\sin(2\theta)$:

$$\begin{aligned} \sin\bigl(2(90^\circ-\theta)\bigr)&=\sin(180^\circ-2\theta),\\ &=\sin(2\theta). \end{aligned}$$

The first line distributes the factor of two. The second uses the sine identity $\sin(180^\circ-\beta)=\sin\beta$.

For $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$, the range factors are $\sin60^\circ$ and $\sin120^\circ$, both $\sqrt3/2$. The higher launch has a longer flight time and a higher apex despite equal range.

Interactive: Vacuum vs Air Resistance

Real projectiles experience drag opposite their velocity relative to the air. This demonstration assumes still air and uses the quadratic model

$$\mathbf F_{\rm drag}=-\frac12C_d\rho A\lVert\mathbf v\rVert\mathbf v.$$

Here $C_d$ is a dimensionless drag coefficient, $\rho$ is air density in $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $A$ is cross-sectional area in $\mathrm{m^2}$, and $\mathbf v$ is velocity in $\mathrm{m/s}$. Its magnitude is $v=\lVert\mathbf v\rVert$. The product $v\mathbf v$ makes force magnitude proportional to speed squared; the minus sign reverses its direction.

Divide the force by mass $m$ and define the combined parameter $b$:

$$\begin{aligned} b&=\frac{C_d\rho A}{2m}, & [b]&=\mathrm{m^{-1}},\\ a_x&=-bv\,v_x, & a_y&=-g-bv\,v_y. \end{aligned}$$

Drag couples the two velocity components through $v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$. Therefore the simple constant-acceleration range formulas no longer apply. At zero velocity the drag force is zero; gravity remains.

For fixed initial speed and equal launch/landing heights, $R$ depends on $\sin(2\theta)$, $H$ on $\sin^2\theta$, and $T$ on $\sin\theta$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{R_{60}}{R_{30}}&=\frac{\sin120^\circ}{\sin60^\circ}=1,\\ \frac{H_{60}}{H_{30}}&=\frac{(\sqrt3/2)^2}{(1/2)^2}=3,\\ \frac{T_{60}}{T_{30}}&=\frac{\sqrt3/2}{1/2}=\sqrt3. \end{aligned}$$

Worked Examples

Example 1: Football Kick

A football is kicked with $v_0=20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at $\theta=37^\circ$. Find:

  • (a) Time of flight
  • (b) Maximum height
  • (c) Range

Hand calculation

Assume landing at launch height and no air resistance. Choose horizontal forward and vertical up positive. First split the initial velocity:

$$\begin{aligned} v_{0x}&=v_0\cos\theta=(20)\cos37^\circ=16.0\,\mathrm{m/s},\\ v_{0y}&=v_0\sin\theta=(20)\sin37^\circ=12.0\,\mathrm{m/s}. \end{aligned}$$

(a) Find the shared flight time $T$. Landing has zero vertical displacement:

$$\begin{aligned} 0&=v_{0y}t-\frac12gt^2=t\left(v_{0y}-\frac12gt\right),\\ T&=\frac{2v_{0y}}{g}=\frac{2(12.0\,\mathrm{m/s})}{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}=2.45\,\mathrm s. \end{aligned}$$

Factoring gives the launch root $t=0$ and the later landing root $T$; use the latter.

(b) Find rise $H$ by setting $v_y=0$ at the peak:

$$\begin{aligned} 0&=v_{0y}^2-2gH,\\ H&=\frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}=\frac{(12.0\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{19.6\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}=7.38\,\mathrm m. \end{aligned}$$

Add $2gH$ to both sides, then divide by $2g$.

(c) Use that same flight time in the horizontal equation:

$$R=v_{0x}T=(16.0\,\mathrm{m/s})(2.45\,\mathrm s)=39.2\,\mathrm m.$$

Check: Equal launch/landing heights give equal up/down times. At the peak the ball still moves horizontally at $16.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

Worked example 3.1

Example 2: Cliff Launch

A ball is thrown horizontally from a cliff $45\,\mathrm m$ high with $v_0=15\,\mathrm{m/s}$. How far from the base does it land?

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Hand calculation

Set ground level to $y=0$ and choose upward positive. The release conditions are $y_0=45\,\mathrm m$, $v_{0y}=0$, and $v_{0x}=15\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Set $x_0=0$ at the cliff.

The vertical equation gives time:

$$\begin{aligned} 0&=y_0-\frac12gt^2,\\ \frac12gt^2&=y_0 &&\text{add the gravity term to both sides},\\ t^2&=\frac{2y_0}{g} &&\text{multiply by }2/g,\\ t&=\sqrt{\frac{2(45\,\mathrm m)}{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}}=3.03\,\mathrm s. \end{aligned}$$

Choose the positive square root because we want time after release. Now use the horizontal equation:

$$x=v_{0x}t=(15\,\mathrm{m/s})(3.03\,\mathrm s)=45.4\,\mathrm m.$$

Result: The ball lands about $45.4\,\mathrm m$ from the cliff base.

Check: Horizontal speed does not appear in the fall-time equation. Doubling it doubles the range while leaving fall time unchanged.

Newton’s laws: a first look / Newton yasalarına ilk bakış

The second half of this week introduces the three laws and one force diagram. Friction models, inclines and connected bodies follow next week.

The Three Laws

Use an inertial reference frame and a constant mass. A free-body diagram contains forces acting on the selected object, not forces it exerts on others.

Law Meaning Equation
1st (Inertia) Zero net force means constant velocity, including rest $\sum\vec F=0\Rightarrow\vec a=0$
2nd (net force and acceleration) Net force determines acceleration $\sum\vec F=m\vec a$
3rd (Action-Reaction) Interaction partners act on different objects $\vec F_{A\to B}=-\vec F_{B\to A}$
Key equation 3.1
$$\;\sum\vec F=m\vec a\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum F_x=ma_x,\;\; \sum F_y=ma_y\;$$

Small example: A $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ cart has a $7\,\mathrm N$ force right and a $3\,\mathrm N$ force left. With right positive:

$$\sum F_x=7-3=4\,\mathrm N,\qquad a_x=\frac{4\,\mathrm N}{2\,\mathrm{kg}}=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

If both forces become $3\,\mathrm N$, the net force and acceleration are zero. A cart already moving right then continues right at constant velocity; zero net force does not mean zero velocity.

TÜRKÇE

$ma$'ya yalnızca büyük kuvveti değil, işaretli toplamı eşitliyoruz. Etki–tepki kuvvetleri farklı cisimlere etki eder; aynı cismin diyagramında birbirlerini götüren bir çift değildir.

A first force diagram: which interactions act on one object?

For today's Newton introduction, practise weight, support and an applied force. Draw only forces on the chosen object. A third-law partner acts on the other object.

A 2.0 kg cart on a horizontal frictionless track is pulled right by 6.0 N. Choose right and up positive. Vertical forces balance: $N-mg=0$, so $N=2.0(9.81)=19.6$ N. Horizontally, $$\sum F_x=ma_x\Rightarrow6.0=2.0a_x\Rightarrow a_x=3.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.$$ From rest, after 2.0 s its velocity is $v_x=0+(3.0)(2.0)=6.0$ m/s. The force on the cart causes its acceleration. The opposite force on the hand or rope does not cancel it on the cart's diagram.

Worked example 3.2

A horizontal launch develops a downward velocity

Problem. A puck leaves the horizontal edge of a table at $28.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The tabletop is $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ above the floor. Find the angle below horizontal of the puck's velocity just before impact. Ignore air resistance and use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Picture and principle. Draw the horizontal velocity to the right and vertical velocity downward at impact. These perpendicular arrows form a right triangle whose diagonal is the total velocity. A horizontal launch means $v_{y0}=0$; it does not mean the vertical velocity remains zero.

With rightward and upward chosen positive,

$$v_x=v_{x0}=28.0\,\mathrm{m/s},\qquad a_y=-g,\qquad\Delta y=-h.$$

We can obtain the impact vertical component without finding time. Start with

$$v_y^2=v_{y0}^2+2a_y\Delta y=0+2(-g)(-h)=2gh.$$

Taking a square root gives a magnitude. The downward direction determines the sign:

$$v_y=-\sqrt{2gh}=-\sqrt{2(9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(2.00\,\mathrm{m})}=-6.26\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

For the positive angle $\alpha$ measured below horizontal, use the velocity triangle:

$$\tan\alpha=\frac{|v_y|}{v_x}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\alpha=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{6.26}{28.0}\right)=\boxed{12.6^\circ\text{ below horizontal}}.$$

Meaning and check. Horizontal velocity is much greater than downward velocity, so the impact direction should be shallow, not nearly vertical. An independent time calculation gives $t=\sqrt{2h/g}=0.639\,\mathrm{s}$, and then $v_y=-gt=-6.26\,\mathrm{m/s}$ again. The angle concerns the tangent to the trajectory at impact; it is not the angle of the straight line joining launch and landing points.

Worked example 3.3

An elevated launch needs the full vertical equation

Problem. A ball is launched at $20.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $60.0^\circ$ above horizontal from a point $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ above level ground. Determine the time until it reaches the ground. Ignore air resistance and use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Picture and principle. Draw the ground as $y=0$ and place the launch point at $y_0=2.00\,\mathrm{m}$. The ball first rises and then falls below its original height. Only the initial vertical component determines the flight time:

$$v_{y0}=v_0\sin\theta=(20.0\,\mathrm{m/s})\sin60.0^\circ=17.3\ldots\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

The vertical position is $y=y_0+v_{y0}t-\tfrac12gt^2$. At landing, set $y=0$ and rearrange into quadratic form:

$$\frac12gt^2-v_{y0}t-y_0=0.$$

Applying the quadratic formula before inserting values gives

$$t=\frac{v_{y0}\pm\sqrt{v_{y0}^2+2gy_0}}{g}.$$

The positive root describes the future landing event:

$$t=\frac{17.3+\sqrt{17.3^2+2(9.81)(2.00)}}{9.81}\,\mathrm{s}=\boxed{3.64\,\mathrm{s}}.$$

The other root is approximately $-0.112\,\mathrm{s}$. It belongs to a backward mathematical extension of the parabola and does not describe motion after release. Keeping extra digits gives $3.65\,\mathrm{s}$ for the stated gravitational acceleration.

Meaning and check. Returning merely to launch height would take $2v_{y0}/g\approx3.53\,\mathrm{s}$. The ground is lower, so the actual flight must last longer; $3.64\,\mathrm{s}$ passes this check. The source bank used $g=9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and quotes $3.65\,\mathrm s$.

TÜRKÇE

$2v_{y0}/g$ bağıntısı, başlangıç ve bitiş yükseklikleri eşitse geçerlidir. Burada top iki metre daha aşağıya indiği için bu kısa yol kullanılamaz. Denklemde $y_0$'ı koru; kök bulduktan sonra olayın $t\ge0$ koşulunu sağladığını kontrol et.

Worked example 3.4

Choose the ascending root before finding horizontal distance

Problem. A rock is thrown from $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ above the ground at $30.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $40.0^\circ$ above horizontal. It reaches a window $18.0\,\mathrm{m}$ above the ground while still rising. Find the horizontal launch distance from the window. Ignore air resistance; use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Picture and principle. Place the origin at the launch point, with $+x$ toward the window and $+y$ upward. The required vertical displacement is $\Delta y=18.0-2.00=16.0\,\mathrm{m}$. Resolve the initial velocity:

$$v_{x0}=30.0\cos40.0^\circ=23.0\,\mathrm{m/s},$$

$$v_{y0}=30.0\sin40.0^\circ=19.3\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

First solve the vertical motion for time; then use that same time horizontally:

$$\Delta y=v_{y0}t-\frac12gt^2\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac12gt^2-v_{y0}t+\Delta y=0,$$

$$t=\frac{v_{y0}\pm\sqrt{v_{y0}^2-2g\Delta y}}{g}.$$

Numerical substitution produces two positive roots:

$$t=\frac{19.3\pm\sqrt{19.3^2-2(9.81)(16.0)}}{9.81}\,\mathrm{s},$$

$$t_1=1.19\,\mathrm{s},\qquad t_2=2.75\,\mathrm{s}.$$

“Still rising” selects the earlier root. With zero horizontal acceleration,

$$x=v_{x0}t_1=(23.0\,\mathrm{m/s})(1.19\,\mathrm{s})=\boxed{27.3\,\mathrm{m}}.$$

Meaning and check. At the selected time, $v_y=v_{y0}-gt_1\approx+7.63\,\mathrm{m/s}$, so the rock is indeed rising. The later root gives a downward vertical velocity and belongs to the descending crossing of the same height. Both roots solve the height equation, but only one satisfies the stated condition.

The highest point occurs at $v_{y0}/g\approx1.97\,\mathrm{s}$, between the two roots. This gives another way to distinguish the upward and downward crossings.

Worked example 3.5

Reconstruct a launch from its range and flight time

Problem. A projectile travels $76.2\,\mathrm{m}$ horizontally and returns to its launch height after $4.08\,\mathrm{s}$. Determine its initial speed. Air resistance is negligible. Use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and choose the horizontal travel direction and upward direction as positive.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Picture and principle. Draw a parabolic trajectory whose two endpoints are at the same height. Instead of starting with launch speed and angle, reconstruct the two initial velocity components separately. The horizontal displacement determines $v_{x0}$; the zero net vertical displacement determines $v_{y0}$.

With no horizontal acceleration,

$$R=v_{x0}T\quad\Rightarrow\quad v_{x0}=\frac{R}{T}=\frac{76.2\,\mathrm{m}}{4.08\,\mathrm{s}}=18.7\ldots\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

The vertical displacement over the complete flight is zero:

$$0=v_{y0}T-\frac12gT^2.$$

Since $T\ne0$, divide both sides by $T$, then move the gravitational term to the other side:

$$v_{y0}=\frac12gT=\frac12(9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(4.08\,\mathrm{s})=20.0\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

These components are perpendicular, so their magnitudes are not added directly. Use Pythagoras:

$$v_0=\sqrt{v_{x0}^2+v_{y0}^2}=\sqrt{(18.7)^2+(20.0)^2}\,\mathrm{m/s}=\boxed{27.4\,\mathrm{m/s}}.$$

Meaning and check. The initial velocity has slightly more upward than horizontal motion. Its direction would be $\tan^{-1}(20.0/18.7)\approx46.9^\circ$ above horizontal. The time to maximum height, $v_{y0}/g=2.04\,\mathrm{s}$, is exactly half the total flight time, as expected for equal launch and landing heights. Reconstructed horizontal motion gives $v_{x0}T=76.2\,\mathrm{m}$.

From stopping distance to braking force

Problem. A car of mass $1100\,\mathrm{kg}$ travels at $27.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and stops after $578\,\mathrm m$. Find the magnitude of the effective average braking force. Represent the braking by a constant horizontal force over this distance.

Start from the physics. Choose the car's original direction as positive. Its displacement is positive, but its acceleration is negative because its speed decreases. On a level road the vertical forces balance. The horizontal net force therefore determines the braking acceleration. We know distance rather than stopping time, so use the kinematic equation that does not contain time:

$$v_f^2=v_i^2+2a\Delta x.$$

Rearrange before substituting. Subtract $v_i^2$ from both sides, then divide both sides by $2\Delta x$:

$$\begin{aligned} v_f^2-v_i^2&=2a\Delta x,\\ a&=\frac{v_f^2-v_i^2}{2\Delta x} =\frac{0-(27.0\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2(578\,\mathrm m)}\\ &=-0.631\,\mathrm{m/s^2}. \end{aligned}$$

Now apply Newton's second law to the car, using its mass rather than its weight:

$$F_x=ma=(1100\,\mathrm{kg})(-0.631\,\mathrm{m/s^2})=-694\,\mathrm N.$$

Interpret the result. The requested magnitude is $\boxed{694\,\mathrm N}$, approximately $690\,\mathrm N$ at two significant figures. The signed force points opposite the motion. A real braking force may vary; this constant-force model gives the equivalent force over the stated stopping distance, not a claim about its detailed time history.

Sense check. The unit $\mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}$ is a newton. Substitution gives $27.0^2+2(-0.631)(578)\approx0$, consistent with stopping.

A velocity reversal: subtract signed velocities

Problem. During a test, a $1.50\,\mathrm g$ bullet passes through a $28.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ block in $11.4\,\mathrm{ms}$. The block's velocity changes uniformly from $1.75\,\mathrm{m/s}$ rightward to $1.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ leftward. Find the magnitude of the horizontal force exerted by the bullet on the block.

Identify the system. We are analysing the block, so Newton's second law uses the block's mass, $28.0\,\mathrm{kg}$. The bullet's mass is supplied by the bank but is not needed for this particular question. Take rightward as positive. The initial and final velocities must therefore have opposite signs:

$$v_i=+1.75\,\mathrm{m/s},\qquad v_f=-1.20\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$

Convert the short time interval before dividing:

$$\Delta t=11.4\,\mathrm{ms}=11.4\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm s=0.0114\,\mathrm s.$$

Calculate acceleration explicitly. A change means final minus initial, not the difference between two positive speed readings:

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta v&=v_f-v_i=(-1.20)-(+1.75)=-2.95\,\mathrm{m/s},\\ a_x&=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} =\frac{-2.95\,\mathrm{m/s}}{0.0114\,\mathrm s} =-259\,\mathrm{m/s^2}. \end{aligned}$$

The horizontal interaction force is the horizontal net force on the block during the stated idealized test:

$$F_x=ma_x=(28.0)(-259)\,\mathrm N=-7.25\times10^3\,\mathrm N.$$

Interpret and check. Its magnitude is $\boxed{7.25\times10^3\,\mathrm N}$, directed leftward. This large value is reasonable because the reversal occurs in only about one hundredth of a second. The same change spread over twice the time would require half the force. Dividing the answer by the block mass recovers the acceleration.

Rocket thrust must overcome weight and accelerate

Problem. A rocket of mass $10{,}000\,\mathrm{kg}$ accelerates vertically upward at $2.00\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ near Earth's surface. Neglect air resistance. Find the upward engine thrust, using $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Treat the given mass as constant during this calculation.

Draw the force picture in words. For the rocket as one system, thrust $T$ acts upward and weight $mg$ acts downward. Choose upward as the positive direction. These forces oppose one another, so the net force is their difference. The equation is not $T=ma$ because thrust is only one of the forces acting on the rocket:

$$\sum F_y=T-mg=ma.$$

Isolate the unknown. Add $mg$ to both sides. This cancels the negative weight term on the left:

$$\begin{aligned} T-mg+mg&=ma+mg,\\ T&=ma+mg=m(a+g). \end{aligned}$$

Now substitute compatible SI units:

Key equation 3.2
$$\begin{aligned} mg&=(10{,}000)(9.81)\,\mathrm N=98{,}100\,\mathrm N,\\ ma&=(10{,}000)(2.00)\,\mathrm N=20{,}000\,\mathrm N,\\ T&=98{,}100\,\mathrm N+20{,}000\,\mathrm N\\ &=1.18\times10^5\,\mathrm N\ \text{upward}. \end{aligned}$$

Understand the two contributions. The first $98{,}100\,\mathrm N$ balances gravity. The additional $20{,}000\,\mathrm N$ produces the specified upward acceleration. The rocket does not need its thrust to equal its weight unless its vertical acceleration is zero.

Sense check. Subtracting the weight from the thrust gives $20{,}000\,\mathrm N$; dividing by $10{,}000\,\mathrm{kg}$ returns $2.00\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. If the desired acceleration approached zero, the formula would become $T=mg$, as expected for hovering in this simple model.

Infer a force from a measured travel time

Problem. A $50\,\mathrm{kg}$ box starts from rest on a frictionless horizontal surface. A constant horizontal force makes it travel $28\,\mathrm m$ in $6.9\,\mathrm s$. Find the force magnitude.

Connect motion to force in two stages. The measured distance and time describe the motion; they do not directly give a force. First find the acceleration, then use Newton's second law. Because the force and mass are constant, the horizontal acceleration is constant. The box starts from rest, so $v_0=0$. Choose the force direction as positive and set the starting position to zero.

The displacement equation simplifies to

$$\Delta x=v_0t+\frac12at^2=\frac12at^2.$$

Do the algebra before using numbers. Multiplying both sides by $2$ removes the fraction; dividing both sides by $t^2$ isolates $a$:

$$\begin{aligned} 2\Delta x&=at^2,\\ a&=\frac{2\Delta x}{t^2} =\frac{2(28\,\mathrm m)}{(6.9\,\mathrm s)^2}\\ &=\frac{56}{47.6}\,\mathrm{m/s^2} =1.18\,\mathrm{m/s^2}. \end{aligned}$$

On the frictionless surface, weight and normal force cancel vertically, leaving the applied force as the horizontal net force:

$$F=ma=(50\,\mathrm{kg})(1.18\,\mathrm{m/s^2}) =\boxed{58.8\,\mathrm N\approx59\,\mathrm N}.$$

Sense check. The average speed is $28/6.9=4.06\,\mathrm{m/s}$. With constant acceleration from rest, the final speed should be twice this value: $8.12\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Calculating $at$ gives the same result. This is an independent check on the factor of two in the displacement equation.

An angled pull changes both acceleration and support force

Problem. A worker pulls a $40.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ tool box across a smooth horizontal floor with a $100\,\mathrm N$ force directed $37.0^\circ$ above horizontal. Describe its free-body diagram, calculate its horizontal acceleration, and find the normal force from the floor. Use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Start with three forces. The box has weight $mg$ downward, normal force $N$ upward, and the applied pull $F$ tilted upward and forward. Smooth means friction is neglected. Choose horizontal $+x$ forward and vertical $+y$ upward. The angle is measured from horizontal, so the adjacent component uses cosine:

$$F_x=F\cos\theta,\qquad F_y=F\sin\theta.$$

Horizontal equation. Only the horizontal part of the pull accelerates the box along the floor:

$$\begin{aligned} F\cos\theta&=ma_x,\\ a_x&=\frac{F\cos\theta}{m} =\frac{(100\,\mathrm N)\cos37.0^\circ}{40.0\,\mathrm{kg}}\\ &=\boxed{2.00\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}. \end{aligned}$$

Vertical equation. The box stays on the floor rather than accelerating upward. Therefore $a_y=0$, even though the pull has a vertical component:

$$\begin{aligned} N+F\sin\theta-mg&=0,\\ N&=mg-F\sin\theta\\ &=(40.0)(9.81)-(100)\sin37.0^\circ\ \mathrm N\\ &=392.4-60.2=\boxed{332\,\mathrm N}. \end{aligned}$$

Interpret and check. The worker partially supports the box, so the floor supplies less than the full weight. Our result is positive and smaller than $392\,\mathrm N$, consistent with continued contact. In the limiting case of a horizontal pull, $\theta=0$, the formulas give $a_x=F/m$ and $N=mg$.
