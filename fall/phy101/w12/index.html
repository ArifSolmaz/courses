Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  1. Calculate the center of mass for systems of point masses and extended objects
  2. Apply the center of mass concept to analyze motion of multi-body systems
  3. Define torque and compute it for various force-lever arm configurations
  4. State the two conditions for static equilibrium ($\Sigma F = 0$, $\Sigma \tau = 0$)
  5. Solve static equilibrium problems for beams, levers, and structures
  6. Apply equilibrium analysis to engineering problems (robot arms, bridges, cranes)

Joining this lessonDerse buradan başlayanlar

You can join with a force diagram and a calculator; no earlier Python work is needed. Recall these three ideas:

  • Weight is $W=mg$, downward. For a uniform beam it acts at the midpoint; $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ here.
  • In a planar static problem, $\sum F_x=0$, $\sum F_y=0$ and $\sum\tau=0$. Choose a pivot and one positive turning direction.
  • Torque is $\tau=F d_\perp$, using the perpendicular distance to the force's line. For a downward weight on an inclined rod, $d_\perp=r\cos\theta$ when $\theta$ is measured above horizontal.

Quick entry check: A $100\,\mathrm N$ load acts $0.40\,\mathrm m$ right of a pivot. An upward force at $0.80\,\mathrm m$ on the same side balances it when $F(0.80)=100(0.40)$, giving $F=50\,\mathrm N$. Then use force balance to find the pivot reaction.

Before the equations: a force can turn an objectKuvvet ve döndürme etkisi

Work on paper first; the interactive pictures are optional ways to test a prediction. For a body at rest we need both $\sum F_x=0,\ \sum F_y=0$ and $\sum\tau=0$. A zero net force alone does not prevent rotation.

Draw every force at its application point. Replace a uniform rod's distributed weight by $mg$ at its midpoint. Choose a pivot that removes an unknown force: a force whose line passes through the pivot has zero lever arm.

The geometry bridge: $\tau=F d_\perp=rF\sin\theta$. The lever arm $d_\perp$ is the shortest distance from the pivot to the line of the force, not always the length of the object. A vertical weight on a rod at angle $\phi$ above horizontal has lever arm $r\cos\phi$. A horizontal force at its end has lever arm $r\sin\phi$.

The algebra bridge: If $F(4\,\mathrm m)=(200\,\mathrm N)(2\,\mathrm m)$, divide both sides by $4\,\mathrm m$ to get $F=100\,\mathrm N$. The metres cancel. A negative reaction force means the real direction is opposite to the arrow you guessed; a pin can push or pull, but a simple support cannot pull downward.

For center of mass, first calculate each $m_ix_i$ and $m_iy_i$, add each column, then divide by total mass: $x_{\rm cm}=\frac{\sum_i m_ix_i}{\sum_i m_i}$. A weighted average lies within the range of the positive-mass positions.

Pause: A vertical forearm hangs directly below the elbow. Gravity's line passes through the elbow, so its gravitational torque about that joint is zero.

How this week is workedBu hafta nasıl çalışılır

Every problem, worked example and experiment in this course follows the same six steps. Step 4 is the one added in September 2026, and it is the one that separates a physicist from a calculator operator.

draw → choose a law → rearrange to the symbolic answer → check a limiting case → substitute with units → interpret

  1. Symbolic answer first. Isolate the unknown in symbols, before any number appears.
  2. One limiting case. Push a parameter to a value whose answer you already know — a mass to zero, an angle to $90^\circ$, a friction coefficient to zero — and confirm the formula behaves sensibly. If it does not, the algebra is wrong and you have just caught it for free, before wasting arithmetic on it.
  3. Then numbers, with units carried through every line.

Why bother: the symbolic route is less arithmetic, not more, and it is where the marks are. A correct symbolic answer with a slip in the final multiplication is nearly full credit; a correct number with no derivation is not.

Center of Mass

Definition

The center of mass (COM) is the mass-weighted average position of all particles in a system:

Key equation 12.1
$$\;x_{\text{cm}} = \frac{\sum_i m_i x_i}{\sum_i m_i},\qquad y_{\text{cm}} = \frac{\sum_i m_i y_i}{\sum_i m_i}\;$$

Why It Matters

Property Description
Motion The COM moves as if all mass is concentrated there and all external forces act on it
Momentum $\vec{p}_{\text{total}} = M_{\text{total}} \cdot \vec{v}_{\text{cm}}$
Stability For a body resting on a horizontal support under gravity, the vertical projection of the COM must stay inside its support region to avoid tipping
Balance In a uniform gravitational field, a support through the COM gives zero gravitational torque about that support

Analogy: The Team Captain

Imagine a group of people on a raft. Even if individuals move around, the raft itself moves based on where the "average person" (the center of mass) is. The heavier people pull the average closer to them, just like heavier masses pull the COM closer.

Two-coordinate calculation / İki koordinatı ayrı hesapla. For $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $(0,0)$ and $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $(4,2)\,\mathrm m$:

$$\begin{aligned}M&=1+3=4\,\mathrm{kg},\\x_\mathrm{cm}&=\frac{1(0)+3(4)}{4}=3.0\,\mathrm m,\\y_\mathrm{cm}&=\frac{1(0)+3(2)}{4}=1.5\,\mathrm m.\end{aligned}$$

The center lies three quarters of the way toward the larger mass. TR: Aynı kütle paydasını kullanırken $x$ ve $y$ paylarını birbirine karıştırmayın. Sıfır toplam kütle için bu ortalama tanımlı değildir.

Torque

Definition

Torque is the rotational equivalent of force. It measures how much a force tends to cause rotation:

Key equation 12.2
$$\;\vec\tau=\vec r\times\vec F,\qquad |\tau|=rF\sin\theta=F d_\perp,\qquad d_\perp=r\sin\theta\;$$

The cross product gives a vector. For planar calculations we use its signed component perpendicular to the plane: counterclockwise positive and clockwise negative.

where:

  • $r$ = distance from the pivot to where the force is applied (position-vector length)
  • $F$ = magnitude of the force
  • $\theta$ = angle between $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{F}$
Quantity Symbol SI Unit Sign Convention
Torque $\tau$ $\mathrm{N\,m}$ CCW = positive, CW = negative
Perpendicular lever arm $d_\perp=r\sin\theta$ $\mathrm m$ A nonnegative distance
Force $F$ N Direction matters

Analogy: Opening a Door

Why is the doorknob placed at the far edge from the hinge? For a perpendicular push, $|\tau|=Fr$. A larger perpendicular distance from the hinge gives more torque for the same force. Try pushing a door near the hinge -- it barely moves!

Calculate the perpendicular distance first. A $12\,\mathrm N$ force acts $1.0\,\mathrm m$ from the pivot at $30^\circ$ to the rod:

$$d_\perp=r\sin30^\circ=0.50\,\mathrm m,\qquad |\tau|=F d_\perp=(12)(0.50)=6.0\,\mathrm{N\,m}.$$

The lever arm is measured from the pivot perpendicular to the force's line of action, not along the force arrow or vertically by default. TR: Dik uzaklığı önce şekil üzerinde işaretleyin. Kuvvetin uygulandığı nokta aynı kalsa bile yön değişince tork değişebilir.

Static Equilibrium

Two Conditions

An object initially at rest remains in static equilibrium when both conditions hold. If it is already moving, zero net force and torque do not by themselves make it stop:

  1. Translational equilibrium (no net force): $\sum \vec{F} = 0$
  2. Rotational equilibrium (no net torque about any point): $\sum \tau = 0$
Key equation 12.3
$$\;\sum \vec F = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum \tau = 0 \quad\text{(about any point)}\;$$

Problem-Solving Strategy

  1. Draw a free-body diagram showing all forces
  2. Choose a convenient pivot point (often where an unknown force acts, to eliminate it)
  3. Write $\sum F_x = 0$, $\sum F_y = 0$, and $\sum \tau = 0$
  4. Solve the system of equations

Key Insight

You can choose ANY point as the pivot for the torque equation. Choosing wisely (where unknown forces act) simplifies your math enormously!

Why two equations are needed. Two forces $+10\,\mathrm N$ upward at the right end and $-10\,\mathrm N$ downward at the left end of a $2.0\,\mathrm m$ bar give zero net force. About the center, each contributes $+10\,\mathrm{N\,m}$, so net torque is $+20\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. The bar is not in static equilibrium.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Center of Mass of a System

Three masses are placed on the $x$-axis: $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $x=0$, $5\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $x=3\,\mathrm m$, and $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $x=7\,\mathrm m$. Find the center of mass.

Paper solution. A center of mass is a mass-weighted average. Make a small table:

$m$ (kg) $x$ (m) $mx$ (kg m)
2 0 0
5 3 15
3 7 21
Total 10 Total 36

$$x_{\rm cm}=\frac{0+15+21}{2+5+3}=\frac{36\ \mathrm{kg\,m}}{10\ \mathrm{kg}}=\boxed{3.6\ \mathrm m}.$$ The result lies between 0 and $7\,\mathrm m$, as required for positive masses. It lies right of $3\,\mathrm m$ because the mass at $7\,\mathrm m$ has a larger rightward moment than the mass at $0\,\mathrm m$ has leftward moment about $3\,\mathrm m$.

Example 2: Beam with Two Supports

A uniform $4\,\mathrm m$ beam weighing $200\,\mathrm N$ is supported at both ends (A at $x=0$, B at $x=4\,\mathrm m$). A $500\,\mathrm N$ weight is placed at $x=1\,\mathrm m$ from end A. Find the reaction forces at A and B.

Show the worked route / Çözüm yolunu göster

Paper solution. Draw upward reactions $R_A,R_B$. The uniform beam's own $200\,\mathrm N$ weight acts at its center, $x=2\,\mathrm m$; the $500\,\mathrm N$ load acts at $x=1\,\mathrm m$. Choose A as the torque pivot, counterclockwise positive: $$R_B(4)-200(2)-500(1)=0.$$ Add the two clockwise moments: $4R_B=400+500=900\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. Divide by $4\,\mathrm m$: $R_B=225\,\mathrm N$.

Next use vertical force balance: $R_A+R_B-200-500=0$, so $R_A=700-225=475\,\mathrm N$. Check: $475+225=700\,\mathrm N$ equals the total load. Taking moments about B also gives $475(4)=200(2)+500(3)=1900\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. A carries more load because the $500\,\mathrm N$ weight is nearer A.

Engineering example: Robot Arm Moment Calculation

A simple robot arm is modelled as a single uniform horizontal segment ($L_1=0.50\,\mathrm m$, $m_1=2\,\mathrm{kg}$) holding a $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ payload at its end. Calculate the torque at the shoulder joint needed to hold this position.

Principle. The segment’s weight acts at its midpoint and the payload’s weight at the tip; each torque is weight times horizontal lever arm. $$\tau_\mathrm{shoulder}=m_1g\frac{L_1}{2}+m_pgL_1 =(2)(9.81)(0.25)+(3)(9.81)(0.50)=19.6\,\mathrm{N\,m}.$$ The motor supplies counterclockwise torque to balance gravity. Support forces depend on where the load is placed

Problem. A light $10\,\mathrm{m}$ board rests on two sawhorses: one at its left end and one at its midpoint. A $40\,\mathrm{N}$ load lies between them, $3.0\,\mathrm{m}$ from the left end. Neglect the board's weight and find the upward force supplied by each support.

Reasoning and algebra. Mark positions from the left end: $x=0$ for the first support, $x=3.0\,\mathrm{m}$ for the load and $x=5.0\,\mathrm{m}$ for the midpoint support. The remaining half of the board is an unloaded overhang. Let the upward reactions be $R_0$ and $R_5$.

Take moments about the left support, with counterclockwise positive. Its own reaction has zero lever arm and disappears from this equation:

$$ \begin{aligned} \sum\tau_0&=0,\\ R_5(5.0\,\mathrm{m})-(40\,\mathrm{N})(3.0\,\mathrm{m})&=0,\\ R_5&=\frac{(40\,\mathrm{N})(3.0\,\mathrm{m})}{5.0\,\mathrm{m}} =24\,\mathrm{N}. \end{aligned} $$

Then use vertical force balance:

$$ R_0+R_5-40\,\mathrm{N}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_0=40-24=16\,\mathrm{N}. $$

Answer and check. The left-end support supplies $16\,\mathrm{N}$ and the midpoint support $24\,\mathrm{N}$, both upward. Their sum equals the load. Taking moments about the midpoint gives $(16)(5)=(40)(2)=80\,\mathrm{N\,m}$, confirming equilibrium independently. The nearer support carries more of the load, as the result suggests.

A small tack shifts the balance point of a metre stick

Problem. A nonuniform metre stick of mass $80.0\,\mathrm{g}$ balances at its $51.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ mark. Where should a $5.00\,\mathrm{g}$ tack be placed so that the combined system balances at $50.0\,\mathrm{cm}$? Treat the tack as a small concentrated mass.

Reasoning and algebra. The original balance point identifies the stick's centre of mass: $x_s=0.510\,\mathrm{m}$. Its nonuniformity matters through that location; we must not replace it with the geometric midpoint. The desired new centre of mass is $x_c=0.500\,\mathrm{m}$. Let $x$ be the tack's position from the same zero mark.

Write the mass-weighted position equation and clear the denominator first:

$$ \begin{aligned} x_c&=\frac{m_sx_s+m_tx}{m_s+m_t},\\ (m_s+m_t)x_c&=m_sx_s+m_tx,\\ x&=\frac{(m_s+m_t)x_c-m_sx_s}{m_t}. \end{aligned} $$

With masses in kilograms,

$$ x=\frac{(0.0800+0.00500)(0.500)-(0.0800)(0.510)}{0.00500} =\boxed{0.340\,\mathrm{m}=34.0\,\mathrm{cm}}. $$

Interpretation and check. The tack belongs left of the new pivot, opposing the stick's weight acting just to its right. About the $50\,\mathrm{cm}$ mark, the mass-distance products balance:

$$ (0.0800)(0.0100)=(0.00500)(0.160)=0.000800\,\mathrm{kg\,m}. $$

Multiplying both sides by $g$ gives equal opposing torque magnitudes. The tack is sixteen times lighter, so it needs sixteen times the lever arm. Its position lies on the metre stick, making the result physically feasible.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Cetvelin kütle merkezi $51\,\mathrm{cm}$'dedir; düzgün bir cetvelmiş gibi $50\,\mathrm{cm}$ alamayız. Denklemde önce paydayı karşı tarafa çarptık, sonra cetvelin katkısını çıkardık ve raptiye kütlesine böldük. $34\,\mathrm{cm}$ konumdur; yeni desteğe olan uzaklık $16\,\mathrm{cm}$'dir.

Include the beam itself when balancing two children

Problem. A $30.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ child sits at one end of a uniform beam of mass $20.0\,\mathrm{kg}$, and a $40.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ child sits at the other. The system balances on a fulcrum $1.10\,\mathrm{m}$ from the lighter child. Find the beam's length. Use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ for a force check.

Reasoning and algebra. Choose the lighter child's end as $x=0$, with the other end at $x=L$. The beam's own centre of mass lies at $L/2$. For a single support to balance the system, its location must equal the combined centre of mass:

$$ \begin{aligned} 1.10\,\mathrm{m} &=\frac{(30.0)(0)+(40.0)L+(20.0)(L/2)}{30.0+40.0+20.0},\\ 1.10&=\frac{40L+10L}{90}=\frac{50L}{90},\\ 90(1.10)&=50L,\\ L&=\frac{99.0}{50}=\boxed{1.98\,\mathrm{m}}. \end{aligned} $$

The beam's centre is therefore at $0.990\,\mathrm{m}$, slightly left of the fulcrum. It contributes to the lighter child's side of the torque balance.

Check using moments. Distances from the support are $1.10\,\mathrm{m}$ for the lighter child, $0.880\,\mathrm{m}$ for the heavier child and $0.110\,\mathrm{m}$ for the beam's centre:

$$ 40.0(0.880)=30.0(1.10)+20.0(0.110)=35.2\,\mathrm{kg\,m}. $$

The common factor $g$ cancels. Vertical force balance would require the support to supply $(90.0)(9.81)=883\,\mathrm{N}$ upward.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Tahtanın $20\,\mathrm{kg}$ kütlesini ihmal etmiyoruz; ağırlığı orta noktasından etkir. Kütle merkezi denklemindeki $20(L/2)$ terimi bu katkıdır. Uzunluğu bulduktan sonra torklarla tekrar kontrol etmek, hangi ağırlığın desteğin hangi tarafında kaldığını görmemizi sağlar.

Decide whether a refrigerator slides or tips first

Problem. A refrigerator has mass $120\,\mathrm{kg}$, height $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$, and width $0.850\,\mathrm{m}$ in the direction of pushing. Its centre of mass is at its geometric centre. The floor's static-friction coefficient is $0.300$. Find the greatest height at which a horizontal push can be applied without tipping beginning before sliding. Use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Reasoning and algebra. Compare the two thresholds while the refrigerator is still at rest. A horizontal push has no vertical component, so $N=mg$. Sliding becomes possible when

$$ F_{\text{slide}}=\mu_smg =(0.300)(120)(9.81)=353\,\mathrm{N}. $$

For tipping, choose the lower edge on the far side of the push as the pivot. The push has lever arm $h$; the weight has horizontal lever arm $b/2$. At incipient tipping,

$$ F_{\text{tip}}h=mg\frac b2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_{\text{tip}}=\frac{mgb}{2h}. $$

To avoid tipping first, require $F_{\text{slide}}\leq F_{\text{tip}}$:

$$ \mu_smg\leq\frac{mgb}{2h} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h\leq\frac{b}{2\mu_s} =\frac{0.850}{2(0.300)}\,\mathrm{m} =\boxed{1.42\,\mathrm{m}}. $$

Interpretation and check. This is the ideal limiting height: the two thresholds coincide there. Pushing below it makes sliding begin first. At the limit, the push torque and weight torque both equal $500\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. The mass cancels from the height condition, but not from the force needed to start motion.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Kayma sınırında sürtünme, devrilme sınırında ise tork dengesi belirleyicidir. Devirmeye çalışan kuvvetin kolu itme yüksekliği; ağırlığın kolu genişliğin yarısıdır. $h$'yi büyütmek aynı kuvvetin torkunu büyütür. $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ toplam yükseklik, izin verilen itme yüksekliğiyle aynı şey değildir.

Source: Young/Freedman, University Physics with Modern Physics, 14th Global Edition, Chapter 11, §11.2 Problems, question 11 (11_testbank.doc). Original givens retained; wording and worked explanation expanded.

Compare the sliding and tipping angles of a cube

Problem. A cubical concrete block rests in a dump-truck bed that is slowly tilted. The block–bed coefficients are $\mu_s=0.650$ and $\mu_k=0.450$. Determine whether sliding or tipping begins first. Treat the cube as uniform, so its centre of mass is at its geometric centre.

Reasoning and algebra. While the block is stationary, gravity's downhill component must be balanced by static friction. The normal force is $mg\cos\theta$. Sliding starts at the limiting static-friction value:

$$ \begin{aligned} mg\sin\theta_s&=\mu_smg\cos\theta_s,\\ \tan\theta_s&=\mu_s,\\ \theta_s&=\tan^{-1}(0.650)=\boxed{33.0^\circ}. \end{aligned} $$

Now imagine the block still held against sliding and find its tipping threshold. Let the cube side length be $a$. The centre of mass lies halfway up and halfway across its base. At the threshold, its vertical line of action reaches the downhill edge. Taking moments about that edge gives

$$ mg\sin\theta_t\frac a2 =mg\cos\theta_t\frac a2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tan\theta_t=1, \qquad \boxed{\theta_t=45^\circ}. $$

Answer and check. Since $33.0^\circ<45^\circ$, the block begins to slide first. At the sliding threshold, the centre-of-mass projection has shifted downhill by $(a/2)\tan\theta_s=0.325a$, still less than the half-width $0.500a$. It therefore remains inside the supporting base when sliding starts.

The mass and side length cancel from the threshold comparison. The kinetic coefficient does not determine the onset of motion; it applies after sliding has begun. This example addresses which motion starts first, without assuming a subsequent sliding trajectory.

TÜRKÇE

açıklama. Hareket başlamadan önce $\mu_s$ kullanılır; verilen $\mu_k$'yi doğrudan yerine koymuyoruz. Küp için kütle merkezinin tabana yüksekliği ile kenara uzaklığı eşit olduğundan devrilme açısı $45^\circ$ olur. Daha küçük eşik açısına önce ulaşıldığı için ilk olay kaymadır.
