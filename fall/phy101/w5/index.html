Learning Objectives

By the end of this session you will be able to:

  1. Define the work done by a constant force, and by a variable force as the area under an $F$–$x$ graph
  2. State and apply the work–energy theorem: $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K$
  3. Calculate kinetic energy and relate it to a change in speed
  4. Determine the sign of the work done by each force acting on a body
  5. Compute average and instantaneous power
  6. Solve a work–energy problem symbolically, check a limiting case, and only then substitute numbers

Before the equations: energy in small stepsEnerjiyi adım adım anlayalım

Read → draw → calculate → explain. The physics can be completed on paper with a calculator. The plots are experiments to observe; their Python implementation is optional.

Start with a picture of the initial and final states and list every force that acts through the displacement. This week the account has exactly two sides:

$$W_{\rm net}=\Delta K=K_f-K_i, \qquad K=\tfrac12mv^2.$$

Add up the work done by all the forces first — that is what net means — and the total tells you how the kinetic energy changed. Work against the motion is negative and reduces $K$; a force perpendicular to the displacement does no work at all.

Watch out

Not yet. Nothing here needs $mgh$ or $\tfrac12kx^2$. Storing energy in a position rather than in motion is Week 08's idea; keeping the two weeks apart is what makes each of them simple.

TÜRKÇE

Bu haftaki hesabın iki tarafı var: bütün kuvvetlerin yaptığı toplam iş ve kinetik enerjideki değişim. $mgh$ veya $\tfrac12kx^2$'ye burada ihtiyaç yok; enerjiyi konumda depolamak 8. haftanın fikridir.

The algebra bridge. To solve $\tfrac12mv^2=E$:

  1. Multiply both sides by 2: $mv^2=2E$.
  2. Divide both sides by $m$: $v^2=2E/m$.
  3. Take the positive square root for speed: $v=\sqrt{2E/m}$. Energy alone does not tell the direction.

For example, let $E=18\,\mathrm J$ and $m=4\,\mathrm{kg}$:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac12mv^2&=E,\\mv^2&=2E,\\v^2&=\frac{2E}{m}=\frac{2(18\,\mathrm J)}{4\,\mathrm{kg}}=9\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2},\\v&=\sqrt{9\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}=3\,\mathrm{m/s}.\end{aligned}$$ Taking $18/4$ directly as the speed misses both the factor 2 and the square root. Doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy.

The calculus bridge: work is an area / İş bir alandır. You already know how to find the work done by a constant force: force times distance is the area of a rectangle on a graph of $F$ against $x$.

If the force changes as the object moves, the shape under the graph is no longer a rectangle — but it is still an area, and you can still find it. A force rising steadily from $0$ to $6\,\mathrm N$ over $2\,\mathrm m$ traces a triangle, so $W=\tfrac12(2)(6)=6\,\mathrm J$. Cut a more awkward curve into thin vertical strips, each narrow enough that $F$ barely changes across it, and add the little rectangles up.

That adding-up of infinitely thin strips is exactly what the integral sign means:

$$W=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}F(x)\,dx \qquad \textbf{= the signed area under the } F\textbf{–}x \textbf{ graph.}$$

Read it as a sentence, not as a technique: the work is the area under the force–displacement graph. Area below the axis counts as negative work. Every integral in this course can be read as an area, and every derivative as a slope — if you can find an area on graph paper you can do the physics, and the notation is just bookkeeping for a cleverer way of counting the same squares.

Workİş

Work by a constant force

When a constant force $\vec{F}$ acts on an object that undergoes displacement $\vec{d}$:

Key equation 5.1
$$\;W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = Fd\cos\theta\;$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and displacement vectors.

$\theta$: $0^\circ$

  • $\cos\theta$: $+1$

  • Physical meaning: Force along motion -- positive work

$\theta$: $90^\circ$

  • $\cos\theta$: $0$

  • Physical meaning: Force perpendicular -- zero work

$\theta$: $180^\circ$

  • $\cos\theta$: $-1$

  • Physical meaning: Force opposes motion -- negative work

Analogy -- Pushing a shopping cart

If you push a cart horizontally with $50\,\mathrm N$ and it moves $10\,\mathrm m$, you do $W = 50 \times 10 = 500\,\mathrm J$. If you push at $30^\circ$ below horizontal, only the horizontal component counts: $W = 50 \times 10 \times \cos 30^\circ \approx 433\,\mathrm J$.

Work by a variable force

When the force varies with position, work is the signed area under the force component along the path, plotted against position. Area below the axis contributes negative work:

Key equation 5.2
$$\;W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)\,dx\;$$

You can start with geometry: a constant $6\,\mathrm N$ force over $2\,\mathrm m$ gives rectangle area $(6)(2)=12\,\mathrm J$. If the force increases linearly from zero to $6\,\mathrm N$ over the same distance, triangle area gives $W=\frac12(2)(6)=6\,\mathrm J$. TR: İntegral, değişen kuvvetin küçük aralıklardaki işlerini toplar; dikdörtgen ve üçgen alanı bunun basit örnekleridir.

Key definitions for this week

Quantity Symbol Formula Unit
Work by a constant force $W$ $Fd\cos\theta$ J
Work by a variable force $W$ area under $F$–$x$, i.e. $\int F\,dx$ J
Kinetic energy $K$ $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$ J
Power $P$ $W/\Delta t$ or $\vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}$ W

Gravitational and elastic potential energy ($mgh$, $\tfrac12kx^2$) are deliberately absent: they belong to Week 08. TR: Potansiyel enerji formülleri bilerek yok; onlar 8. haftanın konusudur.

The Work-Energy Theorem

Key equation 5.3
$$\;W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = \tfrac12 mv_f^2 - \tfrac12 mv_i^2\;$$
ŞEKİL 5.1

Aynı kuvvet, aynı yer değiştirme, üç farklı açı. Yalnızca hareket yönündeki bileşen iş yapar; bu yüzden işin işareti cos θ'nın işaretidir: hareket yönünde itmek enerji katar, dik kuvvet hiçbir şey yapmaz, harekete karşı kuvvet enerji götürür.

Work accumulates force along displacement

Physical question: how much does a force change the kinetic energy through the object's motion? Draw the displacement arrow, project the force onto it, then multiply that parallel component by the small distance. Add these contributions along the path:

$$dW=\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r,\qquad W=\int F_{\parallel}\,ds.$$

For straight motion along an $x$ axis, this is the signed area under the $F_x$–$x$ graph. A triangular area under a linearly increasing force explains the factor $1/2$; a rectangle describes a constant force. Areas below the axis count negatively for increasing $x$.

Predict. A cable pulls a carriage along a horizontal guide. Keeping cable tension and displacement fixed, tilt the cable farther upward. Does the cable do more work because it “pulls upward too”?

Project onto the actual displacement

Its work decreases as $Fd\cos\theta$: the carriage has no vertical displacement. The guide force can change even though it does no work in this idealised motion. A perpendicular force can change the direction of velocity while doing no work and hence causing no change in speed.

Rate is a separate question. A hoist lifting the same load through the same height in half the time transfers the same gravitational energy but requires twice the average power. Instantaneously $P=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v$. Motor sizing, conveyor energy accounts and lifting process vessels all require this distinction between energy and energy per second.

Kinetic energy: solve for speed one operation at a time

Kinetic energy describes motion: $K=\tfrac12mv^2$, measured in joules ($1\,\mathrm J=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s^2}$). Net work changes it: $$W_{\rm net}=K_f-K_i=\tfrac12mv_f^2-\tfrac12mv_i^2.$$ The word net matters: add the work of all forces first. Positive work increases kinetic energy, negative work decreases it, and a force perpendicular to displacement does zero work.

A 4.0 kg cart starts at 2.0 m/s and receives 24 J of net work. Its initial energy is $K_i=\tfrac12(4)(2^2)=8$ J. Thus $K_f=8+24=32$ J. Solve $32=\tfrac12(4)v_f^2$: $$64=4v_f^2\Rightarrow16=v_f^2\Rightarrow v_f=4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$ We multiplied both sides by 2, divided by 4 kg, and took the positive square root for speed. Energy does not give the direction; use the diagram.

Worked example 5.1

opposite forces do opposite work

A 3.0 kg box already slides to the right at 2.0 m/s. A 12 N horizontal pull and a 3 N friction force act over 4.0 m. Find its final speed.

The pull does $W_{\rm pull}=12(4)\cos0^\circ=48$ J. Friction does $W_f=3(4)\cos180^\circ=-12$ J. Weight and normal are perpendicular to motion and do zero work. $$W_{\rm net}=48-12=36\,\mathrm J,\qquad K_i=\tfrac12(3)(2^2)=6\,\mathrm J.$$ $$K_f=6+36=42\,\mathrm J=\tfrac12(3)v_f^2 \Rightarrow v_f^2=\frac{2(42)}3=28\Rightarrow v_f=5.29\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$ Check by Newton: $a=(12-3)/3=3$ m/s² and $v_f^2=2^2+2(3)(4)=28$ m²/s². Both routes predict the same speed.

Worked Examples

Example 1 -- Work by gravity on a sliding block

A $5\,\mathrm{kg}$ block slides $3\,\mathrm m$ down a frictionless ramp inclined at $30^\circ$. Find the work done by gravity and the final speed (starting from rest).

Paper solution / Kâğıt üzerinde çözüm. Draw the ramp; the distance along it is $3\,\mathrm m$ but the vertical drop is $h=3\sin30^\circ=1.5\,\mathrm m$. Gravity does positive work because the object moves downward. The normal force is perpendicular to motion and does no work.

$$W_g=mgh=(5)(9.81)(1.5)=73.6\ \mathrm J.$$ Starting from rest, $W_{\rm net}=\tfrac12mv^2$ gives $73.6=2.5v^2$. Divide by 2.5 to get $v^2=29.4\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$, then take the square root: $v=5.42\,\mathrm{m/s}$ down the ramp. The mass cancels if we first write $mgh=\tfrac12mv^2$, giving $v=\sqrt{2gh}$. The same vertical drop gives the same speed on any frictionless path under this particle model.

Worked example 5.2

Example 2 -- Power

A motor lifts a $200\,\mathrm{kg}$ crate vertically at a constant speed of $0.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$. What power does the motor deliver?

At constant speed, $a=0$, so the upward motor force equals the weight. The motor still does positive work:

$$\begin{aligned}F_\mathrm{motor}&=mg=(200)(9.81)=1962\,\mathrm N,\\P&=\frac{W}{\Delta t}=F_\mathrm{motor}\frac{\Delta h}{\Delta t}=F_\mathrm{motor}v\\&=(1962\,\mathrm N)(0.50\,\mathrm{m/s})=\boxed{981\,\mathrm W}.\end{aligned}$$

This is delivered mechanical power; electrical power requires a motor efficiency. TR: Sabit sürat, toplam işin sıfır olması demektir. Motor pozitif, yerçekimi aynı büyüklükte negatif iş yapar; motor gücü sıfır değildir.

Power: how quickly is work done?

Average power is $P=W/\Delta t$. At constant velocity with force parallel to motion, $P=Fv$. Power is measured in watts: $1\,\mathrm W=1\,\mathrm{J/s}$.

Key equation 5.4
$$\;\overline P=\frac{W}{\Delta t},\qquad P=\vec F\cdot\vec v\;$$

Returning to Example 2 above, a motor raises a 200 kg crate at constant speed 0.50 m/s. There is no acceleration, so its upward force is $F=mg=200(9.81)=1962$ N. $$P=Fv=(1962\,\mathrm N)(0.50\,\mathrm{m/s})=981\,\mathrm W.$$ In 4.0 s it lifts 2.0 m and does $W=1962(2)=3924$ J; dividing by 4.0 s again gives 981 W. This is mechanical output power; electrical input would require motor efficiency.

Worked example 5.3

Where does the motor’s work go?

Problem. A carriage moves $2.40\,\mathrm m$ at constant $0.300\,\mathrm{m/s}$ against a $7.50\,\mathrm N$ resisting force on a horizontal track. A student says the motor does no work because the kinetic energy does not change. Evaluate that claim and find the motor’s work and mechanical power.

Picture and principle. Draw equal opposing horizontal forces. Constant speed means net work is zero, not that each force does zero work. The motor does $W_m=FL$ and supplies power $P=Fv$; resistance does $-FL$.

Limiting check. If resistance vanishes, this ideal constant-speed motion needs no motor force. If displacement is zero, mechanical work is zero even if a force is present.

Numbers and units. $$W_m=(7.50\,\mathrm N)(2.40\,\mathrm m)=18.0\,\mathrm J,\qquad P=(7.50\,\mathrm N)(0.300\,\mathrm{m/s})=2.25\,\mathrm W.$$

Interpret. The motor transfers energy that is dissipated by resistance. The carriage’s kinetic energy stays unchanged.

Worked example 5.4

Compare two cable routes

Problem. A cable must do $12.0\,\mathrm J$ of work while moving a fixture $0.800\,\mathrm m$ horizontally. Compare required constant tensions when the cable is $20.0^\circ$ and $50.0^\circ$ above horizontal. Which route meets a $20.0\,\mathrm N$ tension limit?

Picture and principle. Draw the horizontal displacement and each force angle. Work is the dot product $W=FL\cos\theta$, so $F=W/(L\cos\theta)$. The vertical component does no work for this displacement.

Limiting check. At $\theta=0$, the required tension is smallest. As the cable approaches vertical, a finite horizontal work requirement needs unbounded tension.

Numbers and units. $$F_{20}=\frac{12.0\,\mathrm J}{(0.800\,\mathrm m)\cos20.0^\circ}=16.0\,\mathrm N,\quad F_{50}=23.3\,\mathrm N.$$

Interpret. Only the shallower route satisfies the tension limit. The same work does not require the same force when its direction changes.

Worked example 5.5

Audit the lifting energy budget

Problem. A hoist raises a $4.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ instrument by $0.600\,\mathrm m$, starting at rest and ending at $0.500\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Neglect resistance. Find the work delivered by the hoist and explain why $mgh$ alone is insufficient.

Picture and principle. Draw an upward displacement with hoist force upward and weight downward. The work–energy theorem gives $W_h-mgh=\Delta K$, hence $W_h=mgh+mv_f^2/2$. No constant-acceleration assumption is needed.

Limiting check. If final and initial speeds match, the kinetic-energy term vanishes and $W_h=mgh$.

Numbers and units. $$W_h=(4.00)(9.81)(0.600)\,\mathrm J+\tfrac12(4.00)(0.500)^2\,\mathrm J=24.0\,\mathrm J.$$ Of this, $0.500\,\mathrm J$ remains as kinetic energy.

Interpret. The height change and the final motion both require energy. Electrical input would be larger if the hoist were inefficient.

TÜRKÇE

Kaldırma işi yalnızca yükseklik artışını değil, kinetik enerji değişimini de karşılar. Elektriksel kayıplar bu mekanik hesaba dahil değildir.

Worked example 5.6

Locate a worn brake pad

Problem. A $0.600\,\mathrm{kg}$ slider enters a horizontal test track at $1.50\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The first $0.300\,\mathrm m$ has a known resisting force of $0.900\,\mathrm N$. It then stops over a second pad of length $0.250\,\mathrm m$. Infer the second pad’s average resisting force.

Picture and principle. Draw two track sections and label their negative work. Since the final kinetic energy is zero, $mv_0^2/2=F_1L_1+F_2L_2$. Isolate $F_2=[mv_0^2/2-F_1L_1]/L_2$.

Limiting check. If the first pad removes all initial energy, no second-pad work is needed. A negative inferred force would contradict the assumed stopping sequence.

Numbers and units. $$F_2=\frac{\tfrac12(0.600)(1.50)^2-(0.900)(0.300)}{0.250}\,\mathrm N=1.62\,\mathrm N.$$

Interpret. The remaining energy after pad one is $0.405\,\mathrm J$. This is an average force inferred from work, not a measurement of force at every point.

TÜRKÇE

Her bölümdeki enerji kaybını ayrı yaz. İşten bulunan kuvvet ortalama değerdir; kuvvetin yol boyunca sabit olduğunu kanıtlamaz.

Worked example 5.7

Average power is not peak power

Problem. A $20.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ trolley accelerates uniformly from $0.500$ to $2.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$ in $3.00\,\mathrm s$ on an ideal horizontal track. Compare the average mechanical power with the power needed at the end. Could a motor capped at $15.0\,\mathrm W$ follow this exact ramp?

Picture and principle. Draw a rising velocity–time line. Constant force is $F=m(v_f-v_i)/t$. Average power is $\Delta K/t=m(v_f^2-v_i^2)/(2t)$, while instantaneous power is $P=Fv$, largest at the end.

Limiting check. With no velocity change, both values are zero in this resistance-free model. At fixed force, power grows with speed.

Numbers and units. $$P_{\rm avg}=\frac{(20.0)(2.00^2-0.500^2)}{2(3.00)}\,\mathrm W=12.5\,\mathrm W,\quad F=10.0\,\mathrm N,\quad P_{\rm end}=20.0\,\mathrm W.$$

Interpret. The cap exceeds the average but not the required peak. The specified constant-acceleration ramp is therefore impossible with that cap.

TÜRKÇE

Ortalama güç motor seçimi için tek başına yeterli değildir. Sabit kuvvette hız arttıkça anlık güç de artar.
