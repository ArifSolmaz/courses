5.1Before you startBaşlamadan önce

Retrieval question: Find net force first. Review $F\cos\theta$ as the force component along a displacement.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session you will be able to:

  1. Define the work done by a constant force, and by a variable force as the area under an $F$–$x$ graph
  2. State and apply the work–energy theorem: $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K$
  3. Calculate kinetic energy and relate it to a change in speed
  4. Determine the sign of the work done by each force acting on a body
  5. Compute average and instantaneous power
  6. Solve a work–energy problem symbolically, check a limiting case, and only then substitute numbers
Watch out

Not this week. Potential energy, conservation of mechanical energy and energy accounting with $U_g$ and $U_s$ are Week 08, after the review week and the midterm. This week every energy statement is about work done by forces and the kinetic energy that results.

Before the equations: energy in small stepsEnerjiyi adım adım anlayalım

Read → draw → calculate → explain. The physics can be completed on paper with a calculator. The plots are experiments to observe; their Python implementation is optional.

Start with a picture of the initial and final states and list every force that acts through the displacement. This week the account has exactly two sides:

$$W_{\rm net}=\Delta K=K_f-K_i, \qquad K=\tfrac12mv^2.$$

Add up the work done by all the forces first — that is what net means — and the total tells you how the kinetic energy changed. Work against the motion is negative and reduces $K$; a force perpendicular to the displacement does no work at all.

Watch out

Not yet. Nothing here needs $mgh$ or $\tfrac12kx^2$. Storing energy in a position rather than in motion is Week 08's idea; keeping the two weeks apart is what makes each of them simple.

TÜRKÇE

Bu haftaki hesabın iki tarafı var: bütün kuvvetlerin yaptığı toplam iş ve kinetik enerjideki değişim. $mgh$ veya $\tfrac12kx^2$'ye burada ihtiyaç yok; enerjiyi konumda depolamak 8. haftanın fikridir.

The algebra bridge. To solve $\tfrac12mv^2=E$:

  1. Multiply both sides by 2: $mv^2=2E$.
  2. Divide both sides by $m$: $v^2=2E/m$.
  3. Take the positive square root for speed: $v=\sqrt{2E/m}$. Energy alone does not tell the direction.

For example, let $E=18\,\mathrm J$ and $m=4\,\mathrm{kg}$:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac12mv^2&=E,\\mv^2&=2E,\\v^2&=\frac{2E}{m}=\frac{2(18\,\mathrm J)}{4\,\mathrm{kg}}=9\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2},\\v&=\sqrt{9\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}=3\,\mathrm{m/s}.\end{aligned}$$ Taking $18/4$ directly as the speed misses both the factor 2 and the square root. Doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy.

The calculus bridge: work is an area / İş bir alandır. You already know how to find the work done by a constant force: force times distance is the area of a rectangle on a graph of $F$ against $x$.

If the force changes as the object moves, the shape under the graph is no longer a rectangle — but it is still an area, and you can still find it. A force rising steadily from $0$ to $6\,\mathrm N$ over $2\,\mathrm m$ traces a triangle, so $W=\tfrac12(2)(6)=6\,\mathrm J$. Cut a more awkward curve into thin vertical strips, each narrow enough that $F$ barely changes across it, and add the little rectangles up.

That adding-up of infinitely thin strips is exactly what the integral sign means:

$$W=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}F(x)\,dx \qquad \textbf{= the signed area under the } F\textbf{–}x \textbf{ graph.}$$

Read it as a sentence, not as a technique: the work is the area under the force–displacement graph. Area below the axis counts as negative work. Every integral in this course can be read as an area, and every derivative as a slope — if you can find an area on graph paper you can do the physics, and the notation is just bookkeeping for a cleverer way of counting the same squares.

How this week is workedBu hafta nasıl çalışılır

Every problem, worked example and experiment in this course follows the same six steps. Step 4 is the one added in September 2026, and it is the one that separates a physicist from a calculator operator.

draw → choose a law → rearrange to the symbolic answer → check a limiting case → substitute with units → interpret

  1. Symbolic answer first. Isolate the unknown in symbols, before any number appears.
  2. One limiting case. Push a parameter to a value whose answer you already know — a mass to zero, an angle to $90^\circ$, a friction coefficient to zero — and confirm the formula behaves sensibly. If it does not, the algebra is wrong and you have just caught it for free, before wasting arithmetic on it.
  3. Then numbers, with units carried through every line.

Why bother: the symbolic route is less arithmetic, not more, and it is where the marks are. A correct symbolic answer with a slip in the final multiplication is nearly full credit; a correct number with no derivation is not.

Workİş

Work by a constant force

When a constant force $\vec{F}$ acts on an object that undergoes displacement $\vec{d}$:

Key equation 5.1
$$\;W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = Fd\cos\theta\;$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and displacement vectors.

$\theta$ $\cos\theta$ Physical meaning
$0^\circ$ $+1$ Force along motion -- positive work
$90^\circ$ $0$ Force perpendicular -- zero work
$180^\circ$ $-1$ Force opposes motion -- negative work

Analogy -- Pushing a shopping cart

If you push a cart horizontally with $50\,\mathrm N$ and it moves $10\,\mathrm m$, you do $W = 50 \times 10 = 500\,\mathrm J$. If you push at $30^\circ$ below horizontal, only the horizontal component counts: $W = 50 \times 10 \times \cos 30^\circ \approx 433\,\mathrm J$.

Work by a variable force

When the force varies with position, work is the signed area under the force component along the path, plotted against position. Area below the axis contributes negative work:

Key equation 5.2
$$\;W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)\,dx\;$$

You can start with geometry: a constant $6\,\mathrm N$ force over $2\,\mathrm m$ gives rectangle area $(6)(2)=12\,\mathrm J$. If the force increases linearly from zero to $6\,\mathrm N$ over the same distance, triangle area gives $W=\frac12(2)(6)=6\,\mathrm J$. TR: İntegral, değişen kuvvetin küçük aralıklardaki işlerini toplar; dikdörtgen ve üçgen alanı bunun basit örnekleridir.

Key definitions for this week

Quantity Symbol Formula Unit
Work by a constant force $W$ $Fd\cos\theta$ J
Work by a variable force $W$ area under $F$–$x$, i.e. $\int F\,dx$ J
Kinetic energy $K$ $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$ J
Power $P$ $W/\Delta t$ or $\vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}$ W

Gravitational and elastic potential energy ($mgh$, $\tfrac12kx^2$) are deliberately absent: they belong to Week 08. TR: Potansiyel enerji formülleri bilerek yok; onlar 8. haftanın konusudur.

The Work-Energy Theorem

Key equation 5.3
$$\;W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = \tfrac12 mv_f^2 - \tfrac12 mv_i^2\;$$
ŞEKİL 5.1

Aynı kuvvet, aynı yer değiştirme, üç farklı açı. Yalnızca hareket yönündeki bileşen iş yapar; bu yüzden işin işareti cos θ'nın işaretidir: hareket yönünde itmek enerji katar, dik kuvvet hiçbir şey yapmaz, harekete karşı kuvvet enerji götürür.

Kinetic energy: solve for speed one operation at a time

Kinetic energy describes motion: $K=\tfrac12mv^2$, measured in joules ($1\,\mathrm J=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s^2}$). Net work changes it: $$W_{\rm net}=K_f-K_i=\tfrac12mv_f^2-\tfrac12mv_i^2.$$ The word net matters: add the work of all forces first. Positive work increases kinetic energy, negative work decreases it, and a force perpendicular to displacement does zero work.

A 4.0 kg cart starts at 2.0 m/s and receives 24 J of net work. Its initial energy is $K_i=\tfrac12(4)(2^2)=8$ J. Thus $K_f=8+24=32$ J. Solve $32=\tfrac12(4)v_f^2$: $$64=4v_f^2\Rightarrow16=v_f^2\Rightarrow v_f=4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$ We multiplied both sides by 2, divided by 4 kg, and took the positive square root for speed. Energy does not give the direction; use the diagram.

Worked example 5.1

opposite forces do opposite work

A 3.0 kg box already slides to the right at 2.0 m/s. A 12 N horizontal pull and a 3 N friction force act over 4.0 m. Find its final speed.

The pull does $W_{\rm pull}=12(4)\cos0^\circ=48$ J. Friction does $W_f=3(4)\cos180^\circ=-12$ J. Weight and normal are perpendicular to motion and do zero work. $$W_{\rm net}=48-12=36\,\mathrm J,\qquad K_i=\tfrac12(3)(2^2)=6\,\mathrm J.$$ $$K_f=6+36=42\,\mathrm J=\tfrac12(3)v_f^2 \Rightarrow v_f^2=\frac{2(42)}3=28\Rightarrow v_f=5.29\,\mathrm{m/s}.$$ Check by Newton: $a=(12-3)/3=3$ m/s² and $v_f^2=2^2+2(3)(4)=28$ m²/s². Both routes predict the same speed.

Worked Examples

Example 1 -- Work by gravity on a sliding block

A $5\,\mathrm{kg}$ block slides $3\,\mathrm m$ down a frictionless ramp inclined at $30^\circ$. Find the work done by gravity and the final speed (starting from rest).

Paper solution / Kâğıt üzerinde çözüm. Draw the ramp; the distance along it is $3\,\mathrm m$ but the vertical drop is $h=3\sin30^\circ=1.5\,\mathrm m$. Gravity does positive work because the object moves downward. The normal force is perpendicular to motion and does no work.

$$W_g=mgh=(5)(9.81)(1.5)=73.6\ \mathrm J.$$ Starting from rest, $W_{\rm net}=\tfrac12mv^2$ gives $73.6=2.5v^2$. Divide by 2.5 to get $v^2=29.4\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$, then take the square root: $v=5.42\,\mathrm{m/s}$ down the ramp. The mass cancels if we first write $mgh=\tfrac12mv^2$, giving $v=\sqrt{2gh}$. The same vertical drop gives the same speed on any frictionless path under this particle model.

Worked example 5.2

Example 4 -- Power

A motor lifts a $200\,\mathrm{kg}$ crate vertically at a constant speed of $0.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$. What power does the motor deliver?

At constant speed, $a=0$, so the upward motor force equals the weight. The motor still does positive work:

$$\begin{aligned}F_\mathrm{motor}&=mg=(200)(9.81)=1962\,\mathrm N,\\P&=\frac{W}{\Delta t}=F_\mathrm{motor}\frac{\Delta h}{\Delta t}=F_\mathrm{motor}v\\&=(1962\,\mathrm N)(0.50\,\mathrm{m/s})=\boxed{981\,\mathrm W}.\end{aligned}$$

This is delivered mechanical power; electrical power requires a motor efficiency. TR: Sabit sürat, toplam işin sıfır olması demektir. Motor pozitif, yerçekimi aynı büyüklükte negatif iş yapar; motor gücü sıfır değildir.

Power: how quickly is work done?

Average power is $P=W/\Delta t$. At constant velocity with force parallel to motion, $P=Fv$. Power is measured in watts: $1\,\mathrm W=1\,\mathrm{J/s}$.

Key equation 5.4
$$\;\overline P=\frac{W}{\Delta t},\qquad P=\vec F\cdot\vec v\;$$

For the module's lifting example, a motor raises a 200 kg crate at constant speed 0.50 m/s. There is no acceleration, so its upward force is $F=mg=200(9.81)=1962$ N. $$P=Fv=(1962\,\mathrm N)(0.50\,\mathrm{m/s})=981\,\mathrm W.$$ In 4.0 s it lifts 2.0 m and does $W=1962(2)=3924$ J; dividing by 4.0 s again gives 981 W. This is mechanical output power; electrical input would require motor efficiency.

Nonzero applied work at constant speed

Problem. A student slides an $80.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ desk $4.00\,\mathrm m$ across a level floor at constant speed. The kinetic friction coefficient is $0.400$. Taking the applied push to be horizontal, find the student's work. Use $g=9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Distinguish one force's work from net work. Constant speed means the desk's kinetic energy does not change, so the net work is zero. It does not mean every force does zero work. The student supplies positive work while friction does equal negative work. Weight and normal force are perpendicular to the horizontal displacement and do no work.

The horizontal push leaves the normal force unchanged:

$$N=mg=(80.0)(9.80)=784\,\mathrm N.$$

Because the desk is sliding, use kinetic friction. Because its speed is constant, the push balances that friction:

$$F_{\rm push}-f_k=0,\qquad F_{\rm push}=f_k=\mu_kN=(0.400)(784)=314\,\mathrm N.$$

Calculate the student's work. The angle between the applied force and displacement is zero:

$$\begin{aligned} W_{\rm student}&=F_{\rm push}d\cos0^\circ,\\ &=\mu_kmgd\\ &=(0.400)(80.0\,\mathrm{kg})(9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(4.00\,\mathrm m)\\ &=1250\,\mathrm J=\boxed{1.25\,\mathrm{kJ}}. \end{aligned}$$

The frictional work is $W_f=-1250\,\mathrm J$. Consequently $W_{\rm net}=W_{\rm student}+W_f=0$, agreeing with $\Delta K=0$. The bank's $1.26\,\mathrm{kJ}$ answer corresponds to using $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, which gives $1260\,\mathrm J$. With our stated $g=9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, the value is $1250\,\mathrm J$, rounded to $1.25\,\mathrm{kJ}$; the difference comes from the chosen gravity value.

Sense check. A larger desk mass, larger friction coefficient, or longer travel distance would increase the required work. The units reduce to $\mathrm{N\,m}=\mathrm J$, rather than newtons alone.

Work uses the angle between two directions

Problem. An airplane travels $120\,\mathrm{km}$ horizontally in a direction $30.0^\circ$ north of east. The net air force is $2.40\,\mathrm{kN}$, directed $10.0^\circ$ south of west. Find the work done by this air force over the displacement.

Put both directions on one angular scale. Measure angles counterclockwise from east. The displacement direction is $30.0^\circ$. West is $180^\circ$, so a direction $10.0^\circ$ south of west is $190^\circ$. The smaller angle between the two vectors is therefore

$$\phi=190^\circ-30.0^\circ=160^\circ.$$

The force nearly opposes the displacement. We should expect negative work before doing any arithmetic. The work of a constant force is the dot product

$$W=\mathbf F\cdot\Delta\mathbf r=Fd\cos\phi.$$

Convert both prefixes. A kilonewton contains $10^3$ newtons, and a kilometre contains $10^3$ metres:

$$F=2.40\times10^3\,\mathrm N,\qquad d=1.20\times10^5\,\mathrm m.$$

Now substitute:

$$\begin{aligned} W&=(2.40\times10^3)(1.20\times10^5)\cos160.0^\circ\ \mathrm{N\,m}\\ &=(2.88\times10^8)(-0.940)\,\mathrm J\\ &=\boxed{-2.71\times10^8\,\mathrm J}. \end{aligned}$$

Interpret the sign. The negative value describes energy removed from the airplane's translational motion by this particular force. It is not automatically the net work on the airplane: engine thrust can also do work. Constant altitude tells us the displacement is horizontal, not that the speed is necessarily constant.

Sense check. The magnitude must not exceed $Fd=2.88\times10^8\,\mathrm J$ because $|\cos\phi|\le1$. Our answer satisfies that bound and has the expected negative sign.

Lifting work includes the final kinetic energy

Problem. A worker lifts a $20.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ bucket from rest at ground level to the top of a $20.0\,\mathrm m$ building. At the top its speed is $4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find the minimum work done by the worker, neglecting dissipative losses and using $g=9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Use the work–kinetic energy theorem directly. Take the bucket as the system. The worker's lifting force does positive work, while gravity does negative work over the upward displacement. For the minimum required work, neglect air resistance and other energy losses:

$$W_{\rm worker}+W_g=K_f-K_i.$$

The bucket starts from rest, so $K_i=0$. Gravity points opposite its upward displacement, giving

$$W_g=mgh\cos180^\circ=-mgh.$$

Isolate the worker's contribution. Insert the negative gravitational work, then add $mgh$ to both sides:

$$\begin{aligned} W_{\rm worker}-mgh&=\frac12mv_f^2,\\ W_{\rm worker}&=mgh+\frac12mv_f^2. \end{aligned}$$

Evaluate the two parts separately so their meanings remain visible:

$$\begin{aligned} mgh&=(20.0)(9.80)(20.0)\,\mathrm J=3920\,\mathrm J,\\ \frac12mv_f^2&=\frac12(20.0)(4.0)^2\,\mathrm J=160\,\mathrm J,\\ W_{\rm worker}&=3920+160=\boxed{4080\,\mathrm J=4.08\,\mathrm{kJ}}. \end{aligned}$$

Interpret and check. The worker must both counter gravity's negative work and leave the bucket with kinetic energy. A result of $3920\,\mathrm J$ would correspond to reaching the top with zero speed. Our answer is larger by exactly the final kinetic energy. This method needs the starting and ending speeds, not the detailed speed at every intermediate height.

TÜRKÇE

Yerçekiminin işi yukarı çıkışta negatiftir. Denklemdeki $-mgh$ terimini karşıya almak, iki tarafa da $mgh$ eklemek demektir. Son hız sıfır verilmediğinden $\tfrac12mv_f^2$ terimini atamayız. Burada yalnızca kuvvetlerin işi ve kinetik enerji değişimini kullanıyoruz.

Find friction from the distance needed to stop

Problem. A $5.00\,\mathrm{kg}$ box initially slides at $3.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$ on a level floor and stops after $4.00\,\mathrm m$. If kinetic friction is the only horizontal force, determine its coefficient. Use $g=9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Account for the work. The normal force and weight are perpendicular to the displacement, so neither does work. Friction opposes motion throughout the slide and removes the box's initial kinetic energy. Because the floor is level and there is no extra vertical force, $N=mg$ and $f_k=\mu_kmg$.

The work–kinetic energy theorem is therefore

$$W_f=K_f-K_i,\qquad -\mu_kmgd=0-\frac12mv_i^2.$$

Simplify without losing the signs. Both sides are negative. Multiply the complete equation by $-1$; then divide by the nonzero mass:

$$\begin{aligned} \mu_kmgd&=\frac12mv_i^2,\\ \mu_kgd&=\frac12v_i^2,\\ \mu_k&=\frac{v_i^2}{2gd}. \end{aligned}$$

Now substitute the measured quantities:

$$\mu_k=\frac{(3.00\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2(9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2})(4.00\,\mathrm m)} =\frac{9.00}{78.4}=\boxed{0.115}.$$

Interpret the result. A friction coefficient is dimensionless. The given mass disappears from this idealized stopping-distance relation, but the actual friction force still depends on mass. Here $f_k=\mu_kmg=5.63\,\mathrm N$ before rounding.

Sense check. The initial kinetic energy is $\tfrac12(5.00)(3.00)^2=22.5\,\mathrm J$. The frictional work is $-(5.63)(4.00)=-22.5\,\mathrm J$, exactly what is needed to stop. If the same initial speed stopped over a larger distance, the inferred coefficient would be smaller.

TÜRKÇE

Negatif iş ile azalan kinetik enerji birbirine uygundur; eksi işaretleri hatalı değildir. İki tarafı birden $-1$ ile çarpınca ikisi de pozitif olur. Kütle yalnızca bütün terimlerde ortak çarpan olduğu için sadeleşir. Sonuca newton veya joule birimi yazma; $\mu_k$ birimsizdir.

Average engine power from a kinetic-energy increase

Problem. A $1500\,\mathrm{kg}$ car accelerates from rest to $25\,\mathrm{m/s}$ in $7.0\,\mathrm s$. Neglect friction and air resistance. Find the average useful engine power in watts and horsepower, using $1\,\mathrm{hp}=746\,\mathrm W$.

Choose an energy calculation. Power describes how rapidly work is done. Under the stated loss-free model, the engine's useful work becomes the car's translational kinetic energy. We therefore need the change in kinetic energy and the elapsed time, rather than the detailed acceleration history:

$$\overline P=\frac{W}{\Delta t}=\frac{\Delta K}{\Delta t}.$$

Since the initial speed is zero,

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta K&=\frac12m(v_f^2-v_i^2)\\ &=\frac12(1500\,\mathrm{kg})\left[(25\,\mathrm{m/s})^2-0\right]\\ &=468{,}750\,\mathrm J. \end{aligned}$$

Divide by the full time interval.

$$\overline P=\frac{468{,}750\,\mathrm J}{7.0\,\mathrm s} =66{,}964\,\mathrm W\approx\boxed{67\,\mathrm{kW}}.$$

To convert watts into horsepower, multiply by a conversion factor equal to one:

$$\overline P=(66{,}964\,\mathrm W) \left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{hp}}{746\,\mathrm W}\right) =89.8\,\mathrm{hp}\approx\boxed{90\,\mathrm{hp}}.$$

Interpret and check. This is an interval average, not necessarily the instantaneous power at the end. For constant acceleration, the force would be constant while instantaneous power $P=Fv$ rises with speed. Using the final speed in $Fv$ would then give twice the interval average. No assumption of constant acceleration was needed for the energy-based result above.

TÜRKÇE

Önce hızın karesini alarak kinetik enerji değişimini hesapla, sonra zamana böl. Watt, joule bölü saniyedir. Beygir gücüne çevirirken $746$ ile bölmemizin nedeni, her bir beygir gücünün $746$ watt içermesidir. Ortalama güç ile yolculuğun son anındaki güç aynı kavram değildir; verilen süre ortalamayı belirler.
