Start here · Last week’s key question

Why cosine here, but sine there?

Choose the geometry before choosing the function. Multiplying the vector’s magnitude by cosine gives its signed component along the direction from which the angle is measured. Multiplying by sine gives the transverse component in the chosen plane. A dot product uses the along-part; a cross-product magnitude uses the perpendicular height.

In this explorer, $\mathbf A=4\hat{\imath}$ and $|\mathbf B|=5$, in centimetres. Blue A is the base. Rust B rotates. The green horizontal leg is $B_x$; the purple vertical leg is $B_y$. Follow Next and Back through the guided lesson. Switching the angle reference does not move B.

1 · Derive components from a right triangle

Start with an acute angle $\alpha$ measured from +x. B is the hypotenuse. The horizontal leg is adjacent to $\alpha$, and the vertical leg is opposite it. The definitions of the trigonometric ratios give:

$$\begin{aligned}\cos\alpha&=\frac{B_x}{B},& B_x&=B\cos\alpha,\\\sin\alpha&=\frac{B_y}{B},& B_y&=B\sin\alpha.\end{aligned}$$

For $B=5.00$ cm and $\alpha=30.0^\circ$, $B_x=4.33$ cm and $B_y=2.50$ cm. Check: $4.33^2+2.50^2\approx5.00^2$. The slight discrepancy comes from rounding.

Why these ratios? Similar right triangles have the same side ratios for the same angle. Changing B scales both legs equally; it does not change sine or cosine. The identity $\cos^2\alpha+\sin^2\alpha=1$ is Pythagoras divided by $B^2$.

2 · Change the angle reference, not the vector

If $\beta$ is measured clockwise from +y toward B, then $\beta=90^\circ-\alpha$. Now the y-leg is adjacent and the x-leg is opposite. The very same components become:

$$\begin{aligned}B_x&=B\sin\beta,\\B_y&=B\cos\beta.\end{aligned}$$

At $\alpha=30^\circ$, $\beta=60^\circ$: $5\cos30^\circ=5\sin60^\circ=4.33$ and $5\sin30^\circ=5\cos60^\circ=2.50$. Try the reference buttons: the arc and formulas change, but the vector, components and products stay fixed.

Do not memorize “x means cosine.” Identify the angle’s starting axis. With a small unsigned reference angle in another quadrant, assign component signs from the drawing. With the directed angles used here, sine and cosine supply those signs automatically.

3 · Derive the dot product from projection

For any nonzero A, choose the unit direction $\hat{\mathbf A}=\mathbf A/A$. The signed length of B along it is $B\cos\phi$, where $\phi$ is the included angle between A and B, from 0° to 180°.

$$\begin{aligned}B_{\parallel A}&=\mathbf B\cdot\hat{\mathbf A}=B\cos\phi,\\\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B&=A B_{\parallel A}=AB\cos\phi.\end{aligned}$$

In the explorer A points along +x, so $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=4B_x$. At 30°, this is $4(5\cos30^\circ)=17.3$ cm². At 120°, the projection points against A: $B_x=-2.50$ cm and the dot product is −10.0 cm².

The coordinate formula follows from the same geometry: matching unit axes have dot product 1; perpendicular unit axes have dot product 0. Expanding the components leaves $A_xB_x+A_yB_y+A_zB_z$.

The scalar product is signed. A negative result means an opposing component, not a negative geometric area. A projection vector, if needed, is $(B\cos\phi)\hat{\mathbf A}$.

4 · Derive the cross product from perpendicular height

Translate the vectors to form a parallelogram. Its base is A. Its height is the perpendicular distance to the opposite side, not the slanted length B:

$$\begin{aligned}h&=B\sin\phi,\\\mathcal A&=Ah=AB\sin\phi,\\|\mathbf A\times\mathbf B|&=AB\sin\phi.\end{aligned}$$

Here $0\leq\phi\leq180^\circ$, so the height is nonnegative. At 30°, $h=2.50$ cm, so area and cross-product magnitude are $4(2.50)=10.0$ cm². The product’s direction is perpendicular to the plane, determined by the right-hand rule.

For the explorer’s signed planar rotation $\alpha$, distinguish the signed z-component from magnitude:

$$\begin{aligned}(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)_z&=AB\sin\alpha=4B_y,\\|\mathbf A\times\mathbf B|&=AB|\sin\alpha|.\end{aligned}$$

At −30°, the z-component is −10.0 cm² (into the screen), but area is +10.0 cm². Swapping the vector order reverses the cross product. It does not reverse geometric area.

5 · Test limits, then connect to the general component formulas
  • 0°: all of B is along A. Dot = +20 cm²; height and cross product = 0.
  • 90°: none of B is along A. Dot = 0; height = 5 cm; cross-product magnitude = 20 cm².
  • 180°: B points against A. Dot = −20 cm²; height and cross product = 0.
  • −90°: dot = 0; cross-product z-component = −20 cm²; magnitude = +20 cm².
  • A zero vector: both products vanish, but there is no included angle. The main question explorers below let you test this.

If A makes angle $\theta_A$ and B makes angle $\theta_B$ with +x, expand their components and use the angle-difference identities:

$$\begin{aligned}A_xB_x+A_yB_y&=AB\cos(\theta_B-\theta_A),\\A_xB_y-A_yB_x&=AB\sin(\theta_B-\theta_A).\end{aligned}$$

This connects the geometric and component methods. For Question 1, neither vector lies on +x: use the angle between them, not B’s angle to the page. In 3D, the dot product still measures projection and the cross product still supplies a perpendicular area vector.

6 · Can a dot product use sine? Check your angle!

Yes, if the given angle is complementary to the included angle. The geometric definition has not changed. In our explorer, $\phi=|\alpha|$ and $\alpha=90^\circ-\beta$, so:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B&=AB\sin\beta,\\(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)_z&=AB\cos\beta.\end{aligned}$$

These formulas use the specifically defined directed angle $\beta$ from +y, not the included angle. Thus “dot always means press cosine of whatever angle is given” is wrong. First relate the supplied angle to the geometry.

Decision routine: mark the given angle → identify its reference directions → decide whether you need a signed projection or perpendicular height → write the symbolic relation → test 0°/90° → substitute numbers with the calculator in degree mode.

Try without looking at the derivation

A 10 cm vector is 20° east of north. Which component uses cosine?

North is the angle’s reference direction, so $B_y=10\cos20^\circ=9.40$ cm and $B_x=10\sin20^\circ=3.42$ cm. Both are positive. For a 2 cm vector A pointing east, $\phi=70^\circ$: dot $=20\cos70^\circ=6.84$ cm² and cross $=20\sin70^\circ\hat{k}=18.8\hat{k}$ cm². Equivalently use $20\sin20^\circ$ and $20\cos20^\circ$, respectively.

At 120° from +x, must both products be negative?

No. With A = 4 cm along +x and B = 5 cm, dot $=20\cos120^\circ=-10.0$ cm². Cross $=20\sin120^\circ\hat{k}=+17.3\hat{k}$ cm². B points partly backward but still lies above the x-axis.

Question 1 · Vectors in a plane

Two robot moves: how much is along, how much is across?

A positioning robot makes two measured moves on a horizontal board:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf A&=(3.00\hat{\imath}+4.00\hat{\jmath})\ \mathrm{cm},\\\mathbf B&=(-2.00\hat{\imath}+6.00\hat{\jmath})\ \mathrm{cm}.\end{aligned}$$

East is +x, north is +y, and +z points out of the board. Each component was recorded to 0.01 cm; use three significant figures for the reported vector-product results. The centimetre-to-metre conversion is exact.

  1. Identify scalars and vectors; convert the moves to SI units. Estimate their lengths.
  2. Find the final displacement $\mathbf R=\mathbf A+\mathbf B$, its length and direction, and the total distance of the two moves.
  3. Find $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B$, the angle between the moves, and the signed component of B along A.
  4. Find $\mathbf A\times\mathbf B$, its direction, and the area of the parallelogram formed by A and B at a common origin.
  5. Predict and explain the parallel, perpendicular, opposite, reversed-order and zero-vector cases.
1 · Model, draw and estimate

Represent each straight move by a displacement vector; ignore the robot’s size. Position depends on direction, so A, B and R are vectors. Length, distance, angle and area are scalars; the dot product is also a scalar, though it may be negative.

A points northeast, B northwest. To add them, move the tail of B to the head of A without rotating it. The endpoint should be almost due north. For products, draw both tails at the same origin, as in the animation.

Estimate: A is about 5 cm long and B about 6 cm. Their product should be of order $30\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, so an answer of thousands of square metres would fail immediately.

2 · Convert units and find the two lengths

Use $1\ \mathrm{cm}=10^{-2}\ \mathrm m$. This is an exact SI-prefix conversion and introduces no additional measurement uncertainty.

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf A&=(0.0300,0.0400,0)\ \mathrm m,\\\mathbf B&=(-0.0200,0.0600,0)\ \mathrm m.\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}A&=\sqrt{3.00^2+4.00^2}\ \mathrm{cm}\\&=5.00\ \mathrm{cm},\\B&=\sqrt{(-2.00)^2+6.00^2}\ \mathrm{cm}\\&=\sqrt{40}\ \mathrm{cm}\approx6.32\ \mathrm{cm}.\end{aligned}$$

Check: the square root of a sum of squared lengths is a length. Squaring a negative component gives a positive contribution to the magnitude. Keep $\sqrt{40}$ unrounded for the next steps.

3 · Add vectors; distinguish distance from displacement $$\begin{aligned}\mathbf R&=(3.00-2.00,\ 4.00+6.00,\ 0)\ \mathrm{cm}\\&=(1.00,10.00,0)\ \mathrm{cm},\\|\mathbf R|&=\sqrt{1.00^2+10.00^2}\ \mathrm{cm}\\&=\sqrt{101}\ \mathrm{cm}\approx10.05\ \mathrm{cm}.\end{aligned}$$

Component sums retain the 0.01 cm decimal place. The resultant length is shown to that same approximate absolute precision.

$$\theta_R=\operatorname{atan2}(10.00,1.00)\approx84.3^\circ.$$

This is counterclockwise from east, or 5.71° east of north. Both components are positive, so the direction belongs in quadrant I. Use degrees on the calculator.

$$d=A+B=5.00+\sqrt{40}\approx11.32\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

Check: $|B-A|\leq|\mathbf A+\mathbf B|\leq A+B$. Here $1.32\leq10.05\leq11.32$ cm. Total distance is longer than displacement because the two moves are not in the same direction.

4 · Scalar product: multiply matching components

The dot product measures how strongly the vectors point along one another. It combines matching components and produces one number, not a direction.

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B&=A_xB_x+A_yB_y+A_zB_z\\&=(3.00)(-2.00)+(4.00)(6.00)\\&=-6.00+24.0\\&=\boxed{18.0\ \mathrm{cm^2}}.\end{aligned}$$ $$18.0\ \mathrm{cm^2}=18.0(10^{-2}\ \mathrm m)^2=\boxed{1.80\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2}}.$$

Check: both vectors have dimension L, so their dot product has dimension L². Convert square centimetres with $10^{-4}$, not $10^{-2}$. The northward agreement outweighs the east–west opposition, so the answer is positive.

5 · Angle and signed projection

Use the geometric definition to recover the angle. This step requires both vectors to be nonzero.

$$\begin{aligned}\cos\phi&=\frac{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B}{AB}\\&=\frac{18}{5\sqrt{40}},\\\phi&=\boxed{55.3^\circ}.\end{aligned}$$

The positive dot product correctly predicts an acute angle. For the projection, first build a unit vector:

$$\hat{\mathbf A}=\frac{\mathbf A}{A}=0.600\hat{\imath}+0.800\hat{\jmath}.$$ $$\begin{aligned}B_{\parallel A}&=\mathbf B\cdot\hat{\mathbf A}\\&=\frac{18.0\ \mathrm{cm^2}}{5.00\ \mathrm{cm}}\\&=\boxed{3.60\ \mathrm{cm}},\\\mathbf B_{\parallel A}&=B_{\parallel A}\hat{\mathbf A}\\&=(2.16,2.88,0)\ \mathrm{cm}.\end{aligned}$$

The first projection is a signed scalar; the second is a vector. The remaining part is $\mathbf B_\perp=(-4.16,3.12,0)$ cm. Check that $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B_\perp=3(-4.16)+4(3.12)=0$.

6 · Vector product: area and orientation

In the xy-plane, the cross product has only a z-component. The order matters.

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf A\times\mathbf B&=(A_xB_y-A_yB_x)\hat{k}\\&=[3.00(6.00)-4.00(-2.00)]\hat{k}\\&=\boxed{26.0\hat{k}\ \mathrm{cm^2}}\\&=\boxed{2.60\times10^{-3}\hat{k}\ \mathrm{m^2}}.\end{aligned}$$

Curl the right-hand fingers from A toward B through the smaller angle: the thumb points out of the board, +z. The animation uses ⊙ for out and ⊗ for into the board.

$$\mathcal A_{\rm parallelogram}=|\mathbf A\times\mathbf B|=\boxed{26.0\ \mathrm{cm^2}}.$$

Geometric check: the perpendicular height relative to A is $26/5=5.20$ cm. Base × height gives $5.00(5.20)=26.0$ cm². The triangle made by the same edges has half this area, 13.0 cm².

7 · Edge cases: predict, animate, explain

Keep lengths $A=5.00$ cm and $B=\sqrt{40}$ cm. Let $K=AB=10\sqrt{10}\approx31.6$ cm². For these planar vectors use the signed rotation $\alpha$ from A to B:

$$\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=K\cos\alpha,\qquad (\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)_z=K\sin\alpha.$$
  • Same direction, 0°: dot $=+K$, cross $=0$. Projection is all of B; the parallelogram collapses to a line.
  • Counterclockwise 90°: dot $=0$, cross $=+K\hat{k}$. No component along A; maximum area.
  • Opposite, 180°: dot $=-K$, cross $=0$. Antiparallel is not perpendicular.
  • Clockwise 90°: dot $=0$, cross $=-K\hat{k}$. Area is still positive K; orientation has reversed.
  • Reverse the order: $\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf A=18.0$ cm², but $\mathbf B\times\mathbf A=-26.0\hat{k}$ cm².
  • Equal vectors: $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A=A^2=25.0$ cm², whereas $\mathbf A\times\mathbf A=\mathbf0$.
  • B = 0: both products vanish and projection onto nonzero A is zero. The angle with the zero vector is undefined: do not divide by $AB=0$.

Final independent check: $18^2+26^2=1000=(5^2)(40)$. This is $(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B)^2+|\mathbf A\times\mathbf B|^2=A^2B^2$.

Question 2 · Vectors in space

A tilted triangular panel: which way does its normal point?

Two edges from the same corner of a small triangular panel are measured as

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf a&=(2.00,-1.00,2.00)\ \mathrm{mm},\\\mathbf b&=(1.00,2.00,2.00)\ \mathrm{mm}.\end{aligned}$$

The instrument may have a common scale error of ±1.0%: all components of both vectors are multiplied by the same positive factor $s$ between 0.990 and 1.010. Ignore other errors for this model. The axes are right-handed; the panel is idealized as flat.

  1. Convert to SI units; find edge lengths and the third edge $\mathbf b-\mathbf a$.
  2. Find the scalar product, included angle and signed projection of b along a.
  3. Find the vector product, triangle area and an oriented unit normal. Verify perpendicularity.
  4. Explain what swapping the edge order, rotating coordinates, parallel edges and a zero edge do.
  5. Determine how the common scale uncertainty affects lengths, areas, products and angles. Report sensible precision.
1 · Interpret the model and convert the units

Two nonparallel edges define a plane. Their cross product will supply a perpendicular direction; its magnitude will supply twice the triangle area. A photograph alone cannot give the 3D angle reliably because projection distorts angles.

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf a&=(2.00,-1.00,2.00)\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm m,\\\mathbf b&=(1.00,2.00,2.00)\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm m.\end{aligned}$$

Estimate: both edges are a few millimetres long, so the area should be a few mm², or a few $10^{-6}$ m². The product of two lengths has dimension L²; a unit normal has no units.

2 · Lengths, subtraction and the third side $$\begin{aligned}a&=\sqrt{2^2+(-1)^2+2^2}=3.00\ \mathrm{mm},\\b&=\sqrt{1^2+2^2+2^2}=3.00\ \mathrm{mm},\\\mathbf b-\mathbf a&=(-1.00,3.00,0)\ \mathrm{mm},\\|\mathbf b-\mathbf a|&=\sqrt{10}\ \mathrm{mm}\approx3.16\ \mathrm{mm}.\end{aligned}$$

The vector b − a goes from the tip of a to the tip of b. Reversing that order reverses direction, not length. The three side lengths satisfy the triangle inequality: $3.16<3.00+3.00$.

3 · Dot product, angle and projection $$\begin{aligned}\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b&=2(1)+(-1)(2)+2(2)\\&=2-2+4\\&=\boxed{4.00\ \mathrm{mm^2}}\\&=4.00\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}.\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}\phi&=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{4}{3\cdot3}\right)\\&=\boxed{63.6^\circ},\\b_{\parallel a}&=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{a}\\&=\boxed{1.33\ \mathrm{mm}}.\end{aligned}$$

Check: the cosine argument is dimensionless and between −1 and +1. Although the y-components oppose, the total dot product is positive, so the angle is acute. A negative component does not by itself imply an obtuse angle.

4 · Compute every cross-product component

Use the cyclic component rule. Writing all three components avoids accidentally using a two-dimensional shortcut.

$$\begin{aligned}c_x&=a_yb_z-a_zb_y\\&=(-1)(2)-(2)(2)=-6,\\c_y&=a_zb_x-a_xb_z\\&=(2)(1)-(2)(2)=-2,\\c_z&=a_xb_y-a_yb_x\\&=(2)(2)-(-1)(1)=5.\end{aligned}$$ $$\boxed{\mathbf c=\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=(-6.00,-2.00,5.00)\ \mathrm{mm^2}}.$$

Right-hand interpretation: c is perpendicular to the panel, with negative x, negative y and positive z components. There is no single “upward” component that replaces the full 3D direction.

5 · Area and unit normal; two independent checks $$\begin{aligned}|\mathbf c|&=\sqrt{36+4+25}\ \mathrm{mm^2}\\&=\sqrt{65}\ \mathrm{mm^2},\\\mathcal A_\triangle&=\tfrac12|\mathbf c|\\&=\boxed{4.03\ \mathrm{mm^2}}\\&=\boxed{4.03\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}},\\\hat{\mathbf n}&=\frac{(-6,-2,5)}{\sqrt{65}}\\&\approx(-0.744,-0.248,0.620).\end{aligned}$$

The normal has no units and length one. Its orientation follows the chosen order a then b.

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf a&=(-6)(2)+(-2)(-1)+5(2)=0,\\\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf b&=(-6)(1)+(-2)(2)+5(2)=0.\end{aligned}$$

Both dot products vanish, so c is perpendicular to both edges. An independent magnitude check gives $a^2b^2-(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)^2=81-16=65=|\mathbf c|^2$.

6 · Edge cases and orientation

For the fixed 3 mm lengths, the dot product is $9\cos\phi$ mm² and cross-product magnitude is $9\sin\phi$ mm² for $0\leq\phi\leq180^\circ$.

  • Parallel: dot = +9 mm², cross = 0, triangle area = 0.
  • Perpendicular: dot = 0, cross magnitude = 9 mm², triangle area = 4.50 mm².
  • Opposite: dot = −9 mm², cross = 0. Zero cross product alone cannot distinguish same from opposite direction.
  • Nearly parallel, 1°: dot ≈ 8.999 mm², cross magnitude ≈ 0.1571 mm² and area ≈ 0.07854 mm². The area approaches zero continuously. A tiny independent error in direction can strongly affect the normal near this limit.
  • Swap a and b: the scalar product and area stay unchanged; the normal reverses to $-\hat{\mathbf n}$.
  • Zero edge: both products and area vanish; the included angle is undefined. A zero cross product cannot be normalized, so there is no uniquely defined panel normal.
  • Rotate the view or rotate right-handed coordinate axes: the panel’s area and included angle stay unchanged. Component numbers may change under a coordinate rotation, while the geometric vectors remain the same. The view slider changes only the camera.

Important distinction: for nonzero parallel edges, the angle is 0° or 180° but the plane normal is still undefined; for a zero edge, even the included angle is undefined.

7 · Propagate the common scale error

Do not assume independent errors: the problem specifies one shared calibration factor. Write $\mathbf a'=s\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b'=s\mathbf b$.

$$\begin{aligned}a'&=sa,\\\mathbf a'\cdot\mathbf b'&=s^2(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b),\\\mathbf a'\times\mathbf b'&=s^2(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b),\\\mathcal A'&=s^2\mathcal A.\end{aligned}$$

Length changes by ±1.0%. Products and area change by approximately ±2.0%. The exact area bounds use $0.990^2=0.9801$ and $1.010^2=1.0201$:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal A_{\min}&=0.9801\frac{\sqrt{65}}2\\&=3.95091\ldots\ \mathrm{mm^2},\\\mathcal A_{\max}&=1.0201\frac{\sqrt{65}}2\\&=4.11215\ldots\ \mathrm{mm^2}.\end{aligned}$$

A suitable approximate report is $\boxed{\mathcal A=(4.03\pm0.08)\ \mathrm{mm^2}}$ for this calibration-only uncertainty; the exact bounds above are slightly asymmetric. Keeping many calculator digits does not make a measurement more accurate.

$$\cos\phi'=\frac{s^2\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{(sa)(sb)}=\cos\phi.$$

The angle is unchanged, as is the unit normal for positive s. A common scale error changes size, not shape or orientation. This would not generally hold for independent component errors.

Before you leave

Explain why a zero dot product and a zero cross product mean different things. State the nonzero-vector condition before using either result to infer an angle. Check units, magnitude and orientation before accepting a calculator answer.
