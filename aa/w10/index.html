Lecture notes: How does knowing the goal change the search?

A warehouse robot must reach its charging station. Dijkstra already finds the minimum energy cost. But the robot also knows the station's coordinates. Must we explore every cheap cell in the opposite direction? A* (A-star) uses a reliable estimate of remaining cost to guide search toward the goal.

The aim is not to make the robot physically drive faster, but to understand how the same mathematical route problem may require less computation. Recall earlier weeks, calculate g, h and f by hand, experiment with the maps, then measure in the Python lab.

Suppose one candidate has g=9, h=1, f=10; another has g=1, h=3, f=4. Selecting only proximity (h) favors the first; A* selects the second. g and h must use the same cost units: we do not add joules to metres without scaling. h is an estimate of remaining cost, not energy already spent.

Same best route, less searching?

A warehouse robot knows which charging station it wants. Dijkstra explores cheap routes in every direction. Can we give useful preference to routes heading toward that station without accepting a more expensive answer? A* keeps the same cost objective and changes the order in which candidates are examined. Here “faster” means planning time; it does not mean the robot physically drives faster.

Remember: Week 9 · Keep the cost, change the priority

On S999G / .###. / ....., Dijkstra found the lower route: eight moves, cost 8, instead of four moves costing 28. A* must still find cost 8 when its heuristic is valid. Dijkstra orders candidates by g; A* orders them by g+h.

Remember: Week 8 · Fewest moves is a different objective

BFS chose the four-move top route because each move counted equally. If every move costs 1, fewest moves and minimum cost agree. On weighted terrain they can disagree; A* keeps Dijkstra’s minimum-total-cost objective.

Remember: Week 7 · The heap returns the smallest priority

A heap containing priorities 10 and 4 returns 4 first. Here these are (f=10, cell=(0,1), g=9) and (f=4, cell=(1,0), g=1). Keep g separately: the stale-entry check compares stored route costs, not f values. Our tuple order is (f, cell, g); ties use row then column.

In A*, g is what the path has already cost, h is an estimate of the cost still needed, and f = g + h is the queue priority. Manhattan distance counts the required horizontal and vertical moves. Multiply it by a lower bound on the cost of one legal move.

A valid heuristic never overstates the true remaining cost. In this grid each move changes Manhattan distance by at most one, and the scaled estimate changes by no more than the cost of a move. Setting h to zero makes the priority g and recovers Dijkstra’s order with the same tie rule.

The direct-looking neighbor has a smaller h but a much larger g. The lower route goes down, right, right and up for cost 4, while the direct route costs 10. On another map A* may pop fewer cells than Dijkstra, while computing h still has a cost.

Calculate Manhattan distance by counting moves

h(r,c) = m × (|r−rG| + |c−cG|)

m is a nonnegative lower bound on every legal move cost. From (1,0) to G=(0,2), at least one vertical move and two horizontal moves are necessary: distance 3. With m=1, h=3. On the small map, g=1 at that cell, so f=1+3=4. An obstacle may force extra moves, but ignoring it cannot make this estimate too large.

Remember: Week 4 · FIFO no longer chooses the next cell

If a queue receives right then down, FIFO returns right first. In this example their f values are 10 and 4, so the priority queue returns down first. Neighbor generation order and frontier extraction order are separate choices.

Two conditions that make the estimate trustworthy

Admissible: 0≤h(v)≤the true cheapest remaining cost, with h(G)=0. For (1,0) above, h=3 equals the remaining cost 3. At S, h=2 is below the actual best cost 4; being optimistic is allowed.

Consistent: for every legal edge u→v, h(u)≤cost(u,v)+h(v). One four-direction move changes Manhattan distance by at most 1, so multiplying by m changes the estimate by at most m, which is no larger than the move’s cost. For (1,0)→(1,1): 3≤1+2. Consistency with h(G)=0 implies admissibility along every path to G.

With this consistent heuristic, a current minimum-f pop settles that cell’s g. Skip stale entries before checking the goal, and stop when the goal is popped, not when first inserted. The code allows improved cells to be queued again; it has no permanent closed set. That reopening matters if you later use an admissible but inconsistent heuristic. It does not rescue an overestimating heuristic from an incorrect early goal result.

Remember: Week 1 · A stopping rule needs a correctness argument

A scan of [2,9,12] for any value above 8 may stop at 9. Finding the cheapest route asks for the best answer, not any answer. A* may stop on a current goal pop only under the heuristic and reopening conditions just described.

Derive Manhattan distance and a safe heuristic from first principles

Let the goal be (0,4) and the robot be at (2,0). The vertical difference is |2−0|=2, and the horizontal difference is |0−4|=4. Four-direction movement must reduce these differences separately: at least 2+4=6 moves. Absolute value removes the direction sign. Obstacles can force detours but cannot reduce this lower bound.

If every transition costs at least m energy units, six moves cost at least 6m units. Thus h(r,c)=m(|r−rG|+|c−cG|). For m=2, h=12; for m=0, h=0. Now h and g have the same units.

Two distinct correctness conditions

Admissibility: 0≤h(u)≤h*(u), where h*(u) is the true minimum remaining cost. For an unreachable goal the true cost is infinity. h(G)=0.

Consistency: Every legal u→v satisfies h(u)≤w(u,v)+h(v). Manhattan distance decreases by at most one in a four-direction move. Therefore h(u)−h(v)≤m≤w(u,v). The inequality holds. This proves consistency of the scaled Manhattan heuristic used here.

Consistency together with h(G)=0 implies admissibility: sum the inequalities along a path, cancel intermediate h terms, and h(u) cannot exceed path cost. Consistency also makes f nondecreasing along any path: the new f=g(u)+w(u,v)+h(v)≥g(u)+h(u). Therefore a settled vertex does not need to be expanded again with a consistent heuristic.

Why can we stop at the first current goal pop?

Let C* be the optimal cost. If an optimal path has not yet been completed, some frontier candidate on it has f≤C* (when reached through an optimal prefix, h cannot exceed its true remaining cost). The heap selects the smallest f. At the goal h(G)=0, so f=g; if its cost exceeded C*, that other candidate would have been selected first. This contradiction explains optimality. The lesson code queues improved g values again; reopening is important when using an admissible but inconsistent heuristic.

Watch A* avoid unnecessary directions

Both methods find cost 3. Dijkstra pops seven cells before stopping; A* pops only the four cells on the top row. The order depends on the specified tie rule. Run this standalone example, then set goal to (3,3) and predict whether the saving remains. Remove or revise the final assertions when changing the goal.

from heapq import heappop, heappush

def search(use_heuristic):
    # A fixed, open 4 x 4 grid: every move costs 1.
    start, goal = (0, 0), (0, 3)
    def h(cell):
        distance = abs(cell[0] - goal[0]) + abs(cell[1] - goal[1])
        return distance if use_heuristic else 0

    heap = [(h(start), start, 0)]
    costs = {start: 0}
    parents = {start: None}
    popped = []
    while heap:
        priority, cell, cost = heappop(heap)
        if cost != costs[cell]:
            continue
        popped.append(cell)
        if cell == goal:
            path = []
            while cell is not None:
                path.append(cell)
                cell = parents[cell]
            return cost, popped, path[::-1]
        r, c = cell
        for dr, dc in [(-1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (0, -1)]:
            neighbor = (r + dr, c + dc)
            nr, nc = neighbor
            if 0 <= nr < 4 and 0 <= nc < 4:
                candidate = cost + 1
                if candidate < costs.get(neighbor, float("inf")):
                    costs[neighbor] = candidate
                    parents[neighbor] = cell
                    heappush(heap, (candidate + h(neighbor), neighbor, candidate))

for name, enabled in [("Dijkstra", False), ("A*", True)]:
    cost, pops, path = search(enabled)
    print(name, "cost:", cost, "pops:", len(pops))
    print("Order:", pops)
    print("Path:", path)
    assert cost == 3
    assert len(pops) == (4 if enabled else 7)

For goal (3,3) on the same open grid, every cell has g+h=6 when reached optimally. With row-then-column ties, both methods pop all 16 cells before that goal. A perfect remaining-distance estimate does not eliminate all work among equal-f candidates. A different tie rule can change the count without changing the optimal cost.

Remember: Week 5 · Two dictionaries preserve the route

costs[(0,2)] = 2 stores the best known g, while parents[(0,2)] = (0,1) records its predecessor. If a candidate g=3 arrives later, reject it because 3 is not smaller than 2. Follow parents from G and reverse the chain; the heap itself is not the path.

The lab's scaled_manhattan(grid) finds the minimum traversable entry cost and returns a heuristic function that remembers it. This closure avoids rescanning the entire map for each neighbor. The weighted function is a common search engine: h=0 gives Dijkstra, automatically scaled h gives A*. Its name does not refer to the separate Weighted A* approximation method.

Less search is useful; measure the whole job

Remember: Week 2 · Separate growth from one measurement

A scan over n entries grows linearly, while checking every pair grows quadratically. On this four-neighbor grid E≤4V. With a consistent heuristic, constant-time h, and expected constant-time dictionary lookup, the lazy-heap search has an O(V log V) worst-case time bound and O(V) auxiliary space. A* may inspect fewer cells in practice; it does not promise a better worst-case class than Dijkstra here.

V counts free cells and E legal directed moves. Input validation and finding the minimum entry cost also scan all R×C cells, so include O(RC) preprocessing. The heap can hold several entries for one cell, and parents/costs consume memory even for cells absent from the returned path. Inconsistent heuristics can cause re-expansions; the consistent-heuristic bound above must not be applied to that case without further analysis.

Remember: Week 3 · A heap logarithm is not binary search on the map

Binary search in [10,20,30,40,50,60,70,80] repeatedly halves a sorted interval. A heap push/pop follows a logarithmic-height tree to maintain the smallest priority. A* uses the heap operation; it never assumes the map’s cells are sorted by distance.

Remember: Week 6 · The median summarizes timing samples

Sorting [7,2,3,2,100] gives [2,2,3,7,100], whose median is 3. The lab reports the median of five batch-average times and the largest batch average. Neither statistic guarantees a worst-case deadline for an individual call. Always compare correctness first, then search counts and elapsed time.

Calculate complexity and actual search savings

Let V count traversable vertices and E legal directed edges. With consistent h, each vertex settles at most once and each outgoing edge is scanned at most once. Each successful improvement adds a heap entry, with at most E+1 pushes including the start. Old entries may add pop overhead. Thus a safe general time bound is O((V+E) log(V+E+1)), and auxiliary space is O(V+E).

In a four-direction grid E≤4V, so search takes O(V log(V+1)) time and O(V) space. Manhattan evaluation is constant-time; validation and the minimum-cost scan add O(RC) preprocessing. The full function is bounded by O(RC+V log(V+1)). Inconsistent heuristics may cause re-expansions, so do not apply this analysis directly to them.

from heapq import heappop, heappush

DIRECTIONS = [(-1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (0, -1)]


def validate_grid(grid, start, goal):
    if not grid or not grid[0] or any(len(row) != len(grid[0]) for row in grid):
        raise ValueError("Use a nonempty rectangular grid")
    if any(symbol not in ".SG#0123456789" for row in grid for symbol in row):
        raise ValueError("Use only . S G # and single digits 0-9")
    for row, col in (start, goal):
        if not (0 <= row < len(grid) and 0 <= col < len(grid[0])):
            raise ValueError("Endpoint outside grid")
        if grid[row][col] == "#":
            raise ValueError("Endpoint is blocked")


def neighbors(grid, cell):
    row, col = cell
    for dr, dc in DIRECTIONS:
        r, c = row + dr, col + dc
        if 0 <= r < len(grid) and 0 <= c < len(grid[r]) and grid[r][c] != "#":
            yield r, c


def entry_cost(grid, cell):
    row, col = cell
    symbol = grid[row][col]
    return int(symbol) if symbol in "0123456789" else 1


def result(grid, parents, goal, popped):
    path = [goal]
    while parents[path[-1]] is not None:
        path.append(parents[path[-1]])
    path.reverse()
    return len(path) - 1, sum(entry_cost(grid, cell) for cell in path[1:]), popped, path

def weighted(grid, start, goal, heuristic):
    validate_grid(grid, start, goal)
    if heuristic is None:
        heuristic = scaled_manhattan(grid)
    frontier = [(heuristic(start, goal), start, 0)]
    costs = {start: 0}
    parents = {start: None}
    popped = 0
    while frontier:
        _, cell, cost = heappop(frontier)
        if cost != costs[cell]:
            continue
        popped += 1
        if cell == goal:
            return result(grid, parents, goal, popped)
        for neighbor in neighbors(grid, cell):
            candidate = cost + entry_cost(grid, neighbor)
            if candidate < costs.get(neighbor, float("inf")):
                costs[neighbor] = candidate
                parents[neighbor] = cell
                priority = candidate + heuristic(neighbor, goal)
                heappush(frontier, (priority, neighbor, candidate))
    return None, None, popped, []


def zero(cell, goal):
    return 0

def manhattan(cell, goal):
    return abs(cell[0] - goal[0]) + abs(cell[1] - goal[1])

def scaled_manhattan(grid):
    minimum = min(
        entry_cost(grid, (r, c))
        for r, row in enumerate(grid)
        for c, symbol in enumerate(row)
        if symbol != "#"
    )
    def heuristic(cell, goal):
        return minimum * manhattan(cell, goal)
    return heuristic


def astar(grid, start, goal):
    return weighted(grid, start, goal, None)


def check_path(grid, start, goal, answer):
    moves, cost, popped, path = answer
    if not path:
        assert moves is None and cost is None
        return
    assert path[0] == start and path[-1] == goal
    assert moves == len(path) - 1
    for a, b in zip(path, path[1:]):
        assert b in neighbors(grid, a)
    assert cost == sum(entry_cost(grid, cell) for cell in path[1:])

Boundary cases have different meanings

For ('S',) with start=goal=(0,0), astar returns (0,0,1,[(0,0)]): a valid zero-move route. For ('S#G',), start=(0,0), goal=(0,2), it returns (None,None,1,[]): a free but unreachable goal. Choose the right heuristic for a real robot

Manhattan is natural for equal-length four-direction moves. If a diagonal move from (0,0) to (1,1) is permitted at cost 1, Manhattan says 2 and is no longer safe. For equally priced eight-direction moves, max(|Δr|,|Δc|) can serve as a lower bound. Euclidean distance can provide another lower bound with an appropriate physical-distance cost scale; verify it against the legal movements of the model.

With robot heading, turning energy, speed and clearance, a state may contain more than its cell. Ignoring turn costs may preserve a lower bound, provided actual transitions still respect the relevant bound. Moving obstacles violate the fixed-map assumption and require replanning.

Multiplying h by an arbitrary large factor may make search more aggressive but lose the optimality guarantee. For example, h=4 with true remaining cost 1 is unsafe. On a zero-cost map, m=0 gives safe h=0 but no directional guidance. Validate the heuristic before comparing performance.

The direct route costs 3 and the lower route costs 1. At the right neighbor f = 1 + 2 = 3. At the lower neighbor f = 0 + 4 = 4, although only 1 unit remains on the optimal route. The unscaled heuristic can lead to cost 3. Multiplying by minimum step cost zero makes h = 0, and Dijkstra finds cost 1.

At (1,0), the remaining route costs only 1, yet unscaled Manhattan says 4. The heuristic overestimates, so the goal-pop guarantee is lost. Multiplying by m=0 restores a valid bound: A* becomes Dijkstra and finds cost 1. A zero anywhere in the map makes this simple global scaling conservative, even if the zero is irrelevant to the best route. It does not prove that every possible useful heuristic must be zero.
