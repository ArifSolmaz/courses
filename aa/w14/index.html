Week 14 / 14 · Traveling salesperson, heuristics and P versus NP

What if I cannot find an exact tour fast enough?

15 min · Engineering question

The robot must visit four stations for pick-and-place and return to its dock. The route should be short and planning time matters. With many stations, trying all orders can exceed the budget.

Read the concept and worked example first. Then make an individual prediction and compare it with a partner.

The decision version asks whether a tour fits budget B. This version of TSP is NP-complete for integer distance tables. P is contained in NP. Checking one proposed tour quickly leaves open whether every instance can be solved quickly.

Before you start

A route to one goal is different from a tour that visits every stop and returns to the depot.

With a fixed depot and k other stops there are k! visit orders before exploiting symmetry. An exact search checks all those orders or uses a rule that can safely discard some. At larger k, a heuristic can often produce a usable tour within the available planning time.

Nearest neighbor repeatedly chooses the closest unvisited stop. It can make an early choice that forces a long final return. A 2-opt move reverses an internal segment and replaces two links. For symmetric distances, the internal links keep their lengths when reversed.

With a budget of 15 the improved tour is a valid witness. Verify that each stop occurs once, the tour returns to A, and the total fits. A search that has only found tours costing more than 15 has not established that none can fit.

Define the tour before choosing an algorithm

Let n count all stations, including depot A. A tour starts at A, visits every other station exactly once, and returns to A. Thus n=4 means three non-depot stops and four traveled links. [A,B,C,D,A] is valid; [A,B,C,D] is missing the return, and [A,B,B,D,A] omits C.

The classroom model uses a complete, symmetric distance table: every pair can be connected, and d(A,B)=d(B,A). Euclidean distances also satisfy the triangle inequality: going directly cannot be longer than going via an intermediate point. Real one-way aisles, time windows, capacity limits and pickup rules require additional modeling.

Optimization: find the smallest tour cost. Decision: does any valid tour cost at most B? A route of cost 14 answers “yes” for B=15, but says nothing by itself about whether B=13 is possible.

Remember: Week 8 · One destination is different from visiting all stops

BFS can find a two-move route from (0,0) to (1,1) on an open unit-cost grid. A tour must also decide when to visit every required station and return. Repeating a shortest-path query alone does not optimize their visit order.

Remember: Week 9 · Shortest paths can supply the distance table

Dijkstra compares a candidate 2+1=3 with saved cost 5 and improves it. In a warehouse with walls, use shortest traversable path costs between stations instead of straight-line distances. Then solve the visit-order problem on that table; pairwise optimal paths do not imply an optimal overall order.

Remember: Week 11 · Precedence adds another constraint

For boot→scan→map, scan cannot precede boot. If a robot must pick up at B before delivering at C, [A,C,B,D,A] violates that rule even though it visits all stops. Plain TSP has no pickup-before-delivery constraint; its checker must be extended for that task.

Why trying every order becomes expensive

Fix A first. There are n−1 choices for the next station, n−2 for the next, and so on: (n−1)! orders. For four stations the six orders are BCD, BDC, CBD, CDB, DBC and DCB, each enclosed by A at both ends.

On the 3×4 rectangle, their costs are 14,16,18,16,18,14. The minimum is 14, so exhaustive checking proves optimality for this small instance. Reverse tours pair up at the same cost because distances are symmetric; this halves the distinct candidates for n≥3 if the algorithm explicitly removes reversals. The lab does not remove them.

Remember: Week 2 · Factorial growth is a different scale

Doubling a linear scan from 100 to 200 roughly doubles its work. Adding one station to a fixed-depot tour changes 7!=5040 orders into 8!=40320: eight times as many candidates, before any per-tour work.

Nearest neighbor: a useful start, not a proof

At each step, compare distances from the current station to all unvisited stations. Choose the smallest, mark that station visited, and continue. After the last stop, add the return link. In the code, a tie goes to the smaller station index, so relabeling tied stations can change the result.

For A=(0,0), B=(3,0), C=(3,4), D=(0,4): from A compare B:3, C:5, D:4 and choose B. From B compare C:4, D:5 and choose C. Then go to D for 3 and back to A for 4. Total: 3+4+3+4=14.

A four-station counterexample on a line

Put C at −2, A at 0, B at 1 and D at 5. Starting at A, nearest neighbor goes A→B→C→D→A: 1+3+7+5=16. The tour A→C→B→D→A costs 2+3+4+5=14. Any closed tour must span from −2 to 5 and back, so it costs at least 2×7=14. Here we have both an achieving tour and a matching lower bound.

Remember: Week 6 · One global sort cannot replace changing distances

Sorting [9,1,5] gives [1,5,9] and median 5. Nearest-neighbor priorities change with the current station: from A, B is 3 away and D is 4; after reaching B, C is 4 away and D is 5. A single initial sort does not update those priorities.

Remember: Week 7 · A heap chooses a minimum, not a globally best tour

A min-heap with priorities 3 and 5 returns 3 first. That local operation is correct, but choosing the closest next stop can still give the 16-cost line tour below while an optimum costs 14. A data structure does not supply the missing greedy proof.

2-opt: change two links and reverse the middle

Use the crossed tour A→C→B→D→A on the 3×4 rectangle. Its links cost 5+4+5+4=18. Remove A–C and B–D, then add A–B and C–D. Reverse the middle segment C→B into B→C so the result is one tour, A→B→C→D→A.

The change in cost is Δ=d(A,B)+d(C,D)−d(A,C)−d(B,D)=3+3−5−5=−4. The unchanged links B–C and D–A each cost 4, so the new total is 14. A negative Δ means an improvement.

For symmetric distances, reversed internal links have unchanged cost, so this four-edge calculation is enough. With asymmetric distances, reversing C→B can change its cost too; the four-edge shortcut is then invalid without accounting for the interior. The teaching implementation recomputes each full candidate tour for clarity.

Read the Python slice

For route=[0,2,1,3,0], use i=1 and j=2. route[:i] is [0], route[i:j+1][::-1] is [1,2], and route[j+1:] is [3,0]. Concatenation gives [0,1,2,3,0]. Both depot entries stay fixed.

The starter scans every candidate reversal, remembers the cheapest strict improvement, applies it, and scans again. A complete scan with no improvement gives a local optimum for these moves, not necessarily a global optimum. Stopping early because of a pass or time limit does not even establish local optimality. The floating-point lab treats changes smaller than 10⁻⁹ as ties.

Remember: Week 4 · Store the whole order

A FIFO containing A,B,C removes A first, whereas a stack removes C. A tour is an ordered list: [A,C,B,D,A] and [A,B,C,D,A] contain the same stations but cost 18 and 14 on our 3×4 rectangle.

Run a small example with exact integer costs

This standalone program uses the 3×4 rectangle’s integer distance table. Call check_matrix for every new matrix before using the routines. It rejects empty, nonsquare, negative, noninteger or asymmetric tables. Duplicate coordinates are allowed as separate station IDs. For a single depot, [0,0] is the zero-cost tour.

The verifier checks IDs, count, coverage and return before summing costs. improve performs at most ten full 2-opt scans by default; this is a work limit, not a strict elapsed-time deadline. exact has a teaching limit of nine total stations to prevent accidental factorial runs. This limit is a classroom safeguard, not a mathematical limit of exact methods.

from itertools import permutations


def check_matrix(d):
    n = len(d)
    if n == 0 or any(len(row) != n for row in d):
        raise ValueError("Use a nonempty square distance matrix")
    if any(type(x) is not int or x < 0 for row in d for x in row):
        raise ValueError("Distances must be nonnegative integers")
    if any(d[i][i] != 0 for i in range(n)):
        raise ValueError("Diagonal distances must be zero")
    if any(d[i][j] != d[j][i] for i in range(n) for j in range(n)):
        raise ValueError("This example requires symmetric distances")


def valid_tour(d, route):
    n = len(d)
    return (len(route) == n + 1 and route[0] == route[-1] == 0
            and all(type(v) is int and 0 <= v < n for v in route)
            and set(route[:-1]) == set(range(n)))


def tour_cost(d, route):
    if not valid_tour(d, route):
        raise ValueError("Visit every station once and return to depot 0")
    return sum(d[a][b] for a, b in zip(route, route[1:]))


def nearest(d):
    remaining = set(range(1, len(d)))
    route = [0]
    while remaining:
        nxt = min(remaining, key=lambda v: (d[route[-1]][v], v))
        route.append(nxt)
        remaining.remove(nxt)
    return route + [0]


def improve(d, route, max_passes=10):
    # Stop after at most max_passes full scans, not a wall-clock deadline.
    route = list(route)
    tour_cost(d, route)  # Validate before slicing.
    for _ in range(max_passes):
        best = route
        best_cost = tour_cost(d, route)
        for i in range(1, len(route) - 2):
            for j in range(i + 1, len(route) - 1):
                candidate = route[:i] + route[i:j + 1][::-1] + route[j + 1:]
                candidate_cost = tour_cost(d, candidate)
                if candidate_cost < best_cost:
                    best, best_cost = candidate, candidate_cost
        if best is route:
            break
        route = best
    return route


def exact(d):
    if len(d) > 9:
        raise ValueError("Teaching limit: exact search allows at most 9 stations")
    return min(([0, *order, 0] for order in permutations(range(1, len(d)))),
               key=lambda route: tour_cost(d, route))

d = [[0, 3, 5, 4], [3, 0, 4, 5], [5, 4, 0, 3], [4, 5, 3, 0]]
check_matrix(d)  # Validate each new matrix before using the routines.
crossed = [0, 2, 1, 3, 0]
better = improve(d, crossed)
optimum = exact(d)
print("Crossed:", tour_cost(d, crossed))

print("Exact:", tour_cost(d, optimum))
print("Budget 14:", tour_cost(d, better) <= 14)
print("Duplicate valid:", valid_tour(d, [0, 1, 1, 3, 0]))
assert tour_cost(d, crossed) == 18
assert tour_cost(d, better) == tour_cost(d, optimum) == 14

# Collinear stations A=0, B=1, C=-2, D=5.

line = [[abs(a - b) for b in x] for a in x]

Remember: Week 3 · A returned position still needs checking

Binary search on [2,5,8] for 6 returns insertion index 2; checking 8≠6 reveals a miss. Likewise, a planner returning a list does not make it a valid tour: [A,B,B,D,A] omits C and repeats B.

Remember: Week 5 · Use membership to reject repeated stops

A set built from [A,B,B,D] has only three entries. A visited set detects the second B immediately. Check the list length and the full expected station set as well; membership alone is not a route-order check.

What can the result actually prove?

A valid candidate has cost U, so OPT≤U: it is an upper bound on the unknown minimum. A proven lower bound L gives L≤OPT. If L=U, optimality is certified. If U≤B, the budget is feasible. If L>B, it is infeasible. If L≤B<U, the bounds alone leave the answer unknown.

Example: with L=26, U=28 and B=30, the tour is feasible but may be up to 2 cost units above the optimum. With the same bounds and B=25, infeasibility is proved. With B=27, the answer remains unresolved. If U=32 is merely the best candidate found for B=30, report “no qualifying tour found within the search limit,” not “impossible.”

Heuristic versus approximation guarantee

A heuristic is a strategy for obtaining useful candidates. An approximation guarantee is a proved bound under stated assumptions. For a complete symmetric metric TSP, doubling an MST, walking it and shortcutting repeated vertices gives a tour costing at most twice OPT: doubling costs 2×MST≤2×OPT, and triangle inequality makes shortcuts no longer. This guarantee belongs to that construction under those assumptions; it does not make an arbitrary 2-opt result a proven optimum.

Remember: Week 10 · A lower bound is not a candidate tour

A* uses f=g+h; g=2,h=3 gives f=5 as a lower-bound estimate when h is admissible. Similarly, a TSP lower bound of 12 and a valid tour of 14 imply 12≤OPT≤14. The bound narrows the answer but is not itself a route.

Remember: Week 12 · A spanning tree can give a lower bound

Kruskal connects all stations without requiring a return tour. For the 3×4 rectangle, AB=3, CD=3 and AD=4 form an MST of cost 10. Deleting a link from any tour leaves a spanning path, so with nonnegative weights MST≤OPT. The 14-cost perimeter gives 10≤OPT≤14; this alone does not prove 14 optimal.

Can dynamic programming help with tours?

Yes, but the state becomes much larger. Let S be a subset of non-depot stations and j its last visited station. D[S,j] is the cheapest path starting at A, visiting exactly S, and ending at j. Initialize D[{j},j]=d(A,j). Then use D[S,j]=min over i in S without j of D[S without j,i]+d(i,j). Finish by minimizing D[all,j]+d(j,A).

For S={B,C} in our rectangle, D[{B,C},C]=3+4=7, while D[{B,C},B]=5+4=9. The same visited set needs separate answers for its different endpoints. Subset size gives an acyclic computation order. The standard subset DP takes O(n²2ⁿ) time and O(n2ⁿ) space under unit-cost arithmetic: much better than factorial enumeration, but still exponential. This explains why “use DP” does not automatically mean “polynomial time.”

Remember: Week 13 · The state must remember which stops remain

Grid DP stored D[r,c]; the paper grid’s goal cost was 4. A tour state needs (visited set, last station): being at B after visiting {A,B} differs from being at B after {A,B,C}. Different remaining stops mean different future problems.

P and NP: finding versus checking

These classes describe decision problems using the size of the encoded input. P contains problems decidable in polynomial time. NP contains problems whose yes answers have certificates checkable in polynomial time. A tour is such a certificate for “cost at most B?” when distances and B are explicitly encoded integers. Checking it does not require checking every other tour.

P⊆NP; whether P=NP remains open. NP does not mean “not polynomial,” and NP-complete does not mean every small instance is difficult. The integer-table TSP decision problem is NP-complete; finding an optimal tour is an NP-hard optimization problem. We have not proved that every exact algorithm must enumerate every permutation.

The Euclidean lab uses floating-point distances as a numerical model. Do not confuse rounded output with an exact certificate for arbitrary real-valued distances. The integer-matrix example avoids that distinction for its budget checks. See the Clay Mathematics Institute explanation of P versus NP.

Read the measured code, not only the algorithm name

Nearest neighbor scans n−1, n−2, …, 1 candidates, for Θ(n²) distance comparisons. The lab’s exhaustive solver evaluates (n−1)! routes, each with n links: Θ(n·(n−1)!) time. It generates orders lazily rather than storing every tour.

Each 2-opt pass considers Θ(n²) reversals, but this simple code copies and scores each candidate in Θ(n) time. One pass therefore takes Θ(n³), and p passes take O(pn³). An implementation using the symmetric four-edge Δ can score a candidate in O(1), but that is not what this lab measures. Do not claim a fixed total number of improvement passes.

In the lab, n includes the depot. For n=6, exhaustive search evaluates 5!=120 orders; for n=8, 7!=5040. Exact reference costs rounded to three decimals are 26.116 and 30.501. The “2-opt” timing includes constructing its nearest-neighbor initial tour. Timed calls include allocation and distance calculations; the station list and printed cost calculation are outside each timed call. All measurements are normalized per call. A largest batch average is not a deadline guarantee.

Remember: Week 1 · Count what the machine actually checks

A first-hit scan of [2,7,9] for ≥7 makes two comparisons. A tour checker instead examines station IDs and all n links, including the last return. Counting only the outward links misses part of the work and cost.

45 min

Measure it

Time nearest neighbor, nearest neighbor plus 2-opt, and exhaustive search on deterministic point sets of 6 and 8 stations. Compare costs and times. Before running, predict which methods to keep for 30 stations and why.

Select Run or copy the code into Python 3. The two times are the median and largest of five batch averages, in milliseconds per call. The largest batch average does not bound an individual call.

from statistics import median
from timeit import repeat


def measure(label, job, repeats=200):
    samples = repeat(job, repeat=5, number=repeats)
    typical = median(samples) * 1000 / repeats
    longest = max(samples) * 1000 / repeats
    print(f"{label:18}  {typical:.6f} ms  {longest:.6f} ms")

from itertools import permutations
from math import dist


def length(points, route):
    return sum(dist(points[a], points[b]) for a, b in zip(route, route[1:]))


def nearest(points):
    remaining = set(range(1, len(points)))
    route = [0]
    while remaining:
        last = route[-1]
        next_stop = min(remaining, key=lambda i: (dist(points[last], points[i]), i))
        route.append(next_stop)
        remaining.remove(next_stop)
    return route + [0]


def two_opt(points, route):
    while True:
        best = route
        best_cost = length(points, route)
        for i in range(1, len(route) - 2):
            for j in range(i + 1, len(route) - 1):
                candidate = route[:i] + route[i:j + 1][::-1] + route[j + 1:]
                candidate_cost = length(points, candidate)
                if candidate_cost + 1e-9 < best_cost:
                    best, best_cost = candidate, candidate_cost
        if best is route:
            return route
        route = best


def exact(points):
    orders = ((0, *order, 0) for order in permutations(range(1, len(points))))
    return min(orders, key=lambda route: length(points, route))


stations = [(0, 0), (1, 2), (3, 1), (8, 0), (7, 7), (2, 5), (5, 4), (1, 7)]
for n in (6, 8):
    points = stations[:n]
    methods = [("nearest", nearest), ("2-opt", lambda p: two_opt(p, nearest(p))), ("exact", exact)]
    for name, method in methods:
        print(n, name, "->", round(length(points, method(points)), 3))
        measure(f"{n}: {name}", lambda: method(points), repeats=3)

Choose a planner under a deadline

The workcell has a fixed depot and eleven other stops. One evaluated order takes 20 microseconds. The planner has 100 ms and the tour must cost at most 30.

Start individually for 10 minutes. Work with a partner for 20. Spend the last 15 comparing with another pair and revising your recommendation.

  1. Estimate the number of orders and time for exhaustive search with a fixed depot. Determine how many orders fit in the allocation.
  2. Choose a practical strategy using nearest neighbor, bounded 2-opt or both. State what you report if a candidate costs 28 and what you report if the best candidate found costs 32.
  3. Write an acceptance checklist and one comparison on a smaller instance where exact search is practical.
Compare a model answer

There are 11! = 39,916,800 orders, about 798 seconds at the stated rate. Only 5,000 fit in 100 ms. A valid cost-28 tour meets the budget. A cost-32 best-so-far result gives no feasible witness and does not establish impossibility. Report the best valid candidate and the search limit.

A practical deadline plan

The studio’s 11!×20 μs is 798.336 seconds, about 13.3 minutes, against a 100 ms allowance. At that idealized fixed rate, 100 ms/20 μs=5000 orders fit, before other overhead. Even removing reversed duplicates is nowhere near enough.

Build and validate an initial tour, keep the best valid candidate, and improve while a planning allowance remains. Reserve time for final validation and returning the result. A pass cap bounds work but not wall-clock latency; a real deadline needs checks within long loops and allowances for system delays. The uncapped starter lab is not a real-time controller.

Report the candidate route and cost, budget, planning time, stopping reason and any proven lower bound. Keep “budget met,” “budget proved impossible,” and “not resolved within the limit” separate. On small held-out station sets, compare with exact search and report quality gaps as well as runtimes. Include ties, repeated coordinates, a single depot and malformed tours in checks.

Course takeaway: first define the question and assumptions, then choose the state and data structure, justify the algorithm, validate the result, and measure the work that actually runs.

Exam-style questions

Spend 15 minutes on the three questions and two of the extra drills without help. Use the last 10 to check answers and rewrite one explanation.

  1. Which segment do you reverse to uncross it?

    Show answer

    C–B becomes B–C.

  2. What would you check for a proposed tour under budget B?

    Show answer

    Each station occurs once, the tour returns to its start, and the summed distance is at most B.

  3. How do P and NP differ in the decision version?

    Show answer

    P means a decision can be made in polynomial time. NP means a yes answer has a certificate checkable in polynomial time.

More practice

  1. How many orders are tried for four stations when A is fixed first?

    Show answer

    3! = 6. With symmetric distances, reversal pairs represent the same tour cost.

  2. In the first paper example A=(0,0), B=(3,0), C=(3,4), D=(0,4), which station is nearest to A?

    Show answer

    B at distance 3.

  3. On the first paper example’s 3×4 rectangle, what is the cost of A→C→B→D→A?

    Show answer

    5 + 4 + 5 + 4 = 18.

  4. If 2-opt finds no improving swap, is the tour necessarily optimal?

    Show answer

    No. It is locally optimal for that swap rule.

  5. Does fast verification tell you whether P equals NP?

    Show answer

    No. The question of whether every such decision problem also has a polynomial-time solver is open.
