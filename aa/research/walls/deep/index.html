The walls that fell

For decades, two classic problems seemed stuck at their textbook speed. In October 2026 both got faster, by an exponent. This site shows how the old algorithms work, why they looked unbeatable, and how the new ones get past them. Every idea has something you can click.

Part 1 · How the classic algorithms work

Two puzzles

Both are easy to state and easy to solve slowly. The question was always: how fast can you go?

Puzzle 1: 3SUM

You get a list of n numbers. Do any three of them add up to exactly 0? The smart textbook method sorts the list, fixes one number, and walks two fingers inward from both ends of the rest. If the sum is too small, the left finger moves right. If it’s too big, the right finger moves left.

Puzzle 2: All-pairs shortest paths (APSP)

You get a map of n cities with road lengths. What’s the shortest route between every pair? Floyd–Warshall (1962) asks one question over and over: “Is it shorter to go from the start city to the end city through a middle city?” An example from the map below: is going from Q to R through P shorter than the current Q → R? It asks this for every middle city and every start–end pair.

Part 2 · Where n² and n³ come from

Counting the steps

“n²” isn’t a guess. It’s what you get when you count how many times the innermost step runs.

3SUM, two fingers

Fixing the 1st number leaves at most n − 2 finger moves, the 2nd at most n − 3, and so on down to 1. Add them up (Gauss’s sum):

(n−2) + (n−3) + … + 1 = (n−1)(n−2)/2 ≈ n²/2

For 6 numbers: 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 moves, half of a square.

APSP, three loops

The check runs once for every middle city, every start city and every end city. Each check is 1 addition and 1 comparison:

n × n × n = n³ checks
for via in cities:
  for start in cities:
    for end in cities:
      d[start][end] = min(d[start][end], d[start][via] + d[via][end])

Derivation 1: why brute force is n(n−1)(n−2)/6 ≈ n³/6

a

Pick the first number: n ways. The second: n − 1 ways (it must be different). The third: n − 2 ways. That gives n(n−1)(n−2) ordered picks.

b

But (−7, 3, 4), (3, −7, 4), (4, 3, −7)… are the same triple. Three numbers can be ordered in 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 ways, so divide by 6.

c

For the list in Part 1 (n = 6): 6 × 5 × 4 / 6 = 20 triples. Multiply it out and the biggest term is n³/6.

Derivation 2: why two fingers is (n−1)(n−2)/2 ≈ n²/2

live from Part 1

When a number is fixed, the fingers walk over the k numbers to its right. Every step moves one finger inward by one, and they stop when they meet, so they make at most k − 1 steps. Here is that count for your list, row by row (run the walk in Part 1 to fill the last column):

The sum (n−2) + (n−3) + … + 1 is Gauss’s sum: write it forwards and backwards, add the two rows, and each column gives n − 1. There are n − 2 columns, so twice the sum is (n−1)(n−2).

(n−1)(n−2)/2 = (n² − 3n + 2)/2 = n²/2 − 1.5n + 1

For n = 1,000: 500,000 − 1,500 + 1 = 498,501. The n²/2 term alone is within 0.3% of the total, and gets closer as n grows. That is why we just say “n²”: constants like ½ and smaller terms like 1.5n are dropped.

Doubling n multiplies n² by 2² = 4 and n³ by 2³ = 8. The exponent is exactly this ratio: the “2” in n² means “double the input, 2² times the work”. An n1.9992 algorithm gives 21.9992 ≈ 3.998× instead.

Part 3 · Why nobody could do better

Why it looked impossible

Two real proofs said “you can’t beat n² / n³”. Both were correct, but only for algorithms that follow certain rules.

Game: beat the sneaky friend (the n² proof)

interactive

Your friend built a list of 6 numbers where every pair is almost a hit: a + b + c is 0.001 or −0.001. Click pairs to check them. When you think you’re done, answer “no triple adds to 0”. Your friend may nudge any pair you skipped to exactly 0.

Skip even one pair and you can lose. That’s Erickson’s 1999 argument in miniature: an algorithm that only asks “is a + b + c positive, negative or zero?” must ask about ≈ n² of them.

Every trick that makes multiplication faster than n³ (Strassen, 1969) computes too much and then subtracts the extra. Shortest paths use “smallest of” instead of “plus”, and you can’t subtract a minimum. Kerr (1970) proved it: using only + and min, you need about n³ steps.

The new algorithms don’t follow those rules. They ask about exact sums and use algebra with subtraction, so neither proof applies.

Part 4 · Alman & Vassilevska Williams, October 2026

Claude’s approach

The core algorithm was found by an internal Anthropic research model, credited in the paper as Claude. Josh Alman (Columbia) and Virginia Vassilevska Williams (MIT) understood it, simplified it, extended it and wrote it up.

How it was found

An Anthropic employee was using an internal research model on open problems in cryptography, including constructions based on the hardness of Zero-k-Clique. The model found the algorithm during an unattended session. Anthropic shared it with the authors in September 2026.

How it was checked

The paper was posted to arXiv on 5 October 2026. Anthropic then certified the main deterministic results in the Lean 4 proof assistant and published the formal proof on GitHub. Within weeks, two independent groups found simpler proofs (Part 5).

The idea in four moves

Leave the “smallest-of” world, where subtraction is impossible. Turn both puzzles into one exact-sum problem about triangles. Then compute only the answers that are actually needed.

1

Exact sums are algebra-friendly

interactive

Write each number as a power of x and multiply: multiplying adds exponents, so one product counts every pair sum at once. This is ordinary arithmetic, with subtraction, so fast multiplication tricks work.

2

Shrink big numbers with remainders

interactive

The power trick is only fast for small numbers, so replace each number by its remainder after dividing by a prime p. True sums survive. Fake ones can appear, so every hit is double-checked.

Question: does 1003 + 2017 equal c?
3

Both puzzles are triangle hunts

Three numbers that add to 0, or a detour start → middle city → end that beats the direct road: both are three things that fit together, a triangle. The general version, Exact Triangle (“find a triangle whose weights add to exactly a target”), is at least as hard as both. Speed it up and both puzzles speed up.

4

The new part: only fill the cells you need

interactive

Counting triangles through pairs is a table product: each cell counts common friends. Fast multiplication tricks compute every cell, and just writing an N × N table costs N². Here the table is thin (N × D times D × N, D small) and only a few cells are asked for. The new algorithm prunes the fast-multiplication recipe (built on an identity of Schönhage and a 1982 method of Coppersmith) so it computes only what those cells need, and shares that work.

In the paper: at most N²/√D cells are asked, and they cost about N² / D0.063 operations, less than writing the whole table.

Why a small saving lowers the exponent

interactive

The same mechanism, in Strassen’s 1969 algorithm: split a table into 2 × 2 blocks and multiply with 7 block products instead of 8. One level saves little. Repeated at every level, the saving becomes a power of n, and the exponent drops. The 2026 saving (D0.063, with D a power of n) works the same way.

8k = n³  ·  7k = nlog₂7 ≈ n2.807  ·  saving (8/7)k = n0.193

Where 7k comes from, level by level

Each problem splits into 7 problems of half the size. Count the problems at each level until the blocks are single numbers:

The bottom level has 7k tiny multiplications, and k = log₂ n, so 7k = 7log₂ n = nlog₂ 7 = n2.807. (The extra additions cost about n² per level, which is less than this.) With 8 instead of 7 the same count gives 8log₂ n = n³. The same mechanism explains 2026: a saving that repeats until it becomes a power of n lowers the exponent.

Part 5 · Other ways in

Other approaches

Within a month, two groups found simpler routes to the same conclusion. Before 2026, many people shaved the old bounds without lowering the exponent.

Karingula & Lovett · 14 October 2026

A simplified proof

Keeps the key idea (only compute the requested cells) but replaces the machinery with one explicit rule: each wanted output = six products from a shared pool − one private correction, built from a small 2 × 3 case of Schönhage’s identity. Applied to sets: is the pair of sets disjoint? Known reductions then give 3SUM and APSP.

Weaker exponents Much shorter proof

Chan, Itakura, Ma & Yu · 2 November 2026

3SUM without matrix multiplication

Write numbers in binary. If two numbers have no 1s in the same place, adding them is just stacking their 1s: no carries. Then “a + b = c?” becomes a question about sets, which a classic table method answers in bulk. Random shuffles make the right pair carry-free in some trial.

Part 6 · The honest answer

How much faster?

In practice, barely. In principle, completely. A log-factor saving fades as n grows; a power-of-n saving never does.
