Stop 1 · The problem

One question: is a detour shorter?

The robot must go from Charge to Dock. The direct road is 12 metres. Could stopping at another station on the way be shorter? Pick a route below.

All the work is this one question repeated: “Is going through a middle station shorter?” The robot asks it for every start, every end and every middle station.

Stop 1 · The problem

The robot asks it for every pair

This method is called Floyd–Warshall (1962). The robot picks one middle station and, for every start–end pair, asks “is going through this station shorter?”. Then it moves on to the next middle station. Watch it step by step.

Stop 2 · Why it is slow

Where do the 125 checks come from?

For each middle station the robot looks at 5 starts × 5 ends = 25 pairs. With 5 middle stations that makes 5 × 25 = 125. Each small square is one check; the ones you did on the previous screen are filled in.

With 5 stations there are 5 × 5 × 5 = 125 checks. In general: stations × stations × stations.

Double the stations and the work grows 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 times. This kind of growth is called n³ (“n cubed”): n × n × n checks for n stations.

Side box · same story, another puzzle

3SUM: can three numbers add up to 0?

Can three different entries of a list add up to exactly 0? In the list −7, −5, −2, 3, 4, 9: −7 + 3 + 4 = 0. The smart method (its name: two pointers) sorts the list, fixes one number and walks inward from both ends of the rest: if the sum is too small the left number moves in, if it is too big the right one does.

10 numbers need at most 36 steps; 1,000 numbers about 500,000. Double the list and the work grows 4 times: this is called n². Here too nobody lowered the 2 for 50 years; the 2026 result did: n1.9992.

Stop 2 · Why it is slow

Why could nobody lower the 3 in n³ for 50 years?

Most tricks that speed computers up rely on computing too much and then subtracting the extra. Shortest routes use “take the smallest” instead, and that cannot be undone. Try it with a queue of four people.

The queue is an intuition, not the proof. Also, small gains were found in those 50 years: for example n³ ÷ log n work instead of n³. But they never touched the 3; what people wanted was an exponent below 3, like n2.99.

A total can be fixed by subtracting; if all you kept is the minimum, you cannot fix it. Proved (Kerr, 1970): a method that only adds and “takes the smallest” cannot avoid n³. Guessed: methods using other operations cannot lower the 3 either. That guess was called the wall; nobody could prove it.

Stop 3 · 2026

An AI model found a way

The wall held for decades. Then, within a few weeks in 2026:

Sep 2026
An internal Anthropic research model (Claude), working on cryptography problems, found the algorithm. Anthropic shared it with mathematicians.
5 Oct 2026
Josh Alman (Columbia) and Virginia Vassilevska Williams (MIT) understood, simplified and extended it, and posted it on arXiv as a preprint (a paper not yet peer reviewed).
Oct 2026
The main results were checked line by line by a computer proof checker (Lean 4).
Oct–Nov 2026
Two separate research groups reached the same result by simpler routes.
Nov 2026
The race goes on: an open leaderboard of computer-checked proofs (Big O Speedrun) lists n1.9203 for 3SUM. That is a community record, not a paper.

The result: when distances are whole numbers that are not too large, shortest routes can be found with n2.9995 operations, and 3SUM with n1.9992. The exponent dropped for the first time: both guesses were refuted. Kerr’s proof is still true; the new method uses operations it does not cover.

Stop 3 · 2026

The idea: cheaper than computing even the asked cells one by one

The new method makes two moves. First: change the question. Instead of “which is shortest?”, ask “do these two routes add up exactly to that third one?”. Exact-equality questions allow subtraction. Second: those questions turn into a table product, and only a few cells of the table are asked for. The product below is real: click a cell to see its calculation.

The real novelty is here

Computing the asked cells one by one was always possible: D multiplications per cell. The 2026 method shares the common work between cells and gets down to about D0.437 operations per cell on average. Make D bigger:

Constant factors are ignored. The method only works for very thin tables: with N rows and D columns, N must be at least D18, and at most N² ÷ √D cells may be asked for. That is why it cannot be run on a small example; the table above really computes only the “one by one” method.

Computing only what was asked is not new. What is new is computing each asked cell with fewer than D operations. The saving is small, but it grows with the number of stations; that is what lowers the exponent.

So why does it matter?

An analogy: for 50 years everyone has guessed that “no human can jump higher than 2.45 metres”, but nobody could prove it. Then someone jumps 2.4502 metres. The record is tiny, but the guess is now wrong. The earlier small gains (like n³ ÷ log n) never touched the bar; this is the first time the bar itself was cleared.

In computer science many results relied on the assumption “this problem is hard, because shortest routes need n³”. That assumption has collapsed, so all those results must be re-examined.

In practice nothing changed for the robot. But a 50-year-old guess was refuted.

The end

The whole story in three sentences

1. The robot finds shortest routes by asking “is it shorter through a middle station?” for every triple: n × n × n checks for n stations.

2. Because “take the smallest” cannot be undone, the fast tricks did not work; for 50 years, apart from small gains, the 3 in n³ did not drop, and people guessed it never would.

3. In 2026 a method found by an AI model lowered the exponent by turning the question into exact equality and doing less work than computing even the asked cells one by one: a small but real saving.

When the shortest person leaves the queue, why can’t we know the new shortest right away?

Because the one number we kept (the shortest height) tells us nothing about the others. A total can be fixed by subtracting; a minimum cannot.

Does the 2026 result noticeably speed up the robot’s route calculation?

No. The difference is under 1%, and in practice it is probably even slower. What matters is that the guess “the 3 cannot be lowered” was refuted.

Did the 2026 result prove Kerr’s 1970 proof wrong?

No. The proof covers only methods that add and “take the smallest”, and it is still true. What was refuted is not the proof but the guess that “no other method can lower the 3 either”.
