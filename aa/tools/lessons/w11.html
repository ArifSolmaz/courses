01 · Same devices, new purpose

Building the whole network and
carrying a message have different goals.

In Week 10 we minimized the total cost of installing cables. This week, we assume that all the drawn links already exist. The numbers now mean the cost of sending a message across an edge. We keep the numbers in the teaching example; the goal and the meaning of cost change.

The shortest-path contract

Input: an undirected weighted network, one source device, and zero or positive costs on every edge. A path’s cost is the sum of the costs of its edges. Output: the minimum cost from the source to each reachable device and a path attaining that cost; unreachable devices are reported separately.

For each device, we keep a tentative distance called d[v]: the cost of the cheapest path found so far. Initially, only the source has d[24]=0; all others have ∞, meaning “no path found yet”. ∞ is neither a numerical cost nor zero.

Do fewer edges always mean lower cost?

24 → 11 → 31 has two edges and costs 3+5=8 units. 24 → 20 → 31 also has two edges but costs 4+2=6 units. Can the edge count distinguish these paths?

Compare the goals

No. BFS distance minimizes the number of edges. That works when traversal costs are equal; with different costs, we must compare their sums. A cable network of minimum total cost does not guarantee shortest paths from one source either.

Each edge is listed once above; it appears in both directions in the adjacency list. For example, the neighbors of 24 are 11:3, 18:3, 20:4. There are no self-loops or duplicate edges. A zero weight is a real link; it does not mean “no edge”.

To make the trace reproducible, we inspect neighbors in increasing ID order. In the queue, the smallest tentative distance comes first; ties are broken by the smaller ID. This ID rule cannot change the minimum cost, but it may select one of several equal-cost paths.

03 · Produce a cheaper candidate

Relaxation:
“is it cheaper via u?”

Let d[u] be the cost of reaching u, and w(u,v) the cost of edge u–v. The candidate cost of reaching v via u is d[u]+w(u,v). If this is smaller than d[v], update both d[v] and the record predecessor [v]=u. The predecessor tells us which device we came from last; it helps us reconstruct the entire path backward from its end.

Why is the first estimate for 31 not final?

Once the distance to 11 is 3, edge 11 – 31 gives a candidate of 3+5=8. Set d[31]=8 and predecessor [31]=11. Later, when the distance to 20 is 4, edge 20 – 31 gives 4+2=6<8. Set d[31]=6 and predecessor [31]=20. The path is 24 → 20 → 31.

We cannot write just the edge cost 2: reaching 20 from the source also cost 4 units. If the candidate is equal, we keep the current predecessor; if it is more expensive, no record changes.

candidate = d[u] + w(u,v)
if v is not settled yet and candidate < d[v]:
    d[v] = candidate
    predecessor [v] = u
    if v was just discovered, insert it into the queue
    if v is already queued, decrease its key

When does ∞ mean “unreachable”?

Initially, the d value of 26 is ∞. Does this prove that no path to 26 exists?

Compare the states

No; a path may not have been found yet. Only when the queue is empty and every reachable device has been processed do we report any device still at ∞ as unreachable. The source’s 0 is the cost of the empty path; it is different from ∞.

04 · Let distance determine the order

Choose the smallest distance
instead of using FIFO.

Week 6’s min-heap used a maintenance job’s priority. Here, the priority is the device’s tentative distance. Each waiting device has just one entry in the queue. When d[31] falls from 8 to 6, we do not add a second 31; we decrease the key of its existing entry.

  1. Initially: only 24:0.
  2. Extract 24 and relax its neighbors: [11:3, 18:3, 20:4].
  3. Extract 11 and inspect its neighbors: [18:3, 20:4, 4:5, 7:5, 31:8].
  4. The candidate from 18 to 7 is 3+2=5, equal to the current 5. The predecessor stays unchanged.
  5. The candidate from 20 to 31 is 4+2=6. The waiting list becomes [4:5, 7:5, 31:6]. The queue length stays unchanged.

For readability, the display shows the queue as a logical list sorted by priority. Its actual storage is a binary min-heap that tracks device positions. The entire heap array is not sorted; its root is the minimum. “Decrease-key” updates an entry whose position is known, in place.

05 · Settle an estimate

Dijkstra:
extract the minimum, relax its neighbors.

all d values are ∞, predecessors empty; d[source] = 0
insert the source into the priority queue with distance 0
while the queue is not empty:
    extract u with the smallest tentative distance and settle it
    for each neighbor v of u:
        candidate = d[u] + w(u,v)
        if v is not settled and the candidate is cheaper, update d, predecessor and queue
when the queue is empty, any remaining ∞ means unreachable from the source

First, in the inspect edge step, predict the candidate; if it is an improvement, the following relax step changes the record. A new d value is not settled when a device enters the queue. Under these conditions, it becomes settled when that device is extracted as the minimum. We do not stop early at a target; we process every reachable device.

    06 · The condition for a safe decision

    Why do we need
    nonnegative weights?

    Why can we lock in the smallest estimate?

    Assume that the distances of settled devices are correct. We select the smallest d[u] from the queue. Suppose there were a cheaper path to u: let y be its first unsettled device. When the preceding device was settled, that edge was scanned, so the cost of this path up to y is at least d[y]. If the remaining edges are nonnegative, the total path cost cannot be smaller than d[y]. The queue’s choice also tells us d[u]≤d[y]. Thus the supposed cheaper path cannot cost less than d[u].

    A zero-cost edge does not break this reasoning; weights need not be strictly positive. With a negative edge, the rest of the path could lower the total; the decision “the smallest estimate is now final” becomes unsafe.

    Test the condition on a separate small example

    Unlike the main network, this counterexample is directed: it has only the arcs 18 → 7:2, 18 → 24:5, 24 → 7:−4; source 18. A method that first settles 7 at the value 2 misses the fact that path 18 → 24 → 7 costs 5−4=1. There is no negative cycle; these three arcs form a DAG.

    In the main undirected network, repeatedly traversing a negative edge in both directions could reduce the cost without limit. We use this small directed example with a finite answer to isolate the condition for the decision.

    This lesson’s model checks every input edge; it also rejects a negative weight in an unreachable component. The button below tests input validation only; it does not run a search with a negative edge.

    When negative weights are needed, we consider a method based on repeated relaxation, such as Bellman–Ford. If a negative cycle is reachable from the source, affected targets have no finite minimum path cost. Before studying a second algorithm’s steps, the question is how to revisit the assumptions.

    07 · Separate cost from work

    The path costs 6 units.
    How many operations did the algorithm perform?

    In the main example, the path cost to 31 is 4+2=6. This is not an operation count. We initialized 8 distance rows and extracted 8 devices as the minimum. Every undirected edge was scanned twice in the adjacency lists: 2E=20 adjacency scans; inspecting an already settled neighbor is included in this count.

    7 devices were discovered for the first time; including the source, there were 8 queue insertions. The key of 31 decreased once. There were 8 successful improvements in total: 7 first discoveries + 1 decrease. The source’s initial assignment of 0 is not included in this improvement count. Equal or more expensive candidates do not count as updates.

    The priority-queue operations we count

    The model tracks each device’s position in the heap; insertion, extract-min and decrease-key each have an O(log(V+1)) upper bound. Each waiting device has one entry; we do not discard stale copies later. The counters record logical operations, not internal heap comparisons or swaps.

    With an existing adjacency list, initializing distances takes O(V); there are at most 2E scans, at most V insertions and V extractions, and at most E successful decreases. A safe total upper bound is O((V+E) log(V+1)). Even with no edges, the work of initializing all V rows remains.

    Distances, predecessors and the queue need O(V) extra space; the input adjacency lists use O(V+E) space. Preparing the input and sorting neighbors for teaching, storing a full copy of every step, showing a sorted queue on screen, and drawing are outside this core algorithm count. Writing the final path from its predecessors also takes work proportional to the path length.

    08 · Reduce the support

    20 is settled.
    How do you update the record for 31?

    d[20]=4. Edge 20 – 31 costs 2. The current tentative value for 31 is 8 and its predecessor is 11; it is not settled yet. The queue contains [4:5, 7:5, 31:8]. After the next edge inspection, decide whether to relax it.

    Compare the solution after working

    4+2=6<8; d[31]=6, predecessor [31]=20. 31 had not yet been extracted as the minimum, so 8 was tentative. The queue is [4:5, 7:5, 31:6]; still 3 entries, with 4 next under the tie rule. The key decreases; no new entry is added. 2 is only the last edge’s cost; reaching 20 also adds cost 4.
