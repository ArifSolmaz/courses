01 · Change the output

Finding an ID and
choosing the next job are different tasks.

In Week 5 we asked, “What is the location label for ID 31?” The dictionary returned “gate”. Now the task is: “Which waiting maintenance job should I do first?” We are not given a target ID; we must make a selection.

Each maintenance request has its own job label. Another job may arrive later from the same device. The device ID tells us which device the job concerns; it does not set the priority. In this lesson, priorities do not change after jobs enter the queue.

Is arrival order enough?

Job A arrived first, with priority number 4. Job H arrived later, with priority number 3. Under the rule “smaller number first”, which job would you choose? Which would FIFO choose?

Compare my decision

The priority rule selects H; FIFO selects A. FIFO preserves arrival order, while this maintenance policy considers priority first. The structures serve different output contracts. These numbers alone do not determine a real maintenance policy; we fixed the rule here for the exercise.

02 · Define operations and ties

A priority queue.
An explicit selection rule.

This lesson’s contract

Who comes first?
The job with the smaller priority number. For equal priorities, the smaller arrival number wins: the job that arrived earlier.
insert(job)
Adds a new job to those waiting. The arrival number is assigned when the job first arrives and stays unchanged as the job moves.
extractMin()
Returns the job record that comes first under the selection rule and removes it from the queue. If empty, returns “no job”.
peekMin()
Shows the same job without removing it. Peeking and extracting are separate operations.

The comparison key is the pair (priority, arrival): compare priority first, then arrival if tied. For example, (4,1) precedes (4,2); both precede (6,3). Arrival numbers are unique. Preserving arrival order in a tie is not an automatic property of every heap; we explicitly include this rule in the comparison.

These maintenance jobs for the seven devices in the earlier directory are already waiting. A–G also gives their arrival order:

03 · Keep only the order you need

The minimum is at the root.
Not everything is sorted.

Min-heap is the representation we use to implement this priority queue. We maintain two conditions together:

  1. Shape: levels fill in order; only the last may be incomplete, and it fills from the left. This preserves a complete binary tree shape.
  2. Order: every parent precedes or ties its children under the comparison rule. Here, compare priority first, then arrival number if tied.

The initial heap in level order is [A, B, C, D, E, F, G], with priorities [4, 7, 6, 12, 9, 8, 10]. A is the root; its children are B and C. B’s children are D and E; C’s children are F and G.

There is a path from the root to every node. Because a parent never follows its child, this relation holds along the whole path: the root precedes every job. This makes peeking at the minimum cheap. But B’s 7 is greater than C’s 6, and D’s 12 is greater than E’s 9. The heap array is not fully sorted; siblings have no ordering requirement.

Array positions, tree relationships

The positions displayed in this lesson start at 1. For position i, the parent is ⌊i/2⌋; the children are 2i and 2i+1, only when those positions do not exceed n. The root has no parent.

For example, position i=3 holds C; its child positions are 6 and 7, holding F and G. ⌊i/2⌋ means divide by two and round down. These formulas apply to positions, not priorities; we use an array with direct access to positions.

Is this Week 5’s search tree?

Given a target device ID, can you search this heap by following “smaller ID to the left”?

Compare my connection

No. There is no rule here that all keys in the left subtree are smaller and those in the right subtree larger. Nor is the order based on device IDs. An ordinary heap does not guarantee a short branch-selection path to find a given ID; this lesson selects the highest-priority job.

04 · Restore the invariant

Append, then sift up.
Remove the root, then sift down.

The initial heap is ready. Now let H: device 26, priority 3, arrival 8 arrive. Put it in the last free position, 8: the tree shape is preserved. Only H’s path to its parents may violate the order. Compare H with its parent; if H comes first, swap the two.

  1. At position 8, H3 has its parent at position 4, D12: H comes first → swap.
  2. At position 4, H3 has its parent at position 2, B7: H comes first → swap.
  3. At position 2, H3 has A4 at the root: H comes first → swap. H is now the root; stop.

The final heap is [H, A, C, B, E, F, G, D]. Three job comparisons, three swaps. Under FIFO, H is still the last arrival; this priority queue selects it first.

When extracting the minimum, set aside the root job as the result. Move the last job to the root and remove the last cell: the shape is preserved, but the root may now be too large. If there are two children, first determine which comes first, then compare that child with the parent. If the child comes first, swap and continue down. With one child, compare only that child; stop when the order is correct.

Consider extracting A from the first seven jobs: the last job, G, moves to the root. Between B7 and C6, choose C; C6 precedes G10 → swap. G now has only F8 below it; F8 comes first → swap. The final heap is [C, B, F, D, E, G]; the extracted job is A, device 18. Three comparisons, two swaps.

    Insert without moving up: append I=(15,8) to the initial heap. Compare it once with parent D=(12,4): 1 comparison. Stop because I has the larger priority number. 0 swaps. One job: set the job aside and remove the cell; 0 comparisons, 0 swaps. Empty queue: “no job”; 0/0.

    The counting units

    One job comparison is a single abstract operation that determines which of two (priority,arrival) pairs comes first. Checking arrival when priorities tie is included in this unit; we are not counting separate programming operators.

    One swap exchanges the positions of two existing jobs. Appending, removing the last cell and moving the last job to the root are also described in the operation history, but do not increase the swap counter. These counters do not measure every memory operation or elapsed seconds.

    05 · Connect the path to growth

    Scan n jobs,
    or repair one path?

    n is the number of waiting jobs; h is the number of edges from the root to the deepest node. For a nonempty heap, h=⌊log₂n⌋, because of the complete binary tree shape. In the first example, n=7,h=2; after inserting H, n=8,h=3. Unlike Week 5’s ordinary search tree, this height is bounded by the shape.

    Insertion follows one path from the new job to the root: at most one comparison and one swap for each level it climbs. Extraction follows one path down from the root: choosing the child and checking it against the parent takes at most two comparisons and at most one swap per level. Not every level has two children.

    For this array representation, we treat position access as constant work and assume enough capacity; memory reallocation is excluded. Under these conditions, insertion/extraction sifting takes work on the scale of log n in the worst case; not every individual operation must take that much work. For example, inserting I stopped after one comparison. Peeking at the minimum is constant work: read the root.

    Connect to compact notation

    With enough array capacity, insertion and minimum extraction are worst-case O(log n), and peeking is O(1); small or empty sizes involve constant work. If growing a dynamic array requires bulk copying, that work also contributes to the total cost of a single insertion. The counter claim here excludes such copying.

    Could a plain list do the same task?

    With n jobs ready in an unsorted array, how many job comparisons suffice to find the highest-priority job? Must you fully sort it to insert the next arrival?

    Compare my choice

    Take the first job as the candidate and compare it with the other n−1 jobs: n−1 comparisons for n≥1. During extraction, moving the last job into the vacated position is sufficient in this unsorted layout. With capacity available, append a new job in constant work; no full sort is needed. A heap provides short paths for many mixed insertions/extractions. With few selections or very small inputs, a plain scan can also be reasonable; a growth claim alone does not establish a real-time advantage.

    Extracting a job from a heap does not sort the whole remaining array. Requesting all jobs in order is another task; next week we move to full sorting.
