01 · A new output

We ordered the jobs.
Now we build the physical network.

The arrows in Week 9 represented which job must finish first. Now a vertex is the same device; an edge is a candidate cable usable in both directions. Its number is a one-time installation cost.

The network-building contract

Input: all devices and the undirected, weighted candidate cables between them. Output: a cycle-free selection connecting every device, with the smallest total cost of selected cables. This is the minimum spanning tree (MST) problem.

If the input is disconnected, the available candidates cannot connect every device. We connect each input component internally with its cheapest tree: together they form a minimum spanning forest. A lone device is included too; it needs no cable on its own.

One message’s path or the whole network?

Does finding a cheap path only between 18 and 31 guarantee that the other devices are connected too? If a path already exists between two devices, is another cable required?

Compare my goal

No. Here we must include every device. Within the same connected part, a new cable between two vertices closes a cycle; it is unnecessary for connecting the whole network. Which cycle-free selection has the smallest total cost still needs a justification.

03 · The parts formed by selected cables

The same component?
What does a new cable change?

A component is a group of devices that can reach each other through our selected cables. Initially there are no cables: eight singleton components. Distinguish the input’s connections from those of the cables selected so far.

  1. 4 – 7 costs 1: the endpoints are in different groups. Accept it; {4, 7} becomes one group. 7 components remain, total 1.
  2. 4 – 11 costs 2: 11 is outside that group. Accept it; the group becomes {4, 7, 11}. 6 components, total 3.
  3. 7 – 11 costs 2: the endpoints are already in the same group. The selected path is 7 – 4 – 11; the new edge closes the cycle 7 – 4 – 11 – 7. Reject it; the components and total stay unchanged.

Each acceptance merges two distinct components into one group. Rejection keeps the current selection cycle-free. Thus the selected edges form a forest at every step.

      05 · Justifying a local decision

      Why can we safely continue
      making cheap, cycle-free choices?

      A cut splits the devices into two groups. An edge crosses the cut if one endpoint is inside a group and the other is outside it. Put one component of the selected forest on one side: none of the currently selected edges leaves this component.

      Keep a cheapest completion containing the selection

      When Kruskal selects edge e between two different components, e is among the lightest candidates crossing the cut of either component. Both endpoints of any earlier accepted edge are now in the same component; the endpoints of any rejected edge also remain in the same component. Therefore, a cheaper edge crossing the current component’s cut cannot have been processed earlier.

      If a cheapest completion containing the current selection lacks e, adding e creates a cycle. The cycle has another edge g crossing the same cut; g’s cost is no smaller than e’s. Replacing g with e keeps every device connected and does not increase the total. Earlier selected edges are preserved. Thus there is a cheapest completion containing the new selection too.

      For disconnected input, this argument applies within each input component. With equal weights, an exchange may leave the total unchanged: the minimum tree need not be unique. In the main network, suitable tie choices replacing 4 – 11 with 7 – 11, or 11 – 24 with 18 – 24, also reach 18.

      Simply saying “there is no cycle” does not prove minimum cost; an expensive tree can be cycle-free too. For a safe choice, we use cost order together with the component’s cut.

      06 · Separate total cost from operation counts

      18 cost units.
      How many operations did we perform?

      The total 18 is the cost of installing the cables, not the algorithm’s operation count. We inspected the endpoints of 10 sorted candidates, making 7 merges and 3 rejections. Of the initial 8 singleton groups, 1 remains. In general, if the final component count is c, then the selected-edge count is V−c; in a connected network, c=1.

      How does this small version maintain components?

      Each device’s label is its group’s smallest ID. Comparing the two endpoint labels takes constant work. On acceptance, we scan all V rows and give both groups the common smaller label. This is a simple method; we do not count a merge as free.

      In the main example: 8 initial rows, 10 decisions, and 7×8=56 row scans for merges. Not every scanned row’s label changes: scan count is not the number of actual label writes. A rejection performs no new merge scan.

      Initialisation takes O(V), all E decisions take O(E), and this version’s V−c merges take O(V(V−c)) work. Including sorting, a safe upper bound is O(V + E log(E+1) + V²). There is no sorting work if edges are already sorted. Saying “we just sorted the edges, so O(E log E) is enough” would omit the merging work of this simple label method.

      Faster component structures can improve this part; we do not study their details here. Labels and the selected forest use O(V) auxiliary space; the candidate and sorted lists use O(E) space. Finding the selected path to write a cycle witness, drawing the display and storing every teaching snapshot are outside these counters. Separate the standard choice rule from the teaching demonstration’s extra work.
